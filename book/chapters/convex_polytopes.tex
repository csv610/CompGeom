\chapter{Convex Polytopes}
\label{ch:convex_polytopes}

\section{Convex Polytopes}
\label{sec:polytopes_intro}

A \textbf{convex polytope}\index{convex polytope} is the $d$-dimensional
generalization of a convex polygon: a bounded convex set whose boundary is
made of finitely many flat pieces, the \emph{facets}. Convex polytopes are
the feasible regions of linear programs (Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}),
the cells of hyperplane arrangements, and the convex hulls built by
Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_hulls} and used by the computational and polyhedral
geometry of Chapters~\ref{ch:polyhedral_geometry}
and~\ref{ch:computational_topology}. In the plane a convex polytope is a
convex polygon; in $\mathbb{R}^3$ it is a convex polyhedron, and the cube
and the tetrahedron are the models to keep in mind throughout.

\begin{definition}[Polytope]\label{def:polytopes_polytope}
  A \textbf{convex polytope} in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is a bounded convex set that
  is the convex hull of finitely many points
  (Section~\ref{sec:math_convex_combinations}), equivalently the bounded
  intersection of finitely many closed halfspaces
  (Section~\ref{sec:math_half_spaces}); the equivalence is the
  Minkowski--Weyl theorem of the next section. A polytope of dimension $d$
  is a \textbf{$d$-polytope}.
\end{definition}

Two complementary descriptions drive the subject. The \textbf{V-description}
lists the extreme points (vertices); the \textbf{H-description} lists the
defining linear inequalities (facets). This chapter develops the dictionary
between them, the combinatorial structure they share (the \emph{face
lattice}), the extremal question of how many facets a polytope with $n$
vertices can have, and the algorithms that compute hulls and enumerate
vertices in three and higher dimensions. A running thread is
\textbf{polarity}, the duality that interchanges vertices with facets and
V- with H-descriptions (Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_polarity}), closely
related to the point--hyperplane duality of Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_duality}.

The subject has classical roots: Euler's 1758 counting formula
$V - E + F = 2$ \cite{euler1758} predates the modern computational questions
by two centuries, and the affine theorems of Carath\'eodory, Helly, and
Radon (Sections~\ref{sec:polytopes_caratheodory}--\ref{sec:polytopes_radon})
organize higher-dimensional convexity into three clean statements.
Computationally, the three-dimensional hull is built in $O(n \log n)$ time,
while the higher-dimensional theory is governed by the upper bound theorem:
a $d$-polytope with $n$ vertices has $\Theta(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})$
facets in the worst case (Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_faces}), separating the
tractable three-dimensional case from the combinatorial explosion of
dimension four and above.

\index{V-description}\index{H-description}\index{face lattice}\index{polarity}

\section{V-Representations and H-Representations}
\label{sec:polytopes_representations}

Every polytope admits two canonical descriptions, each with its own data
structure and its own algorithmic tasks. The \textbf{V-representation}
specifies the polytope as the convex hull of a finite point set; the
\textbf{H-representation} specifies it as the intersection of a finite set
of halfspaces. The fundamental structural theorem states that the two
coincide.

\begin{definition}[V-Polytope and H-Polytope]\label{def:polytopes_v_h}
  A \textbf{V-polytope} is the convex hull
  $\operatorname{conv}(S) = \{ \sum_i \lambda_i p_i : p_i \in S, \lambda_i
  \ge 0, \sum_i \lambda_i = 1 \}$ of a finite set $S \subset \mathbb{R}^d$
  (Section~\ref{sec:math_convex_combinations}). An \textbf{H-polytope} is a
  bounded set of the form $\{ x : Ax \le b \}$, a finite intersection of
  closed halfspaces (Section~\ref{sec:math_half_spaces}).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Minkowski--Weyl Theorem]\label{thm:polytopes_minkowski_weyl}
  A subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$ is a V-polytope if and only if it is an
  H-polytope.
\end{theorem}

The proof has two halves. The convex hull of finitely many points is the
intersection of the halfspaces supporting it (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_supporting}),
so it is an H-polytope. Conversely, every point of the bounded polyhedron
$\{x : Ax \le b\}$ is a convex combination of finitely many extreme points:
move from $x$ along a line to the boundary, recurse on the
lower-dimensional faces, and the dimension drops at every step. The
equivalence is treated in \cite{edelsbrunner1987,preparata1985}.

\begin{definition}[Extreme Point]\label{def:polytopes_vertex}
  A point $p$ of a convex set $C$ is \textbf{extreme} (a \textbf{vertex}) if
  it cannot be written as a nontrivial convex combination of two distinct
  points of $C$; the vertex set is $\operatorname{vert}(P)$.
\end{definition}

The standard examples organize the picture. The \textbf{simplex}
$\Delta_d = \operatorname{conv}\{e_1, \dots, e_{d+1}\}$ has $d+1$ vertices
and $d+1$ facets, the fewest possible. The \textbf{cube} $[0,1]^d$ has
$2^d$ vertices and $2d$ facets. The \textbf{cross-polytope}
$\operatorname{conv}\{\pm e_1, \dots, \pm e_d\}$ has $2d$ vertices and
$2^d$ facets; it is the polar dual of the cube
(Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_polarity}). The \textbf{cyclic polytope}, the
convex hull of points on the moment curve $\gamma(t) = (t, t^2, \dots,
t^d)$, maximizes the number of faces among all $d$-polytopes with the same
number of vertices (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_upper_bound}).

Each description has its own hard problem. Given a V-representation,
computing the facets is \textbf{facet enumeration}; given an
H-representation, computing the vertices is \textbf{vertex enumeration}
(Definition~\ref{def:polytopes_vertex_enumeration}); both are the same
problem up to polarity and are treated in
Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_computation}. Deciding whether a candidate point
is a vertex, and evaluating a linear objective, are the tasks of
Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}. The point--hyperplane duality of
Section~\ref{sec:duality_convex_hulls} converts convex hull problems into
halfplane intersection problems, a transformation whose power is analyzed in
\cite{chazelle1985power}.

\index{extreme point}\index{cyclic polytope}\index{cross-polytope}

\section{Faces and Face Lattices}
\label{sec:polytopes_faces}

\begin{definition}[Faces]\label{def:polytopes_face}
  A \textbf{face} of a polytope $P$ is the intersection $P \cap H$ with a
  supporting hyperplane $H$ (Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_supporting_hyperplanes}),
  together with $P$ itself and the empty set. A face of dimension $k$ is a
  \textbf{$k$-face}: $0$-faces are \textbf{vertices}, $1$-faces are
  \textbf{edges}, $(d-2)$-faces are \textbf{ridges}, and $(d-1)$-faces are
  \textbf{facets}. Faces other than $P$ itself are \textbf{proper faces}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Face Lattice]\label{def:polytopes_face_lattice}
  The \textbf{face lattice} $\mathcal{L}(P)$ is the set of all faces of $P$
  ordered by inclusion, with the empty face at the bottom and $P$ at the
  top.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Face Lattice Structure]\label{thm:polytopes_face_lattice}
  The face lattice of a $d$-polytope is a finite \textbf{graded lattice} of
  rank $d+1$: the meet of two faces is their intersection, the join is the
  convex hull of their union, and the rank of a face is its dimension plus
  one. Every face is the join of its vertices.
\end{theorem}

The face lattice is what graphics, mesh processing, and topological
algorithms pass around implicitly: the half-edge and winged-edge data
structures of Chapter~\ref{ch:half_edge} store the rank-two incidences of a
three-dimensional polytope, and the Betti-number checks of
Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology} read the topological invariants of
the boundary off the lattice. Because the boundary of a $3$-polytope is a
planar graph, the lattice is equivalently that planar graph together with
its embedding; by Steinitz's theorem the planar 3-connected graphs are
exactly the graphs of convex polytopes in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

\begin{definition}[Simple and Simplicial Polytopes]\label{def:polytopes_simple_simplicial}
  A $d$-polytope is \textbf{simplicial} if all its facets are simplices (in
  $\mathbb{R}^3$: all facets are triangles), and \textbf{simple} if every
  vertex has degree $d$. Polarity interchanges the two classes
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_polar_reversal}).
\end{definition}

\subsection{Combinatorial complexity}

How many facets can a polytope with $n$ vertices have? In
$\mathbb{R}^3$ the answer is linear --- at most $2n - 4$ --- by Euler's
formula (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_euler}). In higher dimensions the answer
is polynomial in $n$ with a dimension-dependent exponent, and the extremal
shapes are the cyclic polytopes.

\begin{definition}[Cyclic Polytope]\label{def:polytopes_cyclic}
  The \textbf{cyclic polytope} $C(d,n)$ is the convex hull of $n$ distinct
  points on the moment curve $\gamma(t) = (t, t^2, \dots, t^d)$. It is
  \textbf{neighborly}: every set of at most $\lfloor d/2 \rfloor$ vertices
  spans a face.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Upper Bound Theorem]\label{thm:polytopes_upper_bound}
  A $d$-polytope with $n$ vertices has $O(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})$ facets,
  and the cyclic polytope $C(d,n)$ attains the bound: $\Phi(d,n) =
  \Theta(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})$. By polarity, a $d$-polytope with $n$
  facets has at most $\Theta(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})$ vertices.
\end{theorem}

The upper bound theorem settles the \emph{size} of every higher-dimensional
hull, hence the output complexity of every hull algorithm
(Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_computation}). In $\mathbb{R}^3$ the hull is
linear in size, which is why three-dimensional hull algorithms
(Chapter~\ref{ch:polyhedral_geometry}) run in $O(n \log n)$ time. In
$\mathbb{R}^4$ and above the output size $\Theta(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})$
is superlinear, so no algorithm can be linear in the input size alone. The
combinatorial complexity of polytopes and arrangement cells, and its role in
discrepancy lower bounds, are treated in \cite{matousek1999} and
\cite{edelsbrunner1987}.

\textbf{The dual of a triangulated surface.} The boundary of a simplicial
$3$-polytope is a triangulated sphere, and its dual graph --- one node per
triangular facet, edges across shared edges --- is again planar.

\begin{example}[Dual of a Triangulated Surface]\label{ex:polytopes_dual_triangulation}
  A triangulated surface that bounds a convex polytope with $V$ vertices has
  $F = 2V - 4$ triangles and $E = 3V - 6$ edges
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_upper_bound_3d}). Its dual graph has $F$ nodes,
  each of degree $3$, and $E$ edges; it is exactly the 1-skeleton of the
  \emph{polar} polytope, which is simple
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_polar_reversal}). Duality swaps vertices and
  facets, and $F = 2V - 4$ is the identity behind the linear-size storage of
  triangular meshes in Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}.
\end{example}

\section{Supporting Hyperplanes}
\label{sec:polytopes_supporting_hyperplanes}

\begin{definition}[Supporting Hyperplane]\label{def:polytopes_supporting_hyperplane}
  A hyperplane $H = \{ x : \langle x, u \rangle = c \}$ \textbf{supports} a
  polytope $P$ if $\langle x, u \rangle \le c$ for all $x \in P$ and
  equality holds for at least one point of $P$. Every face of $P$ other than
  the empty face is exposed by a supporting hyperplane.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Supporting Hyperplane Theorem]\label{thm:polytopes_supporting}
  Let $P$ be a polytope and $u$ any direction. The linear functional
  $x \mapsto \langle x, u \rangle$ attains its maximum on $P$ in the face
  $F_P(u) = \{ x \in P : \langle x, u \rangle = h_P(u) \}$, where
  \[
    h_P(u) = \max_{x \in P} \langle x, u \rangle
  \]
  is the \textbf{support function} of $P$ in direction $u$. Every boundary
  point of $P$ lies on a supporting hyperplane, and a face is a vertex iff
  it is the unique maximizer in some direction.
\end{theorem}

The support function $h_P$ is exactly the support function of
Section~\ref{sec:convexint_support_functions}, and it packages the whole
polytope: $P = \{ x : \langle x, u \rangle \le h_P(u) \text{ for all } u \}$
is the H-representation re-parametrized by directions. Evaluating $h_P(u)$ is
the oracle behind the support-function algorithms of
Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_intersection}, and for an H-polytope it is a linear
program, solved by the methods of Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}.

The supporting hyperplanes organize the ambient space into the
\textbf{normal fan} of the polytope.

\begin{definition}[Normal Cone and Normal Fan]\label{def:polytopes_normal_cone}
  The \textbf{normal cone} of a face $F$ of $P$ is the cone of directions
  $u$ for which $F_P(u) = F$. The \textbf{normal fan} $\mathcal{N}(P)$ is
  the collection of the normal cones of all faces; it is a complete fan of
  polyhedral cones whose union is $\mathbb{R}^d$, ordered by inclusion in
  exactly the reverse of the face lattice.
\end{definition}

The normal fan is where polytopes meet arrangements and duality: each
full-dimensional normal cone is a cell of the arrangement of the facet
hyperplanes of the \emph{polar} polytope (Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_polarity}),
and the point--hyperplane duality of Section~\ref{sec:math_duality} and
Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_duality} realizes the normal fan as a section of a
hyperplane arrangement --- the mechanism behind the duality-based hull and
Voronoi algorithms \cite{edelsbrunner1987,chazelle1985power}. For a convex
polygon the normal fan is the subdivision of the plane by the vertex
outward normals; for the cube $[0,1]^d$ it is the arrangement of coordinate
hyperplanes.

\index{supporting hyperplane}\index{support function}\index{normal fan}
\index{normal cone}

\section{Separating Hyperplane Theorems}
\label{sec:polytopes_separating}

Separation theorems assert that disjoint convex sets can be told apart by a
hyperplane; they are the geometric form of duality and the source of
certificates in optimization.

\begin{theorem}[Strict Separation]\label{thm:polytopes_strict_separation}
  Let $A, B \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be disjoint compact convex sets. Then
  there is a vector $u$ and a scalar $c$ with
  $\langle a, u \rangle < c < \langle b, u \rangle$ for all $a \in A$ and
  $b \in B$: a \textbf{strictly separating} hyperplane.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  The distance $\operatorname{dist}(A,B) = \min_{a \in A, b \in B}
  \|a-b\|$ is attained at $(a^*, b^*)$ because the sets are compact
  (Section~\ref{sec:convexint_distance}). Set $u = b^* - a^*$ and
  $c = \langle (a^*+b^*)/2, u \rangle$. If some $a \in A$ satisfied
  $\langle a, u \rangle \ge c$, the segment from $a^*$ to $a$, which lies in
  $A$ by convexity, would approach $b^*$ more closely than $a^*$ does,
  contradicting the minimality of $\|a^* - b^*\|$; symmetrically
  $\langle b, u \rangle \le c$ is impossible for $b \in B$.
\end{proof}

The affine separation theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:math_separation} of Section~\ref{sec:math_separating_surfaces})
is the general formulation for arbitrary convex sets; strictness holds here
because polytopes are compact. Separation underlies the separating axis
theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:convexint_sat}): for two disjoint polytopes the
separating hyperplane can be tilted until it supports both, yielding the
finite set of candidate axes. It is also the certificate machinery of linear
programming, made explicit by the Farkas lemma.

\begin{theorem}[Farkas Lemma]\label{thm:polytopes_farkas}
  Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Exactly one
  of the following two systems is solvable:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $Ax \le b$ for some $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$;
    \item $y \ge 0$, $y^{\top} A = 0$, and $y^{\top} b < 0$ for some
      $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Farkas' lemma \cite{farkas1902} is the infeasibility certificate for linear
systems: when $Ax \le b$ has no solution, the nonnegative combination $y$
proves it, since multiplying the system by $y \ge 0$ gives
$0 = y^{\top} A x \le y^{\top} b < 0$, a contradiction. It follows from
strict separation applied to the polyhedron $\{Ax \le b\}$ and the origin,
and it is the primal--dual bridge behind the duality theory of
Section~\ref{sec:lp_duality_applications}. Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_computation}
returns to the lemma as the pivot test underlying polytope traversal and
vertex enumeration.

\index{separation theorem}\index{separating hyperplane}\index{Farkas lemma}

\section{Carath\'eodory's Theorem}
\label{sec:polytopes_caratheodory}

Carath\'eodory's theorem bounds how many points of a set are needed to
represent any point of its convex hull: the dimension $d$, not the size of
the set, is all that matters.

\begin{theorem}[Carath\'eodory]\label{thm:polytopes_caratheodory}
  Let $S \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and let $x \in \operatorname{conv}(S)$. Then
  $x$ lies in the convex hull of at most $d + 1$ points of $S$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Write $x = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i p_i$ with $p_i \in S$, $\lambda_i > 0$,
  $\sum \lambda_i = 1$, and $k$ minimal. If $k > d + 1$, the points are
  affinely dependent (Section~\ref{sec:math_affine_combinations}): there are
  $\mu_i$, not all zero, with $\sum \mu_i p_i = 0$ and $\sum \mu_i = 0$. For
  small $\varepsilon$ the coefficients $\lambda_i + \varepsilon \mu_i$ still
  sum to $1$, are nonnegative, and represent the same point; choosing the
  largest $|\varepsilon|$ keeping them nonnegative kills at least one
  positive coefficient, contradicting minimality. Hence $k \le d + 1$.
\end{proof}

The bound is tight: the center of the simplex spanned by $d+1$ affinely
independent points requires all of them. The version stated here is also
recorded as Theorem~\ref{thm:math_caratheodory} of the mathematical
background chapter.

The algorithmic content is that convex hulls are determined by their
$(d+1)$-subsets: every point of $\operatorname{conv}(S)$ lies in a
$d$-simplex spanned by $d+1$ points of $S$. This is why all hull algorithms
of Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_computation} restrict attention to
$(d+1)$-tuples and $(d-1)$-faces: a facet of $\operatorname{conv}(S)$ is
spanned by $d$ points of $S$ and is a facet exactly when all other points
lie on one side of its hyperplane. Carath\'eodory's theorem also supplies
the $d+1$ in Helly's and Radon's theorems
(Sections~\ref{sec:polytopes_helly} and~\ref{sec:polytopes_radon}), the two
pillars of the local theory of higher-dimensional convexity.

\index{Carath\'eodory's theorem}\index{convex combination}

\section{Helly's Theorem}
\label{sec:polytopes_helly}

\begin{theorem}[Helly]\label{thm:polytopes_helly}
  Let $\mathcal{F} = \{C_1, \dots, C_m\}$ be a finite family of convex sets
  in $\mathbb{R}^d$. If every subfamily of $d + 1$ sets has nonempty
  intersection, then the entire family has nonempty intersection.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Induct on the number of sets, assuming every proper subfamily intersects;
  then each $D_j = \bigcap_{i \neq j} C_i$ is nonempty, so choose
  $p_j \in D_j$. If $m \ge d + 2$, Radon's theorem
  (Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_radon}) gives a Radon partition $A \cup B$ of
  $\{p_1, \dots, p_m\}$ with $x \in \operatorname{conv}(A) \cap
  \operatorname{conv}(B)$. Every point of $A$ lies in all $C_i$ except its
  own index; by convexity $\operatorname{conv}(A)$ lies in the intersection
  of all $C_i$ except those indexed by $A$, and the analogous claim holds
  for $B$. The two subfamilies together cover all of $\mathcal{F}$, so $x$
  lies in every $C_i$.
\end{proof}

The proof shows the precise relation of the companion theorems: Helly's
$d+1$ is Radon's $d+2$ shifted by one, and Radon's theorem rests on affine
dependence, the same fact underlying Carath\'eodory's bound. Helly's
theorem is the finite combinatorial counterpart of the separation theorems:
the family has empty intersection iff some $d+1$ of its members already do,
so infeasibility of a convex system is always witnessed by a small
subsystem --- at most $d+1$ constraints. This is the geometry behind
linear-programming infeasibility certificates
(Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_separating}) and the low-dimensional witness
principle of Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}.

\begin{example}[Helly in the Plane]
  For $d = 1$, Helly's theorem says that a family of intervals on the line
  with pairwise intersection has total intersection. For $d = 2$, a family
  of convex figures in the plane any three of which share a point share a
  common point --- the form used to bound witnesses in planar piercing
  problems.
\end{example}

\index{Helly's theorem}\index{intersection of convex sets}

\section{Radon's Theorem}
\label{sec:polytopes_radon}

\begin{theorem}[Radon]\label{thm:polytopes_radon}
  Any set of $d + 2$ points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ can be partitioned into two
  disjoint sets $A$ and $B$ such that
  $\operatorname{conv}(A) \cap \operatorname{conv}(B) \neq \emptyset$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  The $d + 2$ points are affinely dependent
  (Section~\ref{sec:math_affine_combinations}): there are $\alpha_i$, not
  all zero, with $\sum \alpha_i p_i = 0$ and $\sum \alpha_i = 0$. Let $A$
  collect the points with $\alpha_i > 0$ and $B$ the rest; both are
  nonempty. Then $\sum_{i \in A} \alpha_i = -\sum_{j \in B} \alpha_j =
  \sigma > 0$ and
  \[
    x = \sum_{i \in A} \frac{\alpha_i}{\sigma} \, p_i
      = \sum_{j \in B} \frac{-\alpha_j}{\sigma} \, p_j
  \]
  is a common point of $\operatorname{conv}(A)$ and $\operatorname{conv}(B)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Radon Partition]\label{def:polytopes_radon_partition}
  A partition of a set of $d + 2$ points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ into two parts
  with intersecting convex hulls is a \textbf{Radon partition}, and the
  common point of the two hulls is a \textbf{Radon point} of the set.
\end{definition}

Radon's theorem is sharp: $d+1$ affinely independent points form a simplex,
whose vertices cannot be separated into parts with intersecting hulls. In
the plane, four points are either in convex position, the crossing diagonals
giving the Radon partition, or one lies inside the triangle of the other
three, the Radon point being that point itself. The partition read along
the moment curve is the combinatorial seed of the neighborliness of cyclic
polytopes (Definition~\ref{def:polytopes_cyclic}).

Radon partitions are the local combinatorial step of higher-dimensional
algorithms. In the randomized incremental hull of
Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_computation}, the conflicted facets of a new
point in $\mathbb{R}^3$ form a topological disk whose boundary (the horizon)
is a single cycle --- a planar Radon statement about the facets the point
sees. Radon's theorem is also the engine of the proof of Helly's theorem
above and underlies the perturbation arguments that put higher-dimensional
inputs into general position.

\index{Radon's theorem}\index{Radon partition}\index{affine dependence}

\section{Euler--Poincar\'e Relations}
\label{sec:polytopes_euler_poincare}

The oldest theorem of polytope combinatorics counts the faces of a convex
polytope in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

\begin{theorem}[Euler's Formula]\label{thm:polytopes_euler}
  For every convex polytope in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges,
  and $F$ facets,
  \[
    V - E + F = 2 .
  \]
\end{theorem}

Euler announced the formula in 1758 \cite{euler1758}; the modern view
\cite{poincare1895} sees the boundary of the polytope as a triangulated
sphere and $V - E + F$ as its Euler characteristic (compare
Section~\ref{sec:math_topology} and Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}).
The $d$-dimensional generalization counts all dimensions at once.

\begin{definition}[Face Vector]\label{def:polytopes_f_vector}
  The \textbf{face vector} (f-vector) of a $d$-polytope is the tuple
  $(f_0, f_1, \dots, f_{d-1})$, where $f_k$ is the number of $k$-faces.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Euler--Poincar\'e Formula]\label{thm:polytopes_euler_poincare}
  For every $d$-polytope,
  \[
    \sum_{k=0}^{d-1} (-1)^k f_k
      = 1 + (-1)^{d-1}
      = \chi(S^{d-1}),
  \]
  the Euler characteristic of the $(d-1)$-sphere, the boundary of the
  polytope.
\end{theorem}

For $d = 3$ this reads $f_0 - f_1 + f_2 = 2$; for $d = 2$ (a polygon) it
reads $f_0 = f_1$; for $d = 4$ it reads $f_0 - f_1 + f_2 - f_3 = 0$. The
formula is proved by induction on the dimension: attaching a new vertex to
the boundary triangulation changes the alternating sum by $0$ because the
boundary is a triangulated sphere. It is the most useful check in
higher-dimensional computing: every face lattice, mesh, and hull satisfies
it, and violations signal exactly the degenerate inputs that robust
implementations must reject.

For a \textbf{simplicial} $3$-polytope, $3F = 2E$ (each facet has three
edges, each edge borders two facets); for a \textbf{simple} one, $3V = 2E$.
Substituting into Euler's formula gives the sharp counting bound.

\begin{theorem}[Facets of a 3-Polytope]\label{thm:polytopes_upper_bound_3d}
  A convex polytope in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with $V$ vertices has $F \le 2V - 4$
  facets, with equality iff all facets are triangles; dually, a simple
  polytope with $F$ facets has $V = 2F - 4$ vertices. A triangulated surface
  with $V$ vertices has $E = 3V - 6$ edges and $F = 2V - 4$ triangular
  faces.
\end{theorem}

These identities are why triangular meshes consume linear storage
(Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}): the edge and face counts of a triangular
surface are determined by its vertex count up to constants. The
dimensional generalization is the upper bound theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_upper_bound}), whose linear three-dimensional
case is exactly $F \le 2V - 4$.

\section{Polarity}
\label{sec:polytopes_polarity}

\textbf{Polarity} is the geometric duality that interchanges the two
descriptions of a polytope: the polar of a V-polytope is an H-polytope, and
vertices and facets are exchanged along with them.

\begin{definition}[Polar]\label{def:polytopes_polar}
  For a set $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ with $0 \in P$, the \textbf{polar} of
  $P$ is
  \[
    P^{\circ} = \{ y \in \mathbb{R}^d : \langle x, y \rangle \le 1
      \text{ for all } x \in P \} .
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Polar Duality]\label{thm:polytopes_polar_duality}
  If $P$ is a full-dimensional polytope with $0 \in \operatorname{int} P$,
  then $P^{\circ}$ is again a full-dimensional polytope with
  $0 \in \operatorname{int} P^{\circ}$, and polarity is an involution:
  $(P^{\circ})^{\circ} = P$.
\end{theorem}

The theorem follows from the Minkowski--Weyl theorem: if
$P = \operatorname{conv}\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$, then
$P^{\circ} = \{ y : \langle v_i, y \rangle \le 1 \text{ for all } i \}$, an
H-polytope, and applying the step again returns $P$ by separation
(Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_separating}). Polarity therefore converts every
V-representation into an H-representation and back, which makes it the
geometric engine of facet and vertex enumeration
(Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_computation}). It is related to, but distinct
from, the projective point--hyperplane duality of Section~\ref{sec:math_duality}
and Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_duality}: both interchange points and
hyperplanes, but polarity is an involution on polytopes that preserves
incidence, whereas projective duality reverses order. The computational
power of the projective form is analyzed in \cite{chazelle1985power}.

\begin{theorem}[Polarity Reverses the Face Lattice]\label{thm:polytopes_polar_reversal}
  Let $P$ be a full-dimensional polytope with $0 \in \operatorname{int} P$.
  Polarity induces an inclusion-reversing bijection between the faces of $P$
  and of $P^{\circ}$: the polar of a $k$-face $F$ of $P$ is the
  $(d - 1 - k)$-face
  \[
    \widehat{F} = \{ y \in P^{\circ} : \langle x, y \rangle = 1
      \text{ for all } x \in F \} .
  \]
  In particular, facets of $P$ correspond to vertices of $P^{\circ}$, and
  vertices of $P$ to facets of $P^{\circ}$.
\end{theorem}

The dimension reversal $k \leftrightarrow d-1-k$ is why a simplicial
polytope becomes a simple one under polarity
(Definition~\ref{def:polytopes_simple_simplicial}), and the normal fan of $P$
is the face fan of $P^{\circ}$ --- the normal cone of $F$ is the cone over
the polar face $\widehat{F}$ --- so the normal-fan picture of
Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_supporting_hyperplanes} and the polarity picture
coincide. The polar of the cube is the cross-polytope, the polar of a
simplex is a simplex, and the polar of the icosahedron is the dodecahedron.

\index{polar}\index{polarity}\index{dual polytope}

\begin{example}[Polar of the Cube]
  Let $P = [-1, 1]^3$. Then $P^{\circ} = \{ y : |y_1| + |y_2| + |y_3| \le 1
  \}$ is the octahedron with vertices $\pm e_1, \pm e_2, \pm e_3$: the eight
  vertices of $P$ become the eight facets of $P^{\circ}$, and the six facets
  of $P$ become the six vertices of $P^{\circ}$.
\end{example}

\section{Computing Higher-Dimensional Hulls}
\label{sec:polytopes_computation}

\subsection{1. Overview}

This section treats the computation of convex hulls in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and
above, together with the dual traversal problems of vertex and facet
enumeration. Three families of algorithms are central.

\textbf{Divide and conquer.} Preparata and Hong \cite{preparata1977} split
the point set by a plane, build the two subhulls recursively, and glue them
by a ``belt'' of triangles wrapping around the union, giving a deterministic
$O(n \log n)$ three-dimensional hull algorithm.

\textbf{Randomized incremental.} Add the points in random order, maintaining
a conflict graph between points and current facets; a new point either lies
inside the hull or destroys a connected patch of facets that is replaced by
new triangles. The paradigm is due to Clarkson and Shor \cite{clarkson1989}
and Seidel \cite{seidel1991}, who established the expected $O(n \log n)$
bound in three dimensions; the same machinery extends to fixed dimension
$d \ge 4$, where its complexity is governed by the upper bound theorem.

\textbf{Output-sensitive.} \textbf{Gift wrapping} \cite{chand1970} revolves a
plane around an edge to generate the next facet, in $O(n h)$ time for a
hull of $h$ facets; Chan's algorithm \cite{chan1996} achieves the optimal
$O(n \log h)$ by wrapping hulls of random samples.

The dual computational problem is \textbf{vertex enumeration}: given the
H-representation, list the vertices. Traversing the adjacency graph of
vertices by pivoting --- moving along an edge to improve a linear objective
--- is the combinatorial face of the simplex method
(Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}), and its correctness rests on the
Farkas lemma (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_farkas}) and the Fourier--Motzkin
elimination theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_fourier_motzkin}). The
section closes with these connections, which unify polytope theory with
linear programming.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Adjacency Graph]\label{def:polytopes_adjacency_graph}
  The \textbf{adjacency graph} (1-skeleton) of a polytope is the graph whose
  vertices are the vertices of $P$ and whose edges are the 1-faces of $P$.
  For a simple polytope the graph is $d$-regular, and for a 3-polytope it is
  planar and 3-connected (Steinitz's theorem).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Conflict and Conflict Graph]\label{def:polytopes_conflict}
  A point $p$ \textbf{conflicts} with a facet $f$ of
  $\operatorname{conv}(S)$ if $p$ lies strictly outside the closed halfspace
  bounded by $f$ that contains $\operatorname{conv}(S)$. The \textbf{conflict
  graph} stores, for every facet, the input points conflicting with it, and
  for every point, the conflicting facets.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Horizon]\label{def:polytopes_horizon}
  Let $p$ be a point outside the current hull. The \textbf{horizon} of $p$
  is the cycle of edges bounding the union of the facets that conflict with
  $p$; the facets spanned by $p$ and the horizon edges are the new facets
  created by inserting $p$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Vertex Enumeration]\label{def:polytopes_vertex_enumeration}
  \textbf{Vertex enumeration} is the problem of listing all vertices of the
  H-polytope $P = \{ x : Ax \le b \}$; \textbf{facet enumeration} is the
  dual problem of listing all facets of $P = \operatorname{conv}(S)$. By
  polarity (Section~\ref{sec:polytopes_polarity}) the two problems are
  equivalent.
\end{definition}

The algorithms assume \textbf{general position}: no four input points are
coplanar in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (no $d+1$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$), so every facet is a
simplex spanned by exactly $d$ points. Degenerate inputs are handled by
symbolic perturbation or by the exact predicates of
Section~\ref{sec:robust_predicates}.

\subsection{3. Theory}

\textbf{Divide and conquer in 3D.} Preparata--Hong \cite{preparata1977}
recursively computes the hulls $H_1, H_2$ of the two halves, then merges
them by finding the two supporting planes that touch both hulls, tracing the
``visible'' band of edges on each, and connecting the bands by a belt of
triangular facets. Each merge runs in $O(n)$ time because the hulls are
convex and the bands are found by walking, giving the recurrence
$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n)$ and $T(n) = O(n \log n)$. Correctness rests on the
observation that every facet of the merged hull is either a facet of a
subhull or a new triangle spanning the two bands, validated by the
orientation tests of Section~\ref{sec:three_dimensional_predicates}.

\textbf{Randomized incremental.} The randomized incremental algorithm
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:polytopes_incremental_hull}) inserts the points in a
random order and maintains the conflict graph. When $p_i$ is inserted, its
conflicting facets are deleted and replaced by the fan of triangles over its
horizon; the key geometric fact is that the conflicting facets form a
topological disk in $\mathbb{R}^3$, so the horizon is a single cycle. The
analysis is by \textbf{backward analysis}: a facet created at step $i$
survives until the insertion of a point conflicting with it, and summing the
expected creation times bounds the total work.

\begin{theorem}[Randomized Incremental Hull, 3D]\label{thm:polytopes_incremental_3d}
  For $n$ points in general position in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the randomized
  incremental algorithm constructs the convex hull in expected
  $O(n \log n)$ time and $O(n)$ space, and the expected number of facets
  created over the run is $O(n)$.
\end{theorem}

The accounting is in \cite{clarkson1989,deberg2008}. The same scheme works
in higher dimensions, where it is governed by the upper bound theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Randomized Incremental Hull, Higher Dimensions]\label{thm:polytopes_incremental_d}
  For points in general position in fixed dimension $d \ge 4$, the
  randomized incremental algorithm runs in expected
  $O(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor} \log n)$ time, and this is optimal in the
  worst case: the output hull can have $\Theta(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})$
  facets (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_upper_bound}). Chazelle's deterministic
  algorithm achieves $O(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor} + n \log n)$
  \cite{chazelle1993}.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Output-sensitive algorithms.} When the hull is small, both of the
above are wasteful. \textbf{Gift wrapping} \cite{chand1970}
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:polytopes_gift_wrapping}) generates the facets one at a
time by pivoting a supporting plane around each facet edge, choosing the
point minimizing the angle with the current facet; each facet costs $O(n)$,
giving $O(n h)$ time. Chan's algorithm \cite{chan1996} computes the hulls of
random samples of size $h$ and wraps their union, iterating on the estimate
of $h$, in $O(n \log h)$ time, the optimal output-sensitive bound (the
planar version is Section~\ref{sec:chans_algorithm},
Algorithm~\ref{alg:chans_hull}).

\textbf{Traversal and vertex enumeration.} Given an H-representation, the
vertices can be visited by traversing the adjacency graph: from a vertex,
each admissible pivoting direction leads across an edge to an adjacent
vertex, and the simplex method of Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}
performs exactly this traversal under a linear objective. Two classical
exact tools are complementary. \textbf{Fourier--Motzkin elimination}
eliminates one variable at a time by combining every pair of constraints
with opposite coefficient signs, deciding feasibility and projecting
polytopes exactly.

\begin{theorem}[Fourier--Motzkin Elimination]\label{thm:polytopes_fourier_motzkin}
  Projecting the polyhedron $P = \{ x : Ax \le b \} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$
  onto $\mathbb{R}^{d-1}$ (deleting one coordinate) yields the polyhedron
  defined by the linear system produced by
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:polytopes_fourier_motzkin}. In particular $P$ is
  feasible iff the eliminated system is feasible, so feasibility is decided
  by iterated elimination.
\end{theorem}

The \textbf{double description} method \cite{motzkin1953} maintains a
V-and-H pair $(V, H)$ for the same polytope, inserting halfspaces one at a
time and adding the vertices each new halfspace cuts off; the Farkas lemma
certifies that a candidate point is a new vertex. For output-polynomial
guarantees in the worst case, the \textbf{reverse search} of Avis and
Fukuda \cite{avis1992} walks the adjacency graph in a deterministic
cycle-free order (each vertex points to its unique parent), enumerating
every vertex in time polynomial in the input size per vertex. Together, the
Farkas lemma and Fourier--Motzkin elimination are the polytopal form of
linear-programming duality: feasibility, infeasibility certificates, and
vertex description are one story, told in the language of
Section~\ref{sec:halfplane_intersection}.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Randomized Incremental 3D Convex Hull}
\label{alg:polytopes_incremental_hull}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A point set $S \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ in general position.
\Ensure The facets of $\operatorname{conv}(S)$, oriented so normals point outward.
\Function{IncrementalHull}{$S$}
  \State choose a random permutation $p_1, \dots, p_n$ of $S$
  \State $\mathcal{F} \gets$ the facets of the hull of the first 4 points (a tetrahedron)
  \State build the conflict lists of all points against $\mathcal{F}$
  \For{$i = 5$ \textbf{to} $n$}
    \State $\mathcal{C} \gets$ facets of $\mathcal{F}$ conflicting with $p_i$
    \If{$\mathcal{C}$ is empty}
      \State continue  \Comment{$p_i$ lies inside the current hull}
    \EndIf
    \State delete $\mathcal{C}$ from $\mathcal{F}$; trace the horizon cycle bounding $\mathcal{C}$
    \For{each horizon edge $e$}
      \State add the triangle $\operatorname{conv}(\{p_i\} \cup e)$ to $\mathcal{F}$
    \EndFor
    \State update the conflict lists of the remaining points against the new facets
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathcal{F}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Gift Wrapping in 3D}
\label{alg:polytopes_gift_wrapping}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A point set $S \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ in general position and a facet $f_0$ of $\operatorname{conv}(S)$.
\Ensure All facets of $\operatorname{conv}(S)$.
\Function{GiftWrap}{$S$, $f_0$}
  \State $\mathcal{F} \gets \emptyset$; $Q \gets \{ f_0 \}$; mark $f_0$ discovered
  \While{$Q$ is not empty}
    \State pop $f$ from $Q$; add $f$ to $\mathcal{F}$
    \For{each edge $e$ of $f$}
      \If{$e$ is shared with a discovered facet}
        \State continue  \Comment{the edge is already wrapped}
      \EndIf
      \State $H \gets$ plane through $e$ and the third vertex of $f$
      \State $p \gets$ point of $S$ minimizing the angle of $\operatorname{conv}(\{e\} \cup \{p\})$ with $H$
      \State $g \gets \operatorname{conv}(\{e\} \cup \{p\})$; mark $g$ discovered; push $g$ onto $Q$
    \EndFor
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $\mathcal{F}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Fourier--Motzkin Elimination of One Variable}
\label{alg:polytopes_fourier_motzkin}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A system $Ax \le b$ in variables $x_1, \dots, x_d$ and an index $j$.
\Ensure A system in the remaining $d-1$ variables whose solutions are exactly the projections of the solutions of $Ax \le b$.
\Function{FourierMotzkin}{$A$, $b$, $j$}
  \State partition the rows by the sign of $\alpha_i = A_{ij}$, the coefficient of $x_j$
  \State $L \gets$ rows with $\alpha_i > 0$; $E \gets$ rows with $\alpha_i = 0$; $U \gets$ rows with $\alpha_i < 0$
  \For{each pair $(\ell, u)$ of a row from $L$ and a row from $U$}
    \State add the scaled sum of the two rows that cancels $x_j$
  \EndFor
  \State \Return the system of all rows of $E$ plus the combination rows
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Random permutation}: correctness is independent of the order,
  but the expected time depends on randomness; a fixed seed gives
  reproducible runs, and adversarial orders are the failure mode that
  randomization avoids.
\item \textbf{General position}: coplanar points break the ``horizon is a
  cycle'' and ``facet is a triangle'' invariants; symbolic perturbation
  restores the assumptions without moving the output.
\item \textbf{Conflict maintenance}: the full conflict graph uses expected
  $O(n)$ space in 3D and is the standard choice; alternatively, locate each
  new point inside a facet by walking (Section~\ref{sec:incremental_3d_hull}),
  trading space for the point-location cost.
\item \textbf{Output-sensitivity}: for hulls small compared with the input,
  gift wrapping or Chan's algorithm should be selected on the basis of an
  estimate of $h$, the number of output facets.
\item \textbf{Exact arithmetic}: orientation and incircle-style tests
  (Sections~\ref{sec:three_dimensional_predicates} and
  \ref{sec:robust_predicates}) must be exact or certified; a wrong
  orientation sign converts a valid facet into a non-facet and corrupts the
  entire output.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Preparata--Hong divide and conquer}: $O(n \log n)$ time,
  $O(n)$ space, deterministic, in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
\item \textbf{Randomized incremental}: expected $O(n \log n)$ time and
  $O(n)$ space in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_incremental_3d});
  expected $O(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor} \log n)$ time in fixed dimension
  $d \ge 4$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_incremental_d}).
\item \textbf{Gift wrapping}: $O(n h)$ time for a hull with $h$ facets,
  $O(n + h)$ space.
\item \textbf{Chan}: $O(n \log h)$ time, the optimal output-sensitive bound
  \cite{chan1996}.
\item \textbf{Vertex enumeration}: output-polynomial by reverse search
  \cite{avis1992} --- each vertex in time polynomial in the input size;
  Fourier--Motzkin decides feasibility exactly but may square the constraint
  count per elimination step (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_fourier_motzkin}).
\end{itemize}

The lower bounds are information-theoretic: the output hull has up to
$\Theta(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})$ facets
(Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_upper_bound}), so every correct algorithm in
dimension $d \ge 4$ takes $\Omega(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})$ time in the
worst case, which the algorithms above attain.

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mesh generation and geometric computing}: the three-dimensional
  hull is the boundary representation of convex meshes and of the Delaunay
  and Voronoi structures of Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}; the
  algorithms here are the ancestors of the incremental polyhedral
  constructions of Chapter~\ref{ch:polyhedral_geometry}.
\item \textbf{Linear programming and optimization}: vertex enumeration and
  the Farkas/Fourier--Motzkin machinery are the geometric skeleton of the
  simplex method and of the duality certificates of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming} (Section~\ref{sec:halfplane_intersection}).
\item \textbf{Higher-dimensional data}: hulls and support functions of
  high-dimensional point clouds are primitives of the statistics and
  learning pipelines of Chapter~\ref{ch:high_dimensional}.
\item \textbf{Visibility and duality}: point--hyperplane duality turns
  convex hull problems into halfplane intersection problems
  (Section~\ref{sec:duality_convex_hulls}), so the algorithms here solve
  both.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Euler check}: verify $V - E + F = 2$ on every output hull
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_euler}); for a triangulated hull, verify
  $F = 2V - 4$. Any violation signals a duplicate or a mis-oriented facet.
\item \textbf{Dual agreement}: build the polar of the output and verify the
  face-lattice reversal of Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_polar_reversal}, then
  run vertex enumeration on the polar to recover the input vertices.
\item \textbf{Algorithm agreement}: compare the facet sets produced by the
  randomized incremental and gift-wrapping algorithms on the same inputs;
  they must coincide up to orientation and order.
\item \textbf{Degenerate inputs}: collinear and coplanar sets, duplicate
  points, and points on the hull boundary must be absorbed by the
  general-position convention and must not create zero-area facets.
\item \textbf{Conflict consistency}: after every insertion, the removed
  facets must be exactly the conflict set of $p_i$; assert that the horizon
  is a single cycle and that no new facet conflicts with an unprocessed
  point that does not see it.
\item \textbf{Stress dimensions}: in $\mathbb{R}^4$, hulls of points on the
  moment curve must realize the upper bound
  $\Theta(n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor})$ facets, testing the output-size handling
  of the higher-dimensional machinery.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:polytopes_exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Prove the Minkowski--Weyl theorem in the plane: show that a bounded
    intersection of halfplanes is the convex hull of its vertices, and
    conversely that the convex hull of a finite planar point set is a
    bounded intersection of halfplanes.
  \item Using Euler's formula, prove that a $3$-polytope has $F \le 2V - 4$
    facets, with equality iff all facets are triangles, and derive the dual
    statement $V = 2F - 4$ for simple polytopes.
  \item State and prove Radon's theorem for four points in the plane
    explicitly: the four points either form a convex quadrilateral, whose
    crossing diagonals give the Radon partition, or have one point inside
    the triangle of the other three.
  \item Compute the polar of the cube $[-1,1]^3$ and verify (i) that it is
    the octahedron, (ii) that $(P^{\circ})^{\circ} = P$, and (iii) the
    face-lattice reversal of Theorem~\ref{thm:polytopes_polar_reversal} by
    matching facets of $P$ to vertices of $P^{\circ}$.
  \item Prove Carath\'eodory's theorem by the minimal-convex-combination
    argument, and show the bound $d+1$ is tight by exhibiting a point of a
    $d$-simplex that cannot be represented with fewer points.
  \item Use Helly's theorem to prove that a finite family of convex sets in
    $\mathbb{R}^d$ has empty intersection iff some $d+1$ members already do,
    and derive from this the size of the infeasibility certificate for a
    linear system in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  \item Simulate Algorithm~\ref{alg:polytopes_incremental_hull} by hand on
    six points in general position in $\mathbb{R}^3$, inserting them in a
    fixed order, and verify that the conflicted facets form a single cycle
    (the horizon) and that $V - E + F = 2$ holds after every step.
  \item Implement Algorithm~\ref{alg:polytopes_fourier_motzkin} for $d = 2$
    and use it to decide feasibility of a system of halfplanes; compare your
    answers with the halfplane intersection algorithm of
    Section~\ref{sec:halfplane_intersection}.
\end{enumerate}
