\chapter{Mathematical Foundations}
\label{ch:math_foundations}

This chapter collects the mathematical vocabulary used throughout the book:
affine and vector spaces, inner products and metrics, lines and hyperplanes,
convexity, orientation, barycentric coordinates, duality, and the topological
notions needed to reason about meshes and complexes. It is not a full course
in any of these subjects; rather, it fixes notation and states the facts that
the algorithmic chapters rely on. Readers familiar with linear algebra and
point-set topology can skip directly to the chapter bibliographic notes.
References such as Matou\v{s}ek's \emph{Lectures on Discrete Geometry}
\cite{matousek1999} and de Berg et al.'s \emph{Computational Geometry:
Algorithms and Applications} \cite{deberg2008} provide more complete
treatments.

\section{Vectors and Affine Spaces}
\label{sec:math_vectors_affine}

Computational geometry is mostly carried out in $\mathbb{R}^d$, but it is
useful to keep the distinction between \emph{points} and \emph{vectors}.
A \textbf{vector} is an element of a vector space: it has a magnitude and a
direction and can be added, scaled, and compared. A \textbf{point} is a
location in space; it cannot be added to another point in a coordinate-free
way. The two are related through the translation operation: the vector $v$
carries a point $p$ to the point $p+v$.

\begin{definition}[Vector Space]
  A \textbf{vector space} over the field $\mathbb{R}$ is a set $V$ together
  with a zero element $\mathbf{0}$, a vector addition $u+v$, and a scalar
  multiplication $\lambda v$ satisfying the standard axioms: associativity
  and commutativity of addition, distributivity, existence of additive
  inverses, and compatibility of scalar multiplication with multiplication in
  $\mathbb{R}$. A \textbf{linear combination} of vectors $v_1,\dots,v_k$ is
  $\lambda_1 v_1 + \cdots + \lambda_k v_k$ with $\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Affine Space]
  An \textbf{affine space} over $\mathbb{R}$ is a set $A$ of \emph{points}
  together with a vector space $V$ of \emph{translations} and a mapping
  $(p,v) \mapsto p+v$ such that $p+\mathbf{0} = p$, $(p+u)+v = p+(u+v)$, and
  for every pair of points $p,q$ there is a unique vector $v$ with $q=p+v$,
  written $v = q - p$.
\end{definition}

The distinction matters for a practical reason: every algorithm in this book
that computes with coordinates implicitly chooses an origin. Results that are
independent of that choice are called \textbf{affine-invariant}; convexity,
collinearity, ratios of collinear lengths, and barycentric coordinates are
affine-invariant, while absolute positions and lengths are not. Choosing a
basis identifies the affine space $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $d$-tuples of reals,
which is how all the pseudocode in this book manipulates geometry.

The \textbf{affine hull} of a set $S\subseteq A$ is the smallest affine
subspace containing $S$. The affine hull of three non-collinear points is a
plane, of four non-coplanar points is $\mathbb{R}^3$, and more generally of
$d+1$ affinely independent points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is all of
$\mathbb{R}^d$. This notion is developed further in
Section~\ref{sec:math_affine_combinations}. Point sets whose affine hull is
low-dimensional (for example, collinear points in the plane) are called
\textbf{degenerate} for algorithms that assume full dimension; the topic is
treated in Section~\ref{sec:intro_degeneracies}.

\begin{example}
  In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the affine hull of two distinct points is the entire
  line through them, and the affine hull of any three non-collinear points is
  the whole plane. Algorithms that build, say, planar triangulations require
  the affine hull to be the plane; the Delaunay algorithm of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations} therefore assumes general
  position, perturbing degenerate inputs if necessary.
\end{example}

\index{affine space}\index{vector space}

\section{Inner Products and Euclidean Distance}
\label{sec:math_inner_products}

The standard Euclidean structure on $\mathbb{R}^d$ comes from the
\textbf{dot product} (inner product)
\[
  \langle u, v \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^d u_i v_i,
\]
which induces the Euclidean norm $\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$ and the
Euclidean distance $\mathrm{dist}(p,q) = \|p-q\|$.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy--Schwarz Inequality]\label{thm:math_cauchy_schwarz}
  For all vectors $u,v \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $|\langle u, v\rangle| \le \|u\|
  \cdot \|v\|$, with equality if and only if $u$ and $v$ are scalar multiples
  of one another.
\end{theorem}

Cauchy--Schwarz implies the triangle inequality
$\|u+v\| \le \|u\| + \|v\|$, which is exactly the requirement that the
Euclidean norm define a metric (Section~\ref{sec:math_norms_metrics}). It also
gives the cosine rule
\[
  \cos \theta = \frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}
\]
for the angle $\theta$ between nonzero vectors, so the dot product lets us
test orthogonality: $u \perp v$ iff $\langle u,v\rangle = 0$.

The dot product is the central computational primitive of the geometry
algorithms in this book. Predicates such as orientation and in-circle tests
(Section~\ref{sec:orientation_predicate}) are determinants built from dot
products; projections onto lines and planes are dot products followed by
scaling; and the closest-point queries of Chapter~\ref{ch:distance_paths} all
reduce to evaluating inner products. Because floating-point arithmetic can
make a nearly zero inner product change sign, the robust evaluation methods
of Section~\ref{sec:robust_predicates} are essential when a dot product
determines a topological decision.

\index{inner product}\index{dot product}\index{Euclidean distance}

\section{Lines and Hyperplanes}
\label{sec:math_lines_hyperplanes}

A \textbf{line} through a point $p$ with direction $v \neq \mathbf{0}$ has
the parametric form $\ell(t) = p + t v$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$. The line through
two distinct points $p$ and $q$ is $\ell(t) = (1-t)p + tq$. A
\textbf{segment} restricts the parameter: $[p,q] = \{(1-t)p + tq : 0 \le t
\le 1\}$.

A \textbf{hyperplane} in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is the set of points $x$ satisfying
a nontrivial linear equation
\[
  H = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^d : \langle n, x\rangle = c \},
\]
where $n \neq \mathbf{0}$ is a \textbf{normal vector} and $c$ is a scalar.
In $\mathbb{R}^2$ a hyperplane is a line; in $\mathbb{R}^3$ it is a plane.
The normal $n$ is unique up to nonzero scaling, and the unit normal
$n/\|n\|$ is the direction perpendicular to the hyperplane.

The signed distance from a point $x$ to the hyperplane $H$ is
\[
  \delta(x) = \frac{\langle n, x\rangle - c}{\|n\|},
\]
which is positive on one side of $H$ and negative on the other. This signed
distance is the basic building block for many predicates and proximity
queries: the distance from a point to a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is
$|\delta(x)|$, and the distance between a point and a plane in
$\mathbb{R}^3$ is the same expression. Hyperplanes divide space into two
\textbf{half-spaces} (Section~\ref{sec:math_half_spaces}), and every convex
polyhedron can be written as the intersection of finitely many half-spaces,
one per facet.

The point-to-line and point-to-plane distance formulas are used throughout
Chapter~\ref{ch:distance_paths}; the sign of $\delta$ is the raw material of
the orientation predicates of Section~\ref{sec:orientation_predicate}.

\index{line}\index{hyperplane}\index{plane}

\section{Affine Combinations}
\label{sec:math_affine_combinations}

\begin{definition}[Affine Combination]\label{def:math_affine_combination}
  A point $x = \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i p_i$ is an \textbf{affine combination}
  of the points $p_1,\dots,p_k$ if $\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i = 1$.
\end{definition}

The condition $\sum \lambda_i = 1$ is exactly what makes the combination
independent of the choice of origin: translating all $p_i$ by $v$ moves $x$
by $(\sum \lambda_i) v = v$. Affine combinations are closed under taking
further affine combinations, so the set of all affine combinations of a
point set is its affine hull (Section~\ref{sec:math_vectors_affine}).

A set of points $p_1,\dots,p_k$ is \textbf{affinely independent} if no point
is an affine combination of the others; equivalently, the vectors
$p_2 - p_1, \dots, p_k - p_1$ are linearly independent. In $\mathbb{R}^d$ at
most $d+1$ points are affinely independent. An \textbf{affine frame} is an
affinely independent set of $d+1$ points $p_0,\dots,p_d$ in
$\mathbb{R}^d$; every point has a unique representation as an affine
combination of the frame, whose coefficients are the
\textbf{barycentric coordinates} of Section~\ref{sec:math_barycentric}.

Affine independence is the coordinate-level meaning of ``general position''
in Section~\ref{sec:intro_degeneracies}: planar algorithms typically assume
that no three points are collinear and no four points are concyclic, which is
equivalent to all triples (resp.\ quadruples) being affinely independent
(resp.\ affinely and spherically independent). The collinearity test itself
is the orientation determinant of Section~\ref{sec:math_orientation_determinants}.

\index{affine combination}\index{affine independence}

\section{Convex Combinations}
\label{sec:math_convex_combinations}

\begin{definition}[Convex Combination and Convex Hull]\label{def:math_convex_hull}
  An affine combination $x = \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i p_i$ is a
  \textbf{convex combination} if in addition $\lambda_i \ge 0$ for all $i$.
  A set $C$ is \textbf{convex} if it contains every convex combination of its
  points. The \textbf{convex hull} $\operatorname{conv}(S)$ of a set $S$ is
  the set of all convex combinations of points of $S$; it is the smallest
  convex set containing $S$.
\end{definition}

Convex sets are closed under intersection and under Minkowski sums
(Section~\ref{sec:math_separating_surfaces} treats one of their most
important structural properties). The convex hull of a finite point set is a
convex polytope whose vertices form a subset of the input points; computing
it is Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_hulls}. The \textbf{vertices} of
$\operatorname{conv}(S)$ are exactly those points of $S$ that are not convex
combinations of the others.

\begin{theorem}[Carath\'eodory]\label{thm:math_caratheodory}
  In $\mathbb{R}^d$, every point of the convex hull of a set $S$ is a convex
  combination of at most $d+1$ points of $S$.
\end{theorem}

Carath\'eodory's theorem justifies the ``small certificates'' that appear in
many algorithms: to check whether a point lies in the convex hull it suffices
to search for a small subset witnessing the inclusion, which is the basis of
the separation-based membership tests in Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_hulls} and
Section~\ref{sec:math_half_spaces}. The proof is the standard one: write the
point with the minimal number $k$ of terms, and if $k > d+1$ the difference
vectors are linearly dependent, so one can perturb the coefficients to zero
one of them while preserving convexity.

\index{convex set}\index{convex hull}\index{Carath\'eodory's theorem}

\section{Orientation and Determinants}
\label{sec:math_orientation_determinants}

The \textbf{orientation} of an ordered point triple $(p,q,r)$ in
$\mathbb{R}^2$ records whether $r$ lies to the left of, on, or to the right
of the directed line from $p$ to $q$. It is the sign of the determinant
\[
  \operatorname{orient}(p,q,r) =
  \det\begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 1 & 1\\
    p_x & q_x & r_x\\
    p_y & q_y & r_y
  \end{pmatrix}
  = (q_x - p_x)(r_y - p_y) - (q_y - p_y)(r_x - p_x),
\]
which equals $2 \cdot \text{(signed area)}$ of the triangle $\triangle pqr$
(Section~\ref{sec:math_signed_area_volume}). The value is positive when the
turning from $p\to q$ to $p\to r$ is counterclockwise, negative when
clockwise, and zero exactly when the three points are collinear.

In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the orientation of an ordered quadruple $(p,q,r,s)$ is
the sign of the $4\times 4$ determinant with rows $(1, p_x, p_y, p_z)$, etc.,
and records whether $s$ lies above or below the plane through $p,q,r$.

Orientation is the fundamental \textbf{predicate} of computational
geometry: it decides convexity of hulls, the validity of triangulations,
point-in-polygon tests, segment intersection, and the legality of edge flips
in the Delaunay construction (Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}).
Because the sign of a nearly zero determinant is unstable in floating point,
production implementations use the adaptive exact evaluation described in
Section~\ref{sec:robust_predicates}. The chapter on geometric predicates
(Section~\ref{sec:orientation_predicate}) develops these tests in detail.

\begin{remark}
  Orientation tests can be interpreted as the sign of a
  $d+1$-dimensional determinant built from homogeneous coordinates, which
  unifies the 2D and 3D cases: see Section~\ref{sec:math_homogeneous} for the
  coordinate transform and Section~\ref{sec:math_orientation_determinants}
  references for the predicate literature.
\end{remark}

\index{orientation}\index{determinant}\index{predicate}

\section{Signed Area and Signed Volume}
\label{sec:math_signed_area_volume}

The \textbf{signed area} of an ordered polygon $p_1,\dots,p_n$ in the plane
is given by the shoelace formula
\[
  A = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i y_{i+1} - x_{i+1} y_i),
\]
with indices taken modulo $n$. The area is positive if the boundary is
oriented counterclockwise and negative if clockwise; for a simple polygon
the absolute value is the usual area. The shoelace formula underlies the
area computations in Chapter~\ref{ch:polygon_ops} and the
planarity/orientation fixes used in polygon repair.

In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the \textbf{signed volume} of a tetrahedron with ordered
vertices $p,q,r,s$ is $\frac{1}{6}$ times the scalar triple product
\[
  V = \frac{1}{6} \bigl\langle (q-p) \times (r-p),\, s-p \bigr\rangle,
\]
which is $\frac{1}{6}$ of the orientation determinant of
Section~\ref{sec:math_orientation_determinants}. The signed volume of a
polyhedron is obtained by summing the signed volumes of tetrahedra formed
with a fixed apex; this is the divergence-theorem (vector-calculus) way to
compute volumes of arbitrary closed meshes, used in Chapter~\ref{ch:polyhedral_geometry}
for volume, center of mass, and moments.

Signed quantities are more than a convenience: they determine sidedness and
orientation robustly and allow ``signed'' triangles to cancel outside a
region, which is exactly how triangulated surface areas and enclosed volumes
are computed without explicit inside/outside tests. The sign convention must
be fixed consistently; the book uses counterclockwise (positive) polygons in
the plane and positively oriented (right-handed) tetrahedra in space.

\index{signed area}\index{signed volume}\index{shoelace formula}

\section{Barycentric Coordinates}
\label{sec:math_barycentric}

Let $p_0,\dots,p_d$ be an affine frame of $\mathbb{R}^d$ (Section~\ref{sec:math_affine_combinations}).
Every point $x$ has unique coefficients $(\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_d)$ with
$\sum_i \lambda_i = 1$ and $x = \sum_i \lambda_i p_i$, called the
\textbf{barycentric coordinates} of $x$ with respect to the frame.

For a triangle $\triangle ABC$ in the plane, the barycentric coordinates
are the (signed) area ratios
\[
  \lambda_A = \frac{A(\triangle xBC)}{A(\triangle ABC)},\quad
  \lambda_B = \frac{A(\triangle xCA)}{A(\triangle ABC)},\quad
  \lambda_C = \frac{A(\triangle xAB)}{A(\triangle ABC)},
\]
where $A(\cdot)$ is the signed area of Section~\ref{sec:math_signed_area_volume}.
The point $x$ lies inside the triangle iff all three coordinates are
nonnegative; on the boundary iff one is zero; and outside iff some coordinate
is negative. This yields a robust point-in-triangle test built from three
orientation evaluations, and it gives the linear interpolation formula used
for color, texture, and data interpolation on meshes.

In a tetrahedron $p_0 p_1 p_2 p_3$, the barycentric coordinates are the
ratios of the four signed volumes
\[
  \lambda_i = \frac{V(\triangle x p_j p_k p_l)}{V(\triangle p_0 p_1 p_2 p_3)},
\]
where $\{i,j,k,l\}$ is a permutation of $\{0,1,2,3\}$. Barycentric
coordinates are the foundation of the linear finite-element basis on
simplices used throughout Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis} and
Chapter~\ref{ch:sampling}; generalized (non-affine) coordinates for
arbitrary polygons are used in the mesh and parameterization literature.

\index{barycentric coordinates}

\section{Half-Spaces}
\label{sec:math_half_spaces}

For a hyperplane $H = \{x : \langle n,x\rangle = c\}$, the two associated
closed \textbf{half-spaces} are
\[
  H^+ = \{x : \langle n,x\rangle \ge c\},\qquad
  H^- = \{x : \langle n,x\rangle \le c\},
\]
and the open half-spaces are defined by strict inequalities. Every
half-space is convex, and the intersection of any family of half-spaces is
convex. The convex hull of Section~\ref{sec:math_convex_combinations} is
related to half-spaces by a separation result: a convex set is the
intersection of all half-spaces that contain it.

\begin{theorem}[Hyperplane Separation]\label{thm:math_separation}
  Two disjoint nonempty convex sets $A$ and $B$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with one
  of them compact are strictly separated: there is a hyperplane
  $H = \{x : \langle n,x\rangle = c\}$ with $\langle n, a\rangle < c <
  \langle n, b\rangle$ for all $a\in A$, $b\in B$.
\end{theorem}

The half-space representation is used pervasively:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a convex polygon in the plane is the intersection of the
    half-spaces of its directed edges (each edge is traversed so that the
    polygon lies to the left);
  \item a convex polyhedron in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the intersection of the
    half-spaces of its facets, one per face;
  \item the feasible region of a linear program is an intersection of
    half-spaces (Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}), and the linear
    programming algorithms solve exactly the question of whether such an
    intersection is empty;
  \item point-in-convex-polygon and polygon-clipping algorithms test
    membership by checking the side of each edge half-space.
\end{itemize}

Because half-space intersection is how convex objects are stored, the
boolean operations on convex solids of Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_intersection}
reduce to the algebra of half-spaces and their boundary facets.

\index{half-space}\index{hyperplane separation}

\section{Hyperplanes and Separating Surfaces}
\label{sec:math_separating_surfaces}

Theorem~\ref{thm:math_separation} (Hyperplane Separation) is the
one-dimensional separation that computational geometry uses constantly. In
$\mathbb{R}^2$, two disjoint compact convex sets are separated by a line
that can be moved until it is \textbf{supporting} both sets simultaneously,
touching each in a point or a segment. Such a line is a \textbf{common
supporting line} and exists exactly when the two sets are disjoint.

For convex polygons and convex polyhedra, separation can be decided
algorithmically: two convex polygons in the plane are disjoint iff there is
a line parallel to one of their edges that separates them, and two convex
polyhedra in $\mathbb{R}^3$ are disjoint iff there is a separating plane
parallel to one of their faces, or a plane perpendicular to a cross product
of a pair of edges. This is the \textbf{separating axis theorem}, the basis
of the collision and intersection tests in Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_intersection}
and of the broad-phase narrowing in motion planning
(Chapter~\ref{ch:motion_planning}).

The Minkowski sum $A \oplus B = \{a + b : a\in A, b\in B\}$ reformulates
separation: $A$ and $B$ are disjoint iff $\mathbf{0} \notin A \oplus (-B)$.
Convexity is preserved by Minkowski sums, so the distance between two convex
sets is the distance from the origin to the Minkowski difference, which is
what the \textsc{gjk} distance algorithm computes
(Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_intersection}). This single equivalence is the
geometric engine behind most collision-detection code in computer graphics
and robotics.

\index{separating axis}\index{Minkowski sum}\index{supporting line}

\section{Norms and Metrics}
\label{sec:math_norms_metrics}

A \textbf{norm} on $\mathbb{R}^d$ is a function $\|\cdot\| \to
[0,\infty)$ that is positive definite ($\|x\|=0$ iff $x=\mathbf{0}$),
homogeneous ($\|\lambda x\| = |\lambda|\|x\|$), and satisfies the triangle
inequality. The standard family is the $\ell_p$ family
\[
  \|x\|_p = \left(\sum_{i=1}^d |x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}, \qquad
  \|x\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i|,
\]
with the Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ from
Section~\ref{sec:math_inner_products} as the $p=2$ member. A \textbf{metric}
on a set is a distance function that is positive definite, symmetric, and
satisfies the triangle inequality; every norm induces a metric via
$d(x,y) = \|x-y\|$.

Distances other than Euclidean arise naturally in geometry algorithms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the $\ell_1$ (\emph{Manhattan}) and $\ell_\infty$ (\emph{Chebyshev})
    metrics describe grid and axis-aligned motion and appear in
    L-shaped/rectilinear path problems (Chapter~\ref{ch:distance_paths}) and
    in the geometry of space-filling curves (Chapter~\ref{ch:space_filling_curves});
  \item the \textbf{geodesic distance} on a surface is the length of the
    shortest path constrained to the surface, a non-Euclidean metric central
    to Chapters~\ref{ch:sampling} and \ref{ch:mesh_analysis};
  \item in high dimension, $\ell_1$ distances are often more stable than
    $\ell_2$ for nearest-neighbor search, a phenomenon studied in
    Chapter~\ref{ch:high_dimensional}.
\end{itemize}

The choice of metric changes which structures are ``natural'': the Voronoi
diagram and Delaunay triangulation (Chapters~\ref{ch:voronoi_diagrams} and
\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}) are metric-dependent, and replacing the
Euclidean metric with an $\ell_p$ metric changes the combinatorial
complexity of the diagram. Metric properties also drive the correctness of
nearest-neighbor algorithms in Chapter~\ref{ch:proximity}, which assume at
minimum the triangle inequality.

\index{norm}\index{metric}\index{Manhattan distance}\index{Chebyshev distance}\index{geodesic distance}

\section{Transformations}
\label{sec:math_transformations}

An \textbf{isometry} (rigid motion) of $\mathbb{R}^d$ preserves distances
and is a composition of translations, rotations, and reflections. A
\textbf{similarity} additionally permits uniform scaling, preserving angles
and ratios of lengths. An \textbf{affine transformation}
$x \mapsto Mx + t$, with $M$ an invertible $d\times d$ matrix and $t$ a
translation vector, preserves collinearity, parallelism, ratios along lines,
and affine combinations, but not lengths or angles in general. A
\textbf{projective transformation} is the most general transformation that
maps lines to lines; it preserves incidence and cross-ratios but not
parallelism, and it is naturally expressed in homogeneous coordinates
(Section~\ref{sec:math_homogeneous}).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lllll}
\toprule
& lines & parallelism & distances & angles \\
\midrule
rigid & yes & yes & yes & yes \\
similarity & yes & yes & scaled & yes \\
affine & yes & yes & no & no \\
projective & yes & no & no & no \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The properties preserved by each class determine which algorithms are
invariant under which changes of coordinates. Clipping, convex hulls, and
barycentric interpolation are affine-invariant; Voronoi diagrams and Delaunay
triangulations are not (uniform scaling is harmless, anisotropic scaling
breaks them). Applications in graphics and computer vision rely on the full
projective group (Chapter~\ref{ch:applications_graphics}); mesh algorithms
typically use isometries and similarities (Chapters~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}).

Rigid transformations are built from an orthonormal basis: a rotation matrix
$R$ satisfies $R^\top R = I$ and $\det R = 1$. Computationally, applying a
rigid transformation to a set of points preserves every computed invariant
of distance-based algorithms, which is why point-cloud registration in
Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_matching} searches over rigid motions only.

\index{transformation}\index{isometry}\index{affine transformation}\index{projective transformation}

\section{Homogeneous Coordinates}
\label{sec:math_homogeneous}

A point $x = (x_1,\dots,x_d) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is represented in
\textbf{homogeneous coordinates} by any nonzero vector
$(\tilde{x}_1,\dots,\tilde{x}_d, w)$ with $x_i = \tilde{x}_i / w$; the
canonical choice is $(x_1,\dots,x_d,1)$. Two homogeneous vectors represent
the same point iff they are scalar multiples of one another, so the
projective space is the quotient of $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus\{0\}$ by
scaling. Points with $w=0$ are \textbf{points at infinity}, one per
direction.

The payoff is that translations become linear: the affine map
$x \mapsto Mx + t$ is the linear map
\[
  \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{x} \\ w \end{pmatrix}
  \mapsto
  \begin{pmatrix} M & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{x} \\ w \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Composing affine transformations is then ordinary matrix multiplication, and
the composition of projective transformations is linear throughout
(Section~\ref{sec:math_transformations}). In computer graphics this is why
model, view, and projection matrices are kept as $4\times 4$ matrices and
why the perspective divide $x_i = \tilde{x}_i/w$ is a single final division.

Homogeneous coordinates also unify the orientation determinant: the
$d$-dimensional orientation test of Section~\ref{sec:math_orientation_determinants}
is the determinant of the $(d+1)\times(d+1)$ matrix whose rows are the
homogeneous vectors of the points. This is the algebraic reason the same
predicate code scales from 2D to 3D, and the reason the in-circle test of
Section~\ref{sec:incircle_predicate} has a determinant of the same form.
Hyperplane duality (Section~\ref{sec:math_duality}) is also most simply
stated in homogeneous coordinates, where points and hyperplanes are dual
vectors.

\index{homogeneous coordinates}\index{point at infinity}\index{projective space}

\section{Duality}
\label{sec:math_duality}

Duality is a correspondence that swaps points with hyperplanes (or lines in
the plane) while preserving incidence and order. In the plane, the standard
\textbf{dual transform} maps the point $p = (a,b)$ to the line
\[
  p^* : y = ax - b,
\]
and maps the line $\ell : y = mx - c$ to the point $\ell^* = (m,c)$. The
transform is an involution up to the sign convention, preserves
above/below relations, and maps vertical lines to points at infinity
(Section~\ref{sec:math_homogeneous}). Its defining property is incidence
preservation: $p$ lies on $\ell$ iff $\ell^*$ lies on $p^*$.

Because convex hulls, intersections, and arrangements are transformed into
dual objects with the same combinatorial structure, duality converts
problems into easier ones. The convex hull of $n$ points in the plane is
dual to the intersection of the $n$ upper half-planes below their dual
lines; envelopes and lower envelopes
(Chapter~\ref{ch:envelopes}) are the dual picture of convex hulls; and the
Delaunay triangulation is dual to the arrangement of lifted tangent planes
(Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_lifting}). Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_duality}
develops the planar theory, including the half-plane intersection
formulation and its use in the randomized incremental algorithms.

In $\mathbb{R}^d$, the polar transform maps the point $a = (a_1,\dots,a_d)$
to the hyperplane $\{x : \langle a, x\rangle = 1\}$, and $0 \in
\operatorname{conv}(S)$ iff the corresponding half-spaces have empty
intersection; this is the polarity that underlies the convex-hull
algorithms for higher dimensions and the certificates used in
Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}. All duality transforms preserve the
ordering of points along a line through the relation between convex hull and
half-space intersection, so the complexity results for hulls transfer
directly to envelopes and back.

\section{Topological Preliminaries}
\label{sec:math_topology}

Computational geometry works mostly with finite combinatorial objects, but
their global structure is topological, and a small vocabulary goes a long
way. A set in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is \textbf{open} if every point has a small
ball around it contained in the set, and \textbf{closed} if its complement
is open; \textbf{compact} sets are closed and bounded. A set is
\textbf{connected} if it cannot be split into two disjoint nonempty open
pieces, and \textbf{path-connected} if any two points are joined by a
continuous curve. A \textbf{homeomorphism} is a continuous bijection with
continuous inverse; two sets are homeomorphic if one exists. The
\textbf{Euler characteristic} of a 2D polyhedral surface is
$V - E + F$, and for an orientable closed surface of genus $g$ it equals
$2 - 2g$; the same invariant in higher dimension is the alternating sum of
the numbers of faces.

A \textbf{simplicial complex} is a finite collection $K$ of simplices such
that every face of a simplex in $K$ is in $K$ and the intersection of any
two simplices is a face of both. A triangulated surface or mesh is a
simplicial complex in this sense, and all the mesh algorithms of
Chapters~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}, \ref{ch:computational_topology}, and
\ref{ch:half_edge} operate on the combinatorial structure of such a
complex. The \textbf{underlying space} $|K|$ is the topological space formed
by the union of the simplices; two complexes that triangulate the same
space are homeomorphic.

The \textbf{manifold} concept lets us describe when a mesh is locally
simple: a 2D surface is a manifold if every point has a neighborhood
homeomorphic to a disk (or to a half-disk on the boundary). Manifold
verification, orientation fixing, and genus computation are the subjects of
the mesh-analysis chapter; the half-edge and half-face data structures of
Chapter~\ref{ch:half_edge} are designed exactly to represent manifold
meshes, and the discrete exterior calculus and cohomology algorithms of
Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology} compute the Betti numbers
$\beta_0,\beta_1,\dots$ of such complexes. These foundations are summarized
in the notation table of the front matter and developed in the appendix
(Appendix~\ref{app:math_reference}).

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:math_exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Show that the set of affine combinations of a point set $S$ is the
    smallest affine subspace containing $S$, and that convex combinations
    give the smallest convex set.
  \item Prove that three points $p,q,r$ are collinear iff the orientation
    determinant of Section~\ref{sec:math_orientation_determinants} vanishes.
  \item Derive the shoelace formula by decomposing a polygon into
    triangles from a fixed vertex and canceling signed areas.
  \item Compute the barycentric coordinates of the incenter of a triangle
    in terms of side lengths, and verify that the three coordinates are
    nonnegative.
  \item Prove that a point lies in a convex polygon iff it is on the left
    side of every directed edge (for a counterclockwise boundary).
  \item Show that the intersection of the half-spaces of a convex polygon
    equals the polygon.
  \item For two axis-aligned boxes, give a condition for disjointness using
    the separating axis theorem of Section~\ref{sec:math_separating_surfaces}.
  \item Prove that the $\ell_1$, $\ell_2$, and $\ell_\infty$ norms are
    equivalent in the sense that each bounds the others up to constant
    factors, and give the constants for $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  \item Verify that the point--line duality $p=(a,b)\mapsto y=ax-b$ of
    Section~\ref{sec:math_duality} preserves the above/below relation.
  \item Compute the Euler characteristic of a triangulated tetrahedron and
    of a closed cube surface, and confirm $V-E+F=2$.
  \item Show that the two homogeneous vectors $(x_1,x_2,1)$ and
    $(\lambda x_1,\lambda x_2,\lambda)$ represent the same point.
  \item Prove that a rigid transformation preserves the distances of a
    point set, and that an arbitrary affine map need not.
\end{enumerate}
