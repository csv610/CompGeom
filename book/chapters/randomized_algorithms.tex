\chapter{Randomized Geometric Algorithms}
\label{ch:randomized_algorithms}

\section{Why Randomization Helps}
\label{sec:randomized_why}

Many geometric algorithms are fast on typical inputs yet slow on a small set of
adversarial configurations. Deterministic algorithms must be correct on every
input, so their analysis must cover the worst case. \textbf{Randomization}
\index{randomization}\index{randomized algorithm} breaks this deadlock: the
algorithm flips coins, and we analyze its \emph{expected} behavior over those
coin flips. Because the randomness lives inside the algorithm rather than in
the input, the expectation is meaningful for \emph{every} input.

\begin{definition}[Expected Time]\label{def:rand_expected_time}
  The \textbf{expected running time} of a randomized algorithm on input $I$ is
  the expectation, over the algorithm's random choices, of its running time on
  $I$. An algorithm whose expected time is $T(n)$ on all inputs of size $n$
  runs in \textbf{expected time} $O(T(n))$.
\end{definition}

Expected time differs from worst-case time (the deterministic guarantee) and
from average-case analysis (which averages over inputs): it is an
adversarial-input guarantee averaged over the algorithm's own randomness. Two
classes of randomized algorithms use this randomness differently.

\begin{definition}[Las Vegas Algorithm]\label{def:rand_las_vegas}
  A \textbf{Las Vegas algorithm} always produces a correct answer; only its
  running time is random. It can be restarted to enforce a hard time bound at
  the price of a tiny failure probability.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Monte Carlo Algorithm]\label{def:rand_monte_carlo}
  A \textbf{Monte Carlo algorithm} may produce an incorrect answer, but only
  with probability at most a reported bound $\delta$; its running time is
  typically deterministic.
\end{definition}

Nearly all algorithms in this chapter are Las Vegas: the randomized incremental
constructions are correct for every permutation, and randomness only controls
how fast they run. Monte Carlo behavior appears in the derived sampling
structures ($\varepsilon$-nets, Section~\ref{sec:randomized_eps_nets}),
analyzed with the concentration results of Section~\ref{sec:randomized_sampling}.
The classical references are the monographs of Motwani and Raghavan
\cite{motwani1995} and Mulmuley \cite{mulmuley1994}.

Why does randomization help in geometry specifically? Three phenomena recur.
First, many structures are \emph{order dependent}: an incremental construction
is fast for most insertion orders and slow only for a measure-zero set that an
adversary forces; a random order averages over equally likely orders. Second,
geometry is \emph{sample friendly}: a small random sample resembles the whole
input, so the sample can guide the computation (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_clarkson_shor}).
Third, random orders are \emph{easier to analyze} than the worst order, thanks
to Seidel's backward analysis (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_backward_analysis}).
The payoff: randomized incremental constructions achieve the optimal expected
bounds for Delaunay triangulations (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_delaunay}),
convex hulls (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_convex_hulls}), and linear
programming (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_linear_programming}), with simpler
algorithms than their deterministic counterparts.

\section{Randomized Incremental Construction}
\label{sec:randomized_incremental}

\subsection{1. Overview}

\textbf{Randomized incremental construction}\index{randomized incremental
construction} (RIC) inserts the input objects one at a time in a uniformly
random order and repairs the current geometric structure locally after each
insertion. Because the order is random, the repair cost at step $i$ is small in
expectation, giving expected $O(n \log n)$ total time. The pattern covers the
Delaunay triangulation (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_delaunay}), the trapezoidal
map of Chapter~\ref{ch:point_location} (Sections~\ref{sec:pointloc_trapezoidal}
--\ref{sec:pointloc_randomized}), convex hulls
(Section~\ref{sec:randomized_convex_hulls}), and linear programming
(Section~\ref{sec:randomized_linear_programming}); it is due to Clarkson and
Shor \cite{clarkson1989}, Seidel \cite{seidel1993}, and Mulmuley
\cite{mulmuley1994}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Incremental Construction]\label{def:rand_incremental_construction}
  Let $\mathcal{F}(P)$ be a geometric structure on an object set $P$ of size
  $n$. An \textbf{incremental construction} of $\mathcal{F}(P)$ fixes an
  insertion order $p_1, \dots, p_n$ and builds
  $\mathcal{F}(\{p_1,\dots,p_i\})$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$; it is
  \textbf{randomized} if the order is a uniformly random permutation.
\end{definition}

The cost of one insertion splits into \textbf{locating} the new object
(typically $O(\log i)$ via a history DAG, Section~\ref{sec:pointloc_search_dags}),
\textbf{inserting} it (destroying and creating cells), and \textbf{updating} the
conflict graph of Section~\ref{sec:randomized_conflict_graphs}. The insertion
cost is proportional to the destroyed cells, which backward analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:randomized_backward_analysis}) controls.

\subsection{3. Theory}

The accounting identity of RIC is that total work is proportional to the number
of cells ever created. If $c_i$ is the number created by the $i$-th insertion
and $C$ is the final structure, then
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}[c_i]
  = \sum_{T \in C} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Pr[\text{$T$ is created by insertion $i$}].
\]

\begin{theorem}[Expected Cost of RIC]\label{thm:rand_ric_expected}
  Consider a randomized incremental construction in which every cell of the
  final structure is determined by a constant number $d$ of inserted objects,
  and in which locating a new object costs $O(\log n)$ expected via a history
  DAG. Then the expected total time is $O(n \log n)$ and the expected space is
  $O(n)$ plus the size of the final structure.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Generic Randomized Incremental Construction}
\label{alg:rand_ric_generic}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A set $S$ of geometric objects and a random source.
\Ensure The structure $\mathcal{F}(S)$.
\Function{RIC}{$S$}
  \State $\sigma \gets$ uniformly random permutation of $S$
  \State $\mathcal{F} \gets$ structure on the first $d$ objects of $\sigma$
  \For{$i = d+1, \dots, |S|$}
    \State $o \gets \sigma[i]$
    \State locate $o$ in $\mathcal{F}$ (walk, history DAG, or conflict list)
    \State insert $o$: destroy the conflicting cells, create the replacements
    \State update the conflict graph for the created cells
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathcal{F}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Random order}: draw one permutation up front with a reproducible
  seed; multiple seeds are the standard variance-reduction tool for benchmarks.
\item \textbf{Base case and location}: handle the first $d$ objects directly
  (a big triangle in the Delaunay chapter); use a history DAG to preserve the
  $O(\log n)$ location guarantee.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: expected $O(n \log n)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:rand_ric_expected});
  worst case $O(n^2)$ for adversarial orders.
\item \textbf{Space}: expected $O(n)$ for the structure plus the history DAG.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item Delaunay triangulation, $O(n \log n)$ expected (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_delaunay}).
\item Trapezoidal map and point location (Chapter~\ref{ch:point_location}).
\item Linear programming and convex hulls (Sections~\ref{sec:randomized_linear_programming},
  \ref{sec:randomized_convex_hulls}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Permutation invariance}: the output must be identical for every
  random order; run several seeds and compare exactly.
\item \textbf{Degenerate objects}: duplicates and collinear triples must not
  create zero-area cells; use the exact predicates of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_predicates}.
\item \textbf{Reproducibility}: identical seeds must give identical runs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

The framework is due to Clarkson and Shor \cite{clarkson1989}, Seidel
\cite{seidel1993}, and Mulmuley \cite{mulmuley1994}; textbook treatments are in
de Berg et al.\ \cite{deberg2008} (Ch.~9) and Motwani--Raghavan
\cite{motwani1995}.

\section{Backward Analysis}
\label{sec:randomized_backward_analysis}

The most widely used tool for analyzing randomized incremental algorithms is
Seidel's \textbf{backward analysis}\index{backward analysis} \cite{seidel1993}:
instead of analyzing step $i$ \emph{forward}, analyze the random process
\emph{backwards}. The insertion of the points of $P$ in a random order is, in
reverse, a process that deletes points one at a time from a set whose elements
are uniformly distributed among the remaining points, so conditioning on the
remaining set is trivial: it is always a uniformly random subset of $P$.

\begin{definition}[Backward Analysis]\label{def:rand_backward_analysis}
  Let the points of $P$ be inserted in a uniformly random order and let
  $\mathcal{F}_i$ be the structure after $i$ insertions. A cell $T$ of the
  final structure is \textbf{created at step $i$} if $T \in \mathcal{F}_i$ but
  $T \notin \mathcal{F}_{i-1}$. \textbf{Backward analysis} estimates
  $\Pr[T \text{ created at step } i]$ by conditioning on the reverse process,
  where the defining objects of $T$ form a uniformly random $d$-tuple among the
  $i$ remaining objects.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Seidel's Backward-Analysis Lemma]\label{thm:rand_backward_analysis}
  Let $T$ be a cell of the final structure determined by exactly $d$ objects of
  the input, where $d$ is a constant. Inserting the input objects in a
  uniformly random order, the probability that $T$ is created at step $i$ is
  $O(d/i)$. Hence the expected number of cells created during step $i$ is
  $O(1)$ when every final cell is determined by a constant number of objects.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  In the reverse process, $T$ is created (in forward time) at the moment its
  last defining object is deleted. Conditioning on $T$ being present when $i$
  objects remain, the next deleted object is uniform among the $i$, and $T$ is
  created only if that object is one of the $d$ defining $T$: probability $d/i$.
  Summing over the $O(n)$ cells of the final structure, the expected number of
  creations per step is $O(1)$, and the total expected creations are $O(n)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Expected Creations in Delaunay]\label{ex:rand_backward_delaunay}
  Each final Delaunay triangle is determined by its three vertices, so a fixed
  triangle is created at step $i$ with probability $O(3/i)$ by
  Theorem~\ref{thm:rand_backward_analysis}. Summing over the $O(n)$ final
  triangles gives expected creation work $O(n)$; with $O(\log n)$ expected
  location per insertion through the history DAG
  (Section~\ref{sec:pointloc_search_dags}) this yields the $O(n \log n)$
  expected construction time.
\end{example}

Backward analysis was introduced by Seidel for the trapezoidal map
\cite{seidel1991}, developed into a general methodology in his survey
\cite{seidel1993}, and used by Guibas, Knuth, and Sharir for the randomized
incremental Delaunay triangulation \cite{guibas1992}.

\begin{remark}
  The same principle---analyze the moment a structure is \emph{created} by
  looking at the reverse deletion process---applies verbatim to convex hulls (a
  hull edge is created when its last vertex is deleted) and to linear
  programming (a constraint becomes binding when it is deleted).
\end{remark}

\section{Conflict Graphs}
\label{sec:randomized_conflict_graphs}

\subsection{1. Overview}

A randomized incremental construction must know, when a new object is inserted,
which cells it destroys. The \textbf{conflict graph}\index{conflict graph}
maintains this information explicitly, linking every uninserted object to the
cells it would destroy, and is updated as cells are created. It was introduced
in this form by Clarkson and Shor \cite{clarkson1989} and makes the RIC
template of Section~\ref{sec:randomized_incremental} concrete.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Conflict]\label{def:rand_conflict}
  An uninserted object $o$ \textbf{conflicts} with a cell $T$ of the current
  structure if inserting $o$ would destroy $T$. The set of cells conflicting
  with $o$ is its \textbf{conflict zone}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Conflict Graph]\label{def:rand_conflict_graph}
  The \textbf{conflict graph} is a bipartite graph whose left vertices are the
  uninserted objects, whose right vertices are the cells of the current
  structure, and whose edges are exactly the conflicts.
\end{definition}

For the Delaunay triangulation the conflict relation is the in-circle test of
Definition~\ref{def:incircle} (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_incremental}); for
convex hulls a point conflicts with the hull facets it lies outside
(Section~\ref{sec:randomized_convex_hulls}).

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of the Conflict-Graph Update]\label{thm:rand_conflict_update}
  Maintaining the conflict graph through a randomized incremental construction
  is correct, provided (i) the conflicts of every newly created cell are
  computed by checking each uninserted object in its neighborhood, and (ii)
  destroyed cells and their edges are removed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  Induction over insertions. Inserting $o$ destroys exactly the cells of its
  conflict zone (verified for each application, e.g., the cavity theorem of
  Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_incremental}), so those cells are removed
  correctly. A cell $T$ newly created by the insertion can be destroyed only by
  an object in the conflict zone of the destroyed cells spanning $T$; checking
  those objects restores the exact adjacency of $T$.
\end{proof}

The update cost is proportional to the sum over destroyed cells of their
conflict-graph neighbors, which backward analysis bounds by $O(1)$ per created
cell in expectation.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Conflict-Graph Maintenance}
\label{alg:rand_conflict_update}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require The current structure $\mathcal{F}$, the conflict graph $G$, an uninserted object $o$.
\Ensure $\mathcal{F}$ and $G$ updated after inserting $o$.
\Function{InsertWithConflicts}{$\mathcal{F}, G, o$}
  \State $\mathcal{Z} \gets$ conflict zone of $o$ in $G$
  \State $\mathcal{B} \gets$ boundary of the cells in $\mathcal{Z}$
  \State remove $\mathcal{Z}$ and their edges from $\mathcal{F}$ and $G$
  \State create the replacement cells from $o$ and $\mathcal{B}$
  \For{each new cell $T$}
    \For{each $q$ adjacent to a destroyed cell meeting $T$}
      \If{$q$ conflicts with $T$} \State add edge $(q, T)$ to $G$ \EndIf
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conflict predicate}: evaluated exactly with the robust predicates
  of Section~\ref{sec:robust_predicates} (in-circle for Delaunay, outside-facet
  for hulls).
\item \textbf{Neighborhood pruning}: test only objects near the destroyed cells
  against new cells, keeping the update local.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: expected $O(1)$ amortized per created cell; total expected
  $O(n \log n)$ for the applications of this chapter.
\item \textbf{Space}: expected $O(n)$ conflicts at any time.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item The Delaunay conflict graph of Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_randomized}.
\item Hull facets store the points outside them
  (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_convex_hulls}).
\item The vertical-decomposition counterpart in point location
  (Section~\ref{sec:pointloc_randomized}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exactness check}: recompute the conflict zone of a random
  uninserted object by brute force after each insertion and compare.
\item \textbf{Boundary ties and empty zones}: an object on a cell boundary
  conflicts with both adjacent cells; an object inside the hull has an empty
  zone and must be a no-op.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Clarkson, K.~L., Shor, P.~W. \cite{clarkson1989}.
\item Mulmuley, K. \cite{mulmuley1994}, Ch.~3, the theory of conflict
  maintenance.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Sec.~9.4.
\end{itemize}

\section{Clarkson--Shor Technique}
\label{sec:randomized_clarkson_shor}

\subsection{1. Overview}

The \textbf{Clarkson--Shor technique}\index{Clarkson--Shor technique} uses
random sampling to bound the number of geometric configurations: a random
sample of the input ``resembles'' the whole input, so the configurations
determined by the sample bound the number at a related ``level''. This yields
the classical $O(nk)$ bound on the number of $k$-sets in the plane and the
first optimal expected-time algorithms for convex hulls and Delaunay
triangulations \cite{clarkson1989}. It is the probabilistic counterpart of the
deterministic method of Chazelle and Friedman \cite{chazellefriedman1990}
(Section~\ref{sec:randomized_sampling}).

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[$k$-Set]\label{def:rand_k_set}
  Let $P$ be a set of $n$ points in the plane in general position. A
  \textbf{$k$-set} of $P$ is a subset of size $k$ separable by a half-plane
  (equivalently, cut off by a level-$k$ line of the dual arrangement; see
  Definition~\ref{def:point_level}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Configurations Determined by a Sample]\label{def:rand_configuration_space}
  Let $\mathcal{C}(P)$ be the set of \textbf{configurations} (cells) determined
  by $P$, each determined by a fixed constant number $d$ of objects. For
  $R \subseteq P$ and $T \in \mathcal{C}(P)$, $T$ is \textbf{crossing} $R$ if
  it is determined by a set meeting both $R$ and $P \setminus R$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Clarkson--Shor Sampling Lemma]\label{thm:rand_clarkson_shor}
  Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a configuration space as in
  Definition~\ref{def:rand_configuration_space}, and let $g_r(m)$ be the
  maximum, over sets of $m$ objects, of the expected number of configurations
  determined by a uniform random sample of size $r$. Then the number of
  configurations crossed by at most $k$ objects is $O(g_{n/(k+1)}(n))$. In
  particular, the number of $(\le k)$-sets of an $n$-point planar set is
  $O(nk)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  Let $R$ be a uniform random $r$-subset of $P$ and $X$ the number of
  configurations it determines, so $\mathbb{E}[X] \le g_r(n)$. A configuration
  $T$ is determined by $R$ exactly when $R$ contains the $d$ objects of $T$ and
  none of the (at most $k$) objects crossing $T$, with probability
  \[
    \binom{n-d-k}{r-d} \Big/ \binom{n}{r} \approx (r/n)^d (1 - r/n)^k.
  \]
  For $r = n/(k+1)$ both factors are $\Omega(1)$, so every ``level-$k$''
  configuration is counted with probability $\Omega(1)$; hence their number is
  $O(g_{n/(k+1)}(n))$. For planar point sets the sample's hull and
  triangulation are linear, so $g_r(n) = O(r)$ and the count is $O(nk)$.
\end{proof}

The combinatorial corollary is the $O(nk)$ bound on the $k$-level of a planar
line arrangement for each $k$ \cite{clarkson1989}, the basis of the complexity
analysis of arrangements (Chapter~\ref{ch:envelopes}). Choosing the sample as
the recursive input gives the expected-time bounds for hulls and Delaunay
triangulations.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Clarkson--Shor Sampling for Convex Hulls}
\label{alg:rand_clarkson_shor_hull}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require point set $P$ in the plane.
\Ensure the convex hull of $P$.
\Function{SampledHull}{$P$}
  \If{$|P|$ is small} \State \Return HullByAnyMethod($P$) \EndIf
  \State $R \gets$ uniform random sample of $P$ of size $\lceil |P|/2 \rceil$
  \State $H \gets$ SampledHull($R$)
  \State find the points of $P$ outside $H$ by the walk of Section~\ref{sec:pointloc_triangulations}
  \State insert the outside points into $H$ one at a time
  \State \Return the resulting hull
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sample size}: $r = n/(k+1)$ balances the two factors in the
  probability; a half-and-half split is a convenient recursion choice.
\item \textbf{Point location}: the walk locates the outside points in expected
  linear time.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Combinatorial}: $O(nk)$ $(\le k)$-sets and $O(nk)$ $(\le
  k)$-levels of a planar arrangement.
\item \textbf{Algorithmic}: expected $O(n \log n)$ for convex hulls and Voronoi
  diagrams \cite{clarkson1989,clarkson1993}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item The $k$-level complexity of arrangements (Chapter~\ref{ch:envelopes}).
\item Output-sensitive hull constructions
  (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_convex_hulls}).
\item Randomized closest-pair and Voronoi algorithms \cite{clarkson1993}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Combinatorial verification}: enumerate $k$-sets on small random
  sets by brute force and compare counts with the $O(nk)$ bound.
\item \textbf{Sampling bias}: verify sample uniformity by statistical tests
  when the random source is suspect.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Clarkson, K.~L., Shor, P.~W. \cite{clarkson1989}.
\item Clarkson, K.~L. \cite{clarkson1993}.
\item Chazelle, B., Friedman, J. \cite{chazellefriedman1990}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Random Sampling}
\label{sec:randomized_sampling}

Random sampling is the source of the randomness in this chapter: a small,
uniformly chosen subset of the input is used as a surrogate for the whole. The
justification is \textbf{concentration}: with high probability the relative
frequencies of every combinatorial range in the sample match those of the
input. This is the content of the Chernoff bound and its geometric refinements,
and it is what makes randomized construction and $\varepsilon$-nets
(Section~\ref{sec:randomized_eps_nets}) correct with high probability.

\begin{definition}[Range Space]\label{def:rand_range_space}
  A \textbf{range space} is a pair $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{R})$ of a ground set
  $\mathcal{X}$ and a family of subsets $\mathcal{R} \subseteq
  2^{\mathcal{X}}$, the \textbf{ranges}; the most important geometric examples
  are the half-plane ranges in the plane and half-space ranges in
  $\mathbb{R}^d$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[VC Dimension]\label{def:rand_vc_dimension}
  A set $S \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ is \textbf{shattered} by $\mathcal{R}$ if
  every subset of $S$ equals $S \cap R$ for some range $R$. The \textbf{VC
  dimension} of the range space is the size of the largest shattered set.
  Half-planes in the plane have VC dimension 3; half-spaces in $\mathbb{R}^d$
  have VC dimension $d + 1$ \cite{hausslerwelzl1987}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Chernoff Bound]\label{thm:rand_chernoff}
  Let $X_1, \dots, X_r$ be independent Bernoulli trials with $\Pr[X_j = 1] = p$
  and $\bar{X} = (1/r) \sum_j X_j$. For every $\delta \in (0,1)$,
  \[
    \Pr[\bar{X} \le (1-\delta)p] \le e^{-\delta^2 p r / 2},
    \qquad
    \Pr[\bar{X} \ge (1+\delta)p] \le e^{-\delta^2 p r / 3}.
  \]
\end{theorem}

Chernoff bounds control the number of ``bad'' events in a randomized
construction (a union bound converts them into a failure probability $\delta$
at the cost of a $O(\log 1/\delta)$ factor in the sample size) and the
deviation of sample statistics from their expectation. The geometric refinement
is the \textbf{random sampling lemma}.

\begin{theorem}[Random Sampling Lemma]\label{thm:rand_sampling_lemma}
  Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{R})$ be a range space of VC dimension $d$ and let
  $R$ be a uniform random sample of $r$ points of $\mathcal{X}$. With
  probability at least $1 - \delta$, simultaneously for all ranges $T \in
  \mathcal{R}$,
  \[
    \left| \frac{|R \cap T|}{r} - \frac{|T|}{n} \right| \le
    O\!\left( \sqrt{\frac{d}{r}\log\frac{r}{d}} +
    \sqrt{\frac{1}{r}\log\frac{1}{\delta}} \right).
  \]
  This is the uniform bound of Haussler and Welzl \cite{hausslerwelzl1987}.
\end{theorem}

The lemma is the bridge to the structures of the next section: an
$\varepsilon$-approximation is a sample in which every range deviates by at
most $\varepsilon$, and an $\varepsilon$-net is a sample intersecting every
range of measure at least $\varepsilon$. Both have size independent of $n$ for
constant $d$, which is why they reduce large geometric problems to small random
ones \cite{harpeled2011}.

\textbf{Derandomization}\index{derandomization} recovers the same guarantees
deterministically. The Chazelle--Friedman method \cite{chazellefriedman1990}
replaces the random sample by a \emph{deterministic} sample built so that no
range of a canonical family contains an extreme number of points, using a
discrepancy bound; the derandomized $\varepsilon$-nets and the optimal
deterministic convex-hull algorithm of Chazelle \cite{chazelle1993} rest on
this principle. The price is complexity and constants, which is why randomized
sampling remains the default in implementations.

\begin{remark}
  The discrepancy viewpoint is developed by Matou\v{s}ek \cite{matousek1999};
  the half-plane discrepancy of the sampling chapter
  (Definition~\ref{def:halfplane_discrepancy}) is exactly the deviation
  controlled by the sampling lemma.
\end{remark}

\section{\texorpdfstring{$\varepsilon$}{epsilon}-Nets}
\label{sec:randomized_eps_nets}

\subsection{1. Overview}

An \textbf{$\varepsilon$-net}\index{epsilon-net} is a small subset of the input
that hits every large range. If a problem is solved correctly whenever the
solution set is an $\varepsilon$-net of the input, then a tiny random sample
suffices, independent of $n$. This is the engine of many approximation
algorithms in geometric data structures (Chapter~\ref{ch:range_searching}) and
was introduced by Haussler and Welzl \cite{hausslerwelzl1987} to support range
searching.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[$\varepsilon$-Net]\label{def:rand_eps_net}
  Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{R})$ be a range space and $P \subseteq
  \mathcal{X}$ finite. A subset $N \subseteq P$ is an \textbf{$\varepsilon$-net}
  of $P$ if every range $R \in \mathcal{R}$ with $|R \cap P| \ge \varepsilon
  |P|$ contains a point of $N$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\varepsilon$-Approximation]\label{def:rand_eps_approximation}
  A subset $A \subseteq P$ is an \textbf{$\varepsilon$-approximation} of $P$ if
  for every range $R \in \mathcal{R}$,
  \[
    \left| \frac{|R \cap A|}{|A|} - \frac{|R \cap P|}{|P|} \right| \le
    \varepsilon.
  \]
  An $\varepsilon$-approximation is in particular an $\varepsilon$-net.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Small Nets by Random Sampling]\label{thm:rand_eps_net_small}
  Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{R})$ be a range space of VC dimension $d$. A
  uniform random sample $N$ of size
  \[
    r = O\!\left( \frac{d}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{d}{\varepsilon} +
    \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta} \right)
  \]
  is an $\varepsilon$-net of $P$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$; a
  sample of size $O(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac{1}{\delta})$ is an
  $\varepsilon$-approximation with the same probability
  \cite{hausslerwelzl1987}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  Fix a range $R$ with $|R \cap P| \ge \varepsilon |P|$. The sample misses $R$
  with probability $(1 - \varepsilon)^r \le e^{-\varepsilon r}$. The packing
  argument of Haussler and Welzl bounds the number of ranges relevant at this
  scale by $O(r^d)$, so the union bound gives failure probability
  $r^d e^{-\varepsilon r} \le \delta$ for the stated size. The approximation
  claim follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:rand_sampling_lemma} and a similar union
  bound.
\end{proof}

For half-plane ranges ($d = 3$) the bound is $O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})$, independent of $n$; the deterministic construction
of Matou\v{s}ek \cite{matousek1990} achieves the same size in time
$O(n \operatorname{poly}(1/\varepsilon))$.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Randomized $\varepsilon$-Net Construction}
\label{alg:rand_eps_net_sampling}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require point set $P$ in the plane, parameter $\varepsilon$, failure probability $\delta$.
\Ensure An $\varepsilon$-net of $P$ (half-plane ranges) with probability at least $1 - \delta$.
\Function{EpsilonNet}{$P, \varepsilon, \delta$}
  \State $r \gets \big\lceil \tfrac{3}{\varepsilon}\log\tfrac{3}{\varepsilon} + \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\tfrac{1}{\delta} \big\rceil$
  \State $N \gets$ uniform random sample of $P$ of size $r$
  \If{some half-plane contains $> \varepsilon |P|$ points and none of $N$}
    \State resample, or fall back to the construction of \cite{matousek1990}
  \EndIf
  \State \Return $N$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\varepsilon$}: the sample size is governed by the
  $O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})$ term; $\varepsilon$
  trades accuracy against sample size.
\item \textbf{Failure probability $\delta$}: the
  $\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}$ term grows logarithmically, so
  very small failure probabilities are cheap.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Size}: $O(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{d}{\varepsilon})$,
  independent of $n$.
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n + r \log r)$ randomized; $O(n
  \operatorname{poly}(1/\varepsilon))$ deterministic \cite{matousek1990}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item Approximate simplex range counting (Section~\ref{sec:rangesearch_simplex})
  and range searching (Chapter~\ref{ch:range_searching}).
\item The coresets of Har-Peled and Mazumdar \cite{harpeled2004} are
  $\varepsilon$-approximations under geometric distances.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Brute-force verification}: on small inputs enumerate all ranges
  and assert the net property holds for every one.
\item \textbf{Small $\varepsilon$}: verify the $O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log
  \frac{1}{\varepsilon})$ growth of the net size empirically.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Haussler, D., Welzl, E. \cite{hausslerwelzl1987}, the original theorem.
\item Matou\v{s}ek, J. \cite{matousek1990}, deterministic construction.
\item Matou\v{s}ek, J. \cite{matousek1999} and Har-Peled, S. \cite{harpeled2011}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Applications to Convex Hulls}
\label{sec:randomized_convex_hulls}

\subsection{1. Overview}

The randomized incremental convex hull inserts the points in a random order and
maintains the hull of the inserted prefix, using the conflict graph to find,
for every point still outside, the hull edge it lies in front of. Backward
analysis shows each hull edge is created with probability $O(2/i)$ at step $i$,
giving expected $O(n \log n)$ total time, matching the optimal deterministic
bound of Chan's algorithm (Theorem~\ref{thm:chans_complexity}) and the methods
of Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_hulls} (Section~\ref{sec:quickhull}).

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Hull Conflict]\label{def:rand_hull_conflict}
  Let $\operatorname{conv}(Q)$ be the convex hull of the inserted points $Q$.
  An uninserted point $p$ \textbf{conflicts} with a hull edge $e$ if $p$ lies
  in the open half-plane on the outside of $e$; the conflict zone of $p$ is the
  contiguous chain of hull edges visible from $p$. Points inside the hull have
  an empty conflict zone.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Randomized Incremental Hull]\label{thm:rand_hull_expected}
  Inserting the points of a planar $n$-point set in a uniformly random order,
  maintaining the hull and its conflict graph, computes the convex hull in
  expected $O(n \log n)$ time and $O(n)$ space.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  The total work is the number of hull edges ever created. A final hull edge is
  determined by its two endpoints, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:rand_backward_analysis}
  with $d = 2$ it is created at step $i$ with probability $O(2/i)$. The final
  hull has at most $n$ edges, giving expected creations $\sum_i O(2/i) \cdot
  O(i) = O(n \log n)$; locating each inserted point through the conflict list
  costs $O(1)$ amortized.
\end{proof}

In $\mathbb{R}^3$ the same argument gives expected $O(n \log n)$; the
Clarkson--Shor analysis \cite{clarkson1989} yields the same bound with optimal
constants for random inputs.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Randomized Incremental Convex Hull}
\label{alg:rand_convex_hull}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require point set $P$ in the plane.
\Ensure the convex hull of $P$.
\Function{RandomizedHull}{$P$}
  \State $\sigma \gets$ uniformly random permutation of $P$
  \State $H \gets$ convex hull of the first two points of $\sigma$
  \State initialize the conflict graph of the remaining points
  \For{$i = 3, \dots, n$}
    \State $p \gets \sigma[i]$
    \If{$p$'s conflict zone is empty} \State continue \Comment{$p$ is inside $H$}
    \Else
      \State remove the visible chain of $H$; add the two tangent edges from $p$
      \State repartition the conflicts of the removed edges onto the two new edges
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $H$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Initialization}: start from the first two points as a degenerate
  ``hull''; collinear prefixes are handled by the tangency routine.
\item \textbf{Tangents and predicates}: walk the hull from the visible chain to
  find the two tangents; use the robust orientation predicate of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_predicates}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: expected $O(n \log n)$; worst case $O(n^2)$ for
  adversarial deterministic orders.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item A testing oracle for the deterministic hulls of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_hulls} (Sections~\ref{sec:graham_scan}--\ref{sec:quickhull}).
\item Extends to $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the same conflict-graph machinery; linked
  to LP by point-line duality (Section~\ref{sec:randomized_linear_programming}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cross-validation}: compare with the monotone chain and Graham
  scan outputs for many permutations.
\item \textbf{Degenerate inputs}: drop duplicates, tolerate collinear boundary
  points, and assign points on a hull edge a consistent conflict zone.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Clarkson, K.~L., Shor, P.~W. \cite{clarkson1989}.
\item Seidel, R. \cite{seidel1990}, convex hulls made easy.
\item Chazelle, B. \cite{chazelle1993}, the optimal deterministic hull.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Sec.~11.2.
\end{itemize}

\section{Applications to Delaunay Triangulations}
\label{sec:randomized_delaunay}

\subsection{1. Overview}

The Delaunay triangulation is the flagship example of randomized incremental
construction: inserting the points in a random order and repairing the
triangulation by edge flips yields the expected $O(n \log n)$ construction of
Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_randomized} (Theorem~\ref{thm:randomized_delaunay}).
This section re-derives that bound from the backward-analysis and
conflict-graph machinery of this chapter, and discusses higher-dimensional
behavior.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Delaunay Conflict]\label{def:rand_delaunay_conflict}
  In the Delaunay setting the conflict relation of
  Definition~\ref{def:rand_conflict} is the in-circle test
  (Definition~\ref{def:incircle}): an uninserted point $p$ conflicts with a
  triangle $T$ if $p$ lies strictly inside the circumcircle of $T$. The
  conflict zone of $p$ is exactly the cavity of
  Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_incremental} (Definition~\ref{def:conflict_graph}).
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Expected Delaunay Cost]\label{thm:rand_delaunay_expected}
  The randomized incremental construction of the Delaunay triangulation of an
  $n$-point set in the plane runs in expected $O(n \log n)$ time and expected
  $O(n)$ space; the expected number of triangles ever created is $O(n)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  A final triangle is determined by its three vertices, so by
  Theorem~\ref{thm:rand_backward_analysis} it is created at step $i$ with
  probability $O(3/i)$ and the expected number of created triangles is $O(n)$
  (Example~\ref{ex:rand_backward_delaunay}). The expected conflict-graph size is
  $O(n)$ by the same backward argument, and point location through the history
  DAG (Section~\ref{sec:pointloc_search_dags}) costs expected $O(\log n)$ per
  insertion, giving the claimed time.
\end{proof}

In $\mathbb{R}^d$ the Delaunay complex has worst-case size
$\Theta(n^{\lceil d/2 \rceil})$, and the randomized incremental construction
runs in expected $O(n^{\lceil d/2 \rceil})$ time
(Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_higher_dimensional}); the planar case is the only
one with a clean $O(n \log n)$ bound.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Randomized Incremental Delaunay (Walk Variant)}
\label{alg:rand_delaunay_insert}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require point set $P$, an enclosing ``big triangle''.
\Ensure the Delaunay triangulation of $P$.
\Function{RandomizedDelaunayWalk}{$P$}
  \State $\sigma \gets$ uniformly random permutation of $P$
  \State $\mathcal{T} \gets$ big triangle
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State $p \gets \sigma[i]$
    \State $T \gets$ triangle of $\mathcal{T}$ containing $p$, found by the visibility walk of Section~\ref{sec:pointloc_triangulations}
    \State split $T$ into three triangles; flip illegal edges of the neighborhood (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_randomized})
  \EndFor
  \State remove triangles incident to the big triangle
  \State \Return $\mathcal{T}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This is the version used in practice; the history-DAG version of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:randomized_delaunay} (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_randomized})
replaces the walk by a guaranteed $O(\log n)$ search.

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Walk vs.\ DAG}: the visibility walk
  (Section~\ref{sec:pointloc_triangulations}) is simpler and faster in practice
  but has no worst-case guarantee; use the DAG when a provable bound is needed.
\item \textbf{Flip strategy}: the legalization pass of
  Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping} repairs only the insertion
  neighborhood, keeping the per-insertion cost proportional to the created
  triangles.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: expected $O(n \log n)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:rand_delaunay_expected});
  expected $O(n^{\lceil d/2 \rceil})$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$; worst case $O(n^2)$
  without a DAG.
\item \textbf{Space}: expected $O(n)$ (planar).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item The engine of Delaunay meshing (Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}).
\item A reference oracle for deterministic Delaunay algorithms
  (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_randomized}); the dual Voronoi diagram is obtained
  for free (Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Permutation tests}: the output must agree with a divide-and-conquer
  construction for every permutation.
\item \textbf{Cocircularity and duplicates}: break cocircular ties consistently
  (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_randomized}) and skip coincident points with the
  exact predicate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Guibas, L., Knuth, D.~E., Sharir, M. \cite{guibas1992}.
\item Clarkson, K.~L. \cite{clarkson1993}, randomized closest-point and Voronoi
  constructions.
\item Mulmuley, K. \cite{mulmuley1990}; de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008},
  Sec.~9.4.
\end{itemize}

\section{Applications to Linear Programming}
\label{sec:randomized_linear_programming}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Seidel's randomized algorithm \cite{seidel1990} solves a linear program in
$\mathbb{R}^d$ with $n$ constraints in expected $O(d!\,n)$ time, hence expected
linear time in any fixed dimension. Its principle is the boundary reduction of
Section~\ref{sec:low_dimensional_lp}: when a random constraint is violated by
the current optimum, the new optimum must lie on the boundary of that
constraint, reducing the dimension by one. The analysis is a canonical
backward-analysis argument.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Linear Program]\label{def:rand_lp}
  A \textbf{linear program} in $\mathbb{R}^d$ maximizes $c^{\mathsf T} x$
  subject to a set $H$ of $n$ linear constraints $a_j^{\mathsf T} x \le b_j$,
  each a half-space (Section~\ref{sec:geometric_linear_programs} of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}). The \textbf{optimum} is the feasible
  point maximizing the objective.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Seidel's Algorithm]\label{thm:rand_lp_expected}
  For a linear program in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $n$ constraints in general
  position and a bounded feasible region, Seidel's randomized algorithm
  computes the optimum in expected $O(d!\,n)$ time; in particular, expected
  $O(n)$ time for $d = 2$ and every fixed $d$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  Let $E(n,d)$ be the expected time. The algorithm processes the constraints in
  random order, maintaining the optimum $v$ of the prefix. With probability at
  most $d/(n-d+1)$ the next constraint violates $v$ (by backward analysis: the
  optimum of the remaining constraints is determined by at most $d$ of them),
  giving the recurrence
  \[
    E(n,d) \le E(n-1,d) + O(1) + \frac{d}{n}\,E(n-1,d-1),
  \]
  whose solution is $E(n,d) = O(d!\,n)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Seidel's Randomized Linear Programming}
\label{alg:rand_lp_seidel}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require constraints $H$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$, objective $c$.
\Ensure The optimum $v$, or a certificate of infeasibility/unboundedness.
\Function{SeidelLP}{$H, d$}
  \If{$d = 1$}
    \State \Return the extreme feasible point (a one-dimensional scan)
  \EndIf
  \State $v \gets$ a vertex of the feasible region of a constant-size subset of $H$
  \State $\sigma \gets$ uniformly random permutation of $H$
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, |H|$}
    \State $h \gets \sigma[i]$
    \If{$v$ violates $h$}
      \State \Comment{the optimum lies on the hyperplane of $h$}
      \State $v \gets$ SeidelLP($\{ h' \cap \partial h : h' \in H \}$, $d - 1$)
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $v$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Random order}: draw one permutation per recursion level; reusing
  it preserves the analysis.
\item \textbf{Base case and predicates}: $d = 1$ is a sorted scan with
  infeasibility certificates; feasibility tests use the orientation predicates
  of Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_predicates}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: expected $O(d!\,n)$, i.e., $O(n)$ for fixed $d$; worst
  case $O(nd)$.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item Welzl's smallest-enclosing-circle algorithm \cite{welzl1991} is the
  LP-type specialization of the recursion, in expected $O(n)$ time.
\item Support queries, largest empty circle, and collision separation in
  $\mathbb{R}^2$, $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Section~\ref{sec:low_dimensional_lp}); the
  implementation of choice up to dimension three, where Megiddo's
  \cite{megiddo1983} has no simpler competitor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cross-validation}: compare the randomized optimum with
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:halfplane_intersection} (Chapter~\ref{ch:linear_programming}).
\item \textbf{Infeasible, unbounded, and parallel constraints}: detect an empty
  intersection at dimension 1 and handle parallel half-planes in the base case.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Seidel, R. \cite{seidel1990}, the randomized LP and hull algorithm.
\item Welzl, E. \cite{welzl1991}, smallest enclosing disks.
\item Megiddo, N. \cite{megiddo1983}, deterministic linear-time LP in
  $\mathbb{R}^3$.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Sec.~4.4.
\end{itemize}
