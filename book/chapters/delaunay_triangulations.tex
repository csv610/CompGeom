\section{Voronoi--Delaunay Duality}
\label{sec:delaunay_duality}

\subsection{1. Overview}

The \index{Delaunay triangulation}Delaunay triangulation of a point set $P$ is the
straight-line dual of the Voronoi diagram (Section~\ref{sec:voronoi_segments}):
two sites $p,q \in P$ are connected by a Delaunay edge exactly when their
Voronoi cells share a boundary edge. The dual of a Voronoi diagram of points in
general position is a planar straight-line graph whose bounded faces are
triangles, so the dual is a triangulation of the convex hull of $P$. The two
diagrams form a planar dual pair: Voronoi vertices (circumcenters) correspond to
Delaunay triangles, Voronoi edges to Delaunay edges, and Voronoi faces to
Delaunay vertices~\cite{deberg2008,okabe2000}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Voronoi Cell]\label{def:voronoi_cell_delaunay}
For a site $p \in P$, the Voronoi cell $V(p)$ is the set of points of the plane
at least as close to $p$ as to any other site:
\[
V(p) = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \|x - p\| \le \|x - q\| \text{ for all } q \in P \}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Straight-Line Dual]\label{def:delaunay_dual}
The \emph{Delaunay triangulation} $DT(P)$ is the graph with vertex set $P$ and an
edge $pq$ for every pair of sites whose Voronoi cells $V(p)$ and $V(q)$ share a
common edge of positive length.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[General Position]\label{def:general_position_delaunay}
A planar point set is in \emph{general position} if no four points are
cocircular and no three are collinear. Under this assumption every Voronoi
vertex has degree three and the dual graph is a genuine triangulation.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Duality]\label{thm:delaunay_duality}
Let $P$ be a set of $n$ points in general position in the plane. The
straight-line dual of the Voronoi diagram $VD(P)$ is the Delaunay triangulation
$DT(P)$, with the following correspondences:
\begin{itemize}
\item each Voronoi vertex, the circumcenter of three sites $p,q,r$, corresponds
  to the Delaunay triangle $pqr$, whose circumcircle passes through the three
  sites;
\item each Voronoi edge lies on the perpendicular bisector of its dual Delaunay
  edge;
\item each Voronoi face $V(p)$ corresponds to the Delaunay vertex $p$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A point of the plane equidistant from exactly three sites $p,q,r$ is their
circumcenter; it is the unique point where $V(p)$, $V(q)$, and $V(r)$ meet, so
it is a Voronoi vertex and its dual is the triangle $pqr$. A point equidistant
from exactly two sites $p,q$ lies on their perpendicular bisector, so the shared
boundary of $V(p)$ and $V(q)$ is a straight segment and the dual edge $pq$ is a
straight segment. Distinct such segments meet only at Voronoi vertices, which
are endpoints of the corresponding dual edges, so the straight-line embedding
is planar.
\end{proof}

The planar duality yields exact size bounds.

\begin{corollary}[Size]\label{cor:delaunay_size}
If $h$ points of $P$ lie on the boundary of the convex hull of $P$, then $DT(P)$
has exactly $2n - 2 - h$ triangles and $3n - 3 - h$ edges.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The planar graph $DT(P)$ has $n$ vertices, $e$ edges, and $t + 1$ faces where
$t$ is the number of triangular faces. Euler's formula gives
$n - e + (t + 1) = 2$. The unbounded face has $h$ boundary edges and each
triangle contributes three edge-sides, so $3t + h = 2e$. Eliminating $e$ gives
$t = 2n - 2 - h$ and $e = 3n - 3 - h$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Voronoi Cells from a Delaunay Triangulation}
\label{alg:delaunay_to_voronoi}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A Delaunay triangulation $DT(P)$.
\Ensure The Voronoi diagram $VD(P)$.
\Function{VoronoiFromDelaunay}{$DT(P)$}
  \For{each triangle $T = (p,q,r)$}
    \State $c_T \gets \text{circumcenter}(p,q,r)$
  \EndFor
  \For{each Delaunay edge $(p,q)$}
    \State emit the segment through the circumcenters of the one or two
           triangles sharing $(p,q)$, clipped to $V(p) \cap V(q)$
  \EndFor
  \State \Return the arrangement of circumcenters and connecting segments
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{General position}: real data is rarely in general position;
  algorithms either break cocircular ties symbolically (simulation of
  simplicity) or deterministically (e.g., by a lexicographic rule on the
  point order).
\item \textbf{Representation}: a half-edge (DCEL) structure stores both
  diagrams at once; each Delaunay edge keeps a pointer to its dual Voronoi edge
  and vice versa.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ to construct $DT(P)$ by any of the algorithms
  of Sections~\ref{sec:delaunay_randomized}--\ref{sec:delaunay_divide_conquer};
  the dual $VD(P)$ costs $O(n)$ more.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$ for either diagram; both together remain $O(n)$
  because both are planar graphs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Meshing}: Delaunay refinement (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement})
  exploits the empty-circumcircle property inherited from the duality.
\item \textbf{Nearest-site queries}: point location in $VD(P)$ answers
  ``closest site'' queries in $O(\log n)$.
\item \textbf{Surface reconstruction}: the alpha complex of the Delaunay
  complex (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_higher_dimensional}) reconstructs surfaces
  from point clouds.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cocircular points}: four cocircular sites give a dual
  quadrilateral; verify that the two triangulations obtained by the two
  diagonals are both accepted and reported consistently.
\item \textbf{Collinear sites}: three collinear sites create a degenerate
  (zero-area) Voronoi vertex; the dual must not emit zero-area triangles.
\item \textbf{Duplicate sites}: coincident sites have an empty Voronoi cell;
  the dual must ignore the duplicate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Empty-Circumcircle Property}
\label{sec:delaunay_empty_circumcircle}

\subsection{1. Overview}

The defining property of a Delaunay triangulation is that no vertex of the point
set lies inside the circumcircle of any triangle. This \index{empty circumcircle}
\emph{empty-circumcircle property} is the local certificate that makes the
Delaunay triangulation easy to recognize, to verify, and to maintain under
insertions and flips. It is equivalent to the Voronoi duality of
Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_duality}: a triangle whose circumcircle is empty has a
circumcenter that is a Voronoi vertex.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Empty Circumcircle]\label{def:empty_circumcircle}
A triangle $T$ of a triangulation of $P$ satisfies the \emph{empty-circumcircle
property} if the open disk bounded by the circumcircle of $T$ contains no point
of $P$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[In-Circle Test]\label{def:incircle}
Let $a,b,c,d$ be points with $a,b,c$ oriented counterclockwise. The
\emph{in-circle predicate} returns true if $d$ lies strictly inside the circle
through $a,b,c$:
\[
\operatorname{InCircle}(a,b,c,d) =
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
a_x & a_y & a_x^2 + a_y^2 & 1\\
b_x & b_y & b_x^2 + b_y^2 & 1\\
c_x & c_y & c_x^2 + c_y^2 & 1\\
d_x & d_y & d_x^2 + d_y^2 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
> 0.
\]
The determinant is zero exactly when the four points are cocircular.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Delaunay Triangulation]\label{def:delaunay_triangulation}
A triangulation of $P$ is a \emph{Delaunay triangulation} if every triangle
satisfies the empty-circumcircle property.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Empty Circle Characterizes Delaunay]\label{thm:empty_circle}
A triangulation of a point set $P$ in general position is the Delaunay
triangulation if and only if every triangle has an empty circumcircle.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
($\Leftarrow$) If every triangle of a triangulation $\mathcal{T}$ has an empty
circumcircle, then each triangle's circumcenter is a Voronoi vertex of
$VD(P)$: the open disk is empty, so the circumcenter is as close to the three
triangle vertices as to any other site. The triangulation $\mathcal{T}$ is then
the straight-line dual of $VD(P)$, hence the Delaunay triangulation.
($\Rightarrow$) If $\mathcal{T}$ is the Delaunay triangulation, its triangles
are dual to Voronoi vertices, whose circumcircles contain no site in their
interior.
\end{proof}

The in-circle determinant is closely tied to the orientation test. Writing the
four points as lifted points on the paraboloid $z = x^2 + y^2$, the
determinant is (up to sign) the oriented volume of the tetrahedron
$a^+, b^+, c^+, d^+$, which explains both the sign convention and the numerical
behavior (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_lifting}).

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Adaptive In-Circle Predicate}
\label{alg:incircle}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require points $a,b,c$ oriented counterclockwise and query point $d$.
\Ensure true if $d$ is inside the circle through $a,b,c$.
\Function{InCircle}{$a,b,c,d$}
  \State $\Delta \gets $ determinant of the $4 \times 4$ matrix of
         Definition~\ref{def:incircle}, computed by floating-point arithmetic
  \If{$|\Delta|$ safely larger than the error bound}
    \State \Return $\Delta > 0$
  \Else
    \State recompute $\Delta$ exactly using arbitrary-precision arithmetic
    \State \Return $\Delta > 0$
  \EndIf
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Adaptive evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement} uses the same
technique) runs at full floating-point speed in the common case and only falls
back to exact arithmetic near degenerate configurations~\cite{shewchuk1997}.

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Filtering}: cheap floating-point filters with rigorous error
  bounds reject most inputs before exact arithmetic is invoked
  (Section~\ref{sec:robust_predicates}).
\item \textbf{Exact construction}: when circumcenters themselves are inserted
  (Delaunay refinement), exact predicates still help decide legality even
  though the new points are irrational.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: the predicate is $O(1)$; with adaptive filtering the
  expected cost is a small constant number of floating-point operations.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(1)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Legality tests}: the flip criterion of
  Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping} calls \text{InCircle} once per edge.
\item \textbf{Verification}: checking that a mesh produced by any method is
  Delaunay reduces to one predicate per interior edge.
\item \textbf{Voronoi computation}: empty circles certify Voronoi vertices.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cocircular queries}: verify that the predicate returns exactly
  zero and that callers treat the result as ``legally Delaunay'' (either
  diagonal is acceptable).
\item \textbf{Counterclockwise orientation}: the sign convention assumes
  $a,b,c$ counterclockwise; test both orientations.
\item \textbf{Large coordinates}: exact fallback must be exercised on
  coordinates far from the origin, where floating-point filters are
  inconclusive.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Guibas, L., Stolfi, J. \cite{guibas1985}.
\item Shewchuk, J.~R. \cite{shewchuk1997}, adaptive robust predicates.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Ch.~9.
\end{itemize}

\section{Local Delaunay Criterion}
\label{sec:delaunay_local_criterion}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Checking every triangle of a triangulation against all other points would be
expensive. The \index{local Delaunay criterion}\emph{local Delaunay criterion}
reduces the global empty-circle condition to a test on each interior edge: an
interior edge is locally Delaunay when the vertex of one incident triangle does
not lie inside the circumcircle of the other. A triangulation in which every
interior edge is locally Delaunay is globally Delaunay~\cite{deberg2008}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Locally Delaunay Edge]\label{def:locally_delaunay}
Let $e = (u,v)$ be an interior edge shared by triangles $(u,v,w)$ and $(u,v,z)$.
The edge $e$ is \emph{locally Delaunay} if $z$ does not lie strictly inside the
circumcircle of $(u,v,w)$; by symmetry this is equivalent to $w$ not lying
inside the circumcircle of $(u,v,z)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Legal and Illegal Edges]\label{def:delaunay_illegal_edge}
An interior edge that is not locally Delaunay is \emph{illegal}; the other
diagonal of the quadrilateral $(u,w,v,z)$ is the unique candidate replacement
that is legal in the Delaunay sense.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Local Implies Global]\label{thm:local_global}
A triangulation of a point set in general position is the Delaunay triangulation
if and only if every interior edge is locally Delaunay.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The forward direction is immediate from the empty-circumcircle property.
For the converse, suppose every interior edge is locally Delaunay and let $T$ be
any triangle. If $T$'s circumcircle contained a point $p$ of $P$, consider the
segment from $p$ to a vertex of $T$; walking across the triangulation from the
face containing $p$ to $T$ crosses an interior edge whose flip strictly
increases the minimum angle of the incident quadrilateral, contradicting the
assumption that all edges are locally Delaunay (the standard ``no edge can be
illegal'' exchange argument of Lawson~\cite{lawson1977}).
\end{proof}

The theorem is the basis of all flip-based algorithms: it suffices to fix
illegal edges one at a time.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Local Delaunay Test}
\label{alg:local_delaunay}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{IsLocallyDelaunay}{$u, v, w, z$}
  \If{$z$ lies strictly inside the circle through $u, v, w$}
    \State \Return \textsc{False} \Comment{$e=(u,v)$ is illegal}
  \EndIf
  \State \Return \textsc{True}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Boundary edges}: edges on the convex hull have no second triangle
  and are always treated as locally Delaunay.
\item \textbf{Cocircularity}: when the four vertices of a quadrilateral are
  cocircular, both diagonals are locally Delaunay; pick either consistently.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(1)$ per edge using the in-circle predicate.
\item \textbf{Overall}: a full verification pass over a triangulation costs
  $O(n)$ predicate calls.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Edge flipping} (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping}) flips
  precisely the edges that fail this test.
\item \textbf{Incremental insertion} re-checks only the edges of the affected
  neighborhood after each insertion.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Non-convex quadrilaterals}: after a flip the new edge may not be
  valid for non-convex quadrilaterals; verify the quadrilateral is convex before
  flipping.
\item \textbf{Tie breaking}: cocircular quadrilaterals must be reported as legal
  to guarantee termination.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Lawson, C.~L. \cite{lawson1977}.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Sec.~9.3.
\end{itemize}

\section{Edge Flipping}
\label{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Given any triangulation of a point set, the Delaunay triangulation can be
reached by repeatedly replacing illegal edges with their other diagonal.
\index{edge flip}\emph{Edge flipping} is both an algorithm in its own right
(Lawson's algorithm) and the local repair primitive inside incremental and
divide-and-conquer constructions.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Flip]\label{def:flip}
A \emph{flip} replaces an interior edge $(u,v)$ shared by triangles $(u,v,w)$
and $(u,v,z)$ with the other diagonal $(w,z)$, provided the quadrilateral
$(u,w,v,z)$ is convex.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Lawson's Flip Algorithm]\label{thm:flip_convergence}
Starting from an arbitrary triangulation of a point set in general position,
repeatedly flipping illegal edges terminates with the Delaunay triangulation.
The number of flips is at most $O(n^2)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Each flip of an illegal edge strictly increases the lexicographically sorted
list of angles of the triangulation (the minimum angle never decreases, and
strictly increases at every flip). Since there are finitely many triangulations
of a fixed point set and the angle list is a monotone quantity that is bounded
above, the process terminates. Termination can only occur when no illegal edge
remains, which by Theorem~\ref{thm:local_global} is the Delaunay
triangulation. The $O(n^2)$ bound follows because the Delaunay triangulation
has $O(n)$ edges and a ``history'' argument bounds the total number of
successful flips by the number of edges ever created.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Lawson's Flip Algorithm}
\label{alg:flip_to_delaunay}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require a triangulation $\mathcal{T}$ of $P$.
\Ensure the Delaunay triangulation of $P$.
\Function{FlipToDelaunay}{$\mathcal{T}$}
  \State $Q \gets $ all interior edges of $\mathcal{T}$
  \While{$Q$ is not empty}
    \State $e \gets Q.\text{pop}()$
    \If{not IsLocallyDelaunay($e$)}
      \State $e' \gets $ other diagonal of the two triangles sharing $e$
      \State flip $e$ to $e'$
      \State push the (up to four) newly created edges onto $Q$
    \EndIf
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $\mathcal{T}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Queue discipline}: processing edges in stack or queue order both
  terminate; a heap keyed by ``amount of violation'' reaches convergence faster
  in practice.
\item \textbf{Convexity check}: only flip when the quadrilateral is convex;
  non-convex quadrilaterals are kept unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n^2)$ flips in the worst case, each $O(1)$; with a
  priority queue the expected cost on random inputs is near-linear.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$ for the triangulation and the queue.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Post-processing}: any meshing algorithm can be followed by a flip
  pass to enforce the Delaunay property (e.g., before Delaunay refinement,
  Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement}).
\item \textbf{Local repair}: incremental insertion (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_incremental})
  legalizes only the edges of the modified neighborhood.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Termination}: verify the algorithm terminates on adversarially
  ordered inputs, including nearly cocircular configurations.
\item \textbf{Cocircularity}: ties must be resolved without oscillation;
  prefer the lexicographically smaller diagonal.
\item \textbf{Collinear input}: a triangulation of collinear points has no
  interior edges; the algorithm must do nothing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Lawson, C.~L. \cite{lawson1977}.
\item Guibas, L., Stolfi, J. \cite{guibas1985}.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Sec.~9.3.
\end{itemize}

\section{Incremental Construction}
\label{sec:delaunay_incremental}

\subsection{1. Overview}

\index{incremental construction}Incremental construction builds the Delaunay
triangulation by inserting the points of $P$ one at a time, maintaining the
Delaunay property after every insertion. Each step (i) locates the triangle
containing the new point, (ii) splits it into three triangles, and (iii)
legalizes the edges of the affected neighborhood by flipping. Two families of
algorithms realize this scheme: the flip-based incremental algorithm of
Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping} and the cavity-based
Bowyer--Watson algorithm~\cite{bowyer1981,watson1981}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Conflict]\label{def:conflict}
A new point $p$ \emph{conflicts} with a Delaunay triangle $T$ if $p$ lies
inside the circumcircle of $T$. The set of conflicting triangles is the
\emph{conflict zone} of $p$; it forms a connected set whose union is a simple
polygon, the \emph{cavity} of $p$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Cavity Repair]\label{thm:cavity}
Let $p$ be a point not yet in $DT(P)$. The union of the Delaunay triangles of
$DT(P)$ whose circumcircle contains $p$ is a star-shaped polygon with respect
to $p$, and retriangulating its boundary with the spokes from $p$ to the
boundary vertices yields the Delaunay triangulation of $P \cup \{p\}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Every Delaunay triangle has an empty circumcircle before insertion. The
conflict zone is connected: two conflicting triangles share an edge only if the
cavity boundary stays simple, and the empty-circle condition guarantees that
the zone is exactly the set of triangles whose circumcenter is hidden from $p$.
The new triangles $p$-to-boundary-edge have empty circumcircles: if one
contained a site $q$, then the circumcircle of the original conflicting
triangle it replaced would contain $q$ as well, contradicting Delaunay-ness.
Thus the repair is Delaunay.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Bowyer--Watson Incremental Insertion}
\label{alg:bowyer_watson}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require point set $P$; a surrounding ``big triangle'' that encloses all of $P$.
\Ensure the Delaunay triangulation of $P$.
\Function{InsertDelaunay}{$P$}
  \State $\mathcal{T} \gets $ single big triangle
  \For{each $p \in P$}
    \State $\text{cavity} \gets \{ T \in \mathcal{T} : p \in \text{circumcircle}(T) \}$
    \State remove cavity triangles from $\mathcal{T}$
    \State $\mathcal{B} \gets $ boundary edges of the cavity
    \For{each edge $(a,b) \in \mathcal{B}$}
      \State add triangle $(a,b,p)$ to $\mathcal{T}$
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \State remove all triangles incident to a vertex of the big triangle
  \State \Return $\mathcal{T}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The flip-based variant replaces the cavity step: split the located triangle
into three, then legalize by flipping outward using
Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping}'s repair pass.

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Point location}: locating the triangle containing $p$ can be done
  with a walk, a jump-and-walk heuristic, or a history DAG; the history DAG
  gives the best worst-case behavior (Section~\ref{sec:point_location_walking}).
\item \textbf{Big triangle}: its vertices must be far enough that no circumcircle
  of the final mesh meets them; remove its incident triangles at the end.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ expected for random insertion orders and
  $O(n^2)$ worst case for adversarial orders without a history structure.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dynamic point sets}: insertions support kinetic meshing and
  adaptive refinement (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement}).
\item \textbf{3D}: Bowyer--Watson generalizes directly to $d$ dimensions
  (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_higher_dimensional}) and is the workhorse of
  TetGen-style tetrahedral meshers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Point on an edge}: locate both incident triangles and merge the
  cavity appropriately.
\item \textbf{Point coincident with an existing vertex}: skip insertion
  (exact predicate) to avoid zero-area triangles.
\item \textbf{Points outside the current hull}: the cavity is still connected;
  verify no spurious edges cross the convex hull.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Bowyer, A. \cite{bowyer1981}.
\item Watson, D.~F. \cite{watson1981}.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Ch.~9.
\end{itemize}

\section{Randomized Incremental Algorithm}
\label{sec:delaunay_randomized}

\subsection{1. Overview}

\index{randomized incremental construction}Randomized incremental construction
inserts the points in a uniformly random permutation. Randomization makes the
expected size of the conflict zone small at every step, so the total expected
cost drops to $O(n \log n)$, matching the divide-and-conquer bound with a much
simpler implementation. This is the algorithm behind the classic randomized
incremental Delaunay/Voronoi construction~\cite{guibas1992}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Conflict Graph]\label{def:conflict_graph}
The \emph{conflict graph} links each inserted point to the triangles of the
current triangulation whose circumcircle contains it. When a new point is
inserted, its conflict set is read from the graph, and the graph is updated for
the new triangles.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Randomized Incremental Delaunay]\label{thm:randomized_delaunay}
Inserting the points of $P$ in a uniformly random order, maintaining the
conflict graph, computes the Delaunay triangulation in $O(n \log n)$ expected
time and $O(n)$ expected space.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch (backward analysis)]
Consider the insertion that creates a particular triangle $T$ of the final
triangulation. Reverse the process: delete points one by one in reverse random
order, and call $T$ a ``created'' triangle at the moment its third vertex is
deleted. Conditioning on $T$ being present at that time, its three vertices are
a uniformly random triple among the $k$ remaining points that lie in
$T \cup \{\text{interior}\}$, so the probability that a specific triangle is
created at step $k$ is $O(3/k)$. Summing over the $O(n)$ triangles of the final
diagram gives expected total conflict-zone work $\sum_k O(3/k \cdot k) = O(n)$
for the conflicts plus $O(n \log n)$ for point location through the history
DAG, yielding the claimed bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Randomized Incremental Delaunay}
\label{alg:randomized_delaunay}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require point set $P$.
\Ensure the Delaunay triangulation of $P$.
\Function{RandomizedDelaunay}{$P$}
  \State $\sigma \gets $ uniformly random permutation of $P$
  \State $\mathcal{T} \gets $ big triangle; $D \gets $ empty history DAG
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State $p \gets \sigma[i]$
    \State $T \gets $ leaf of $D$ whose region contains $p$ (walk down $D$)
    \State cavity $\gets$ conflict zone of $p$ in $\mathcal{T}$
    \State retriangulate the cavity with $p$ (Section~\ref{alg:bowyer_watson})
    \State add the new triangles to $D$ as children of their parent triangle
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathcal{T}$ with big-triangle triangles removed
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Random seed}: a fixed seed makes runs reproducible; permuting
  only once amortizes the cost.
\item \textbf{History DAG}: without it, expected time degrades to $O(n^2)$ for
  point location; the DAG adds $O(n)$ space.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ expected, $O(n^2)$ worst case.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$ expected.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Reference implementation}: the simplicity of the randomized
  version makes it the algorithm of choice for correctness testing of other
  constructions.
\item \textbf{Basis for higher dimensions}: the same backward-analysis argument
  extends to $d$ dimensions (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_higher_dimensional}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Adversarial orders}: compare the output against the
  divide-and-conquer result for the same input with many different
  permutations.
\item \textbf{Duplicate and collinear points}: the conflict graph must not
  record degenerate conflicts; test inputs with repeated coordinates.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}

\item Mulmuley, K. \cite{mulmuley1990}.

\end{itemize}

\section{Divide-and-Conquer Construction}
\label{sec:delaunay_divide_conquer}

\subsection{1. Overview}

\index{divide and conquer}The divide-and-conquer construction of Guibas and
Stolfi sorts the points by $x$-coordinate, triangulates each half recursively,
and merges the two triangulations in linear time. The merge proceeds along a
growing ``baseline'' that is the unique edge connecting the two halves and
respects the empty-circumcircle criterion~\cite{guibas1985}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Lower Tangent]\label{def:lower_tangent}
Let $DT(P_L)$ and $DT(P_R)$ be the Delaunay triangulations of the left and
right halves. The \emph{lower tangent} $(b_l, b_r)$ is the unique edge from a
vertex $b_l$ of the left hull to a vertex $b_r$ of the right hull that is an
edge of the convex hull of $P_L \cup P_R$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Delaunay Merge]\label{thm:delaunay_merge}
Given the Delaunay triangulations of $P_L$ and $P_R$ (with $P_L$ entirely left
of $P_R$), the Delaunay triangulation of $P_L \cup P_R$ is obtained by
repeatedly crossing edges between the two halves, always keeping the crossed
edge locally Delaunay, and deleting the edges it destroys. The merge runs in
$O(n)$ time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The Delaunay triangulation of $P_L \cup P_R$ contains the lower tangent, which
is a Delaunay edge (its two endpoints have an empty circle through them).
Starting from the lower tangent, the next edge of the cross-hull is found by
walking the convex hulls of the two halves and testing the in-circle predicate:
a new edge is legal if the triangles on both sides satisfy
Definition~\ref{def:locally_delaunay}. Each candidate edge is considered a
constant number of times as the baseline advances monotonically, so the total
work is proportional to the number of cross edges, $O(n)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Divide-and-Conquer Delaunay (Guibas--Stolfi)}
\label{alg:delaunay_dnc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require point set $P$ sorted by $x$-coordinate.
\Ensure the Delaunay triangulation of $P$.
\Function{DelaunayDnC}{$P$}
  \If{$|P| \le 3$}
    \State \Return trivial triangulation of $P$
  \EndIf
  \State split $P$ into $P_L$ and $P_R$ by the median $x$-coordinate
  \State $\mathcal{T}_L \gets $ DelaunayDnC($P_L$); \quad
         $\mathcal{T}_R \gets $ DelaunayDnC($P_R$)
  \State \Return Merge($\mathcal{T}_L, \mathcal{T}_R$)
\EndFunction
\Function{Merge}{$\mathcal{T}_L, \mathcal{T}_R$}
  \State $(b_l, b_r) \gets $ lower tangent of $\mathcal{T}_L \cup \mathcal{T}_R$
  \Repeat
    \State $c \gets $ the endpoint (in $P_L$ or $P_R$) that, with $(b_l,b_r)$,
           satisfies the empty-circumcircle test
    \State add edge $(b_l,c)$ or $(c,b_r)$ as the next cross edge, crossing if
           it does not intersect existing edges
    \Until{no further legal cross edge exists}
  \State \Return the combined triangulation
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Balanced split}: splitting at the median gives the $O(n \log n)$
  recurrence $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n)$.
\item \textbf{Same-x coordinates}: tie-break by $y$ (or perturb) so the halves
  are cleanly separable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ worst case.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deterministic construction}: unlike the randomized algorithm,
  gives worst-case guarantees without a random source.
\item \textbf{Baseline for benchmarking}: its robust implementation by Guibas
  and Stolfi remains the reference for exactness in practice~\cite{guibas1985}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Merge correctness}: cross-check every cross edge against the
  in-circle predicate; assert no two cross edges cross.
\item \textbf{Vertical splits}: points with equal $x$ must not fragment the
  recursion.
\item \textbf{Small sizes}: the $n \le 3$ base case must handle collinear
  triples without creating zero-area triangles.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Guibas, L., Stolfi, J. \cite{guibas1985}.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Sec.~9.1--9.2.
\end{itemize}

\section{Lifting to a Paraboloid}
\label{sec:delaunay_lifting}

\subsection{1. Overview}

\index{lifting}\emph{Lifting} maps each planar site $p = (x,y)$ to the point
$p^+ = (x, y, x^2 + y^2)$ on the paraboloid $z = x^2 + y^2$. A plane intersects
the paraboloid in a circle projected onto the $xy$-plane, which converts the
in-circle predicate into a half-space (orientation) test in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and
reduces Delaunay construction to a 3D convex-hull problem~\cite{brown1979,deberg2008}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Lifted Point]\label{def:lifted_point}
The \emph{lift} of $p = (x,y)$ is $p^+ = (x, y, x^2 + y^2)$. The lower hull of
the lifted point set is the set of facets visible from below (i.e., with the
plane normal pointing downward in $z$).
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Lifting Theorem]\label{thm:lifting}
Let $P$ be a set of points in general position, and let $P^+$ be their lifts to
the paraboloid $z = x^2 + y^2$. The vertical projection onto the $xy$-plane of
the lower convex hull of $P^+$ is exactly the Delaunay triangulation $DT(P)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A point $q = (x, y, x^2 + y^2)$ lies on the plane $H$ through $a^+, b^+, c^+$
if and only if $(x,y)$ lies on the circle through $a,b,c$; it lies below $H$ if
and only if $(x,y)$ is strictly inside that circle. Therefore the in-circle
predicate of Definition~\ref{def:incircle} is the sign of the oriented volume
of the tetrahedron $a^+b^+c^+d^+$. A triangle of the projection is a facet of
the lower hull exactly when all other lifted points lie above its plane, i.e.,
when the corresponding circumcircle is empty. Hence lower-hull facets and
Delaunay triangles coincide.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Delaunay via Lifting}
\label{alg:delaunay_lifting}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require point set $P$.
\Ensure the Delaunay triangulation of $P$.
\Function{DelaunayByLifting}{$P$}
  \State $P^+ \gets \{ (x, y, x^2 + y^2) : (x,y) \in P \}$
  \State $\mathcal{H} \gets $ 3D convex hull of $P^+$
  \For{each facet of $\mathcal{H}$ whose outward normal has a negative $z$-component}
    \State project the facet vertices back to the $xy$-plane and emit the triangle
  \EndFor
  \State \Return the projected lower hull
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Which hull}: only the \emph{lower} hull matters; computing the
  full hull is wasteful but harmless.
\item \textbf{Exact arithmetic}: lifted coordinates are quadratic, so predicates
  grow in magnitude; adaptive filters (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_empty_circumcircle})
  keep the common case fast.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ for a 3D hull algorithm that is output
  sensitive; note that the lifted set lies on a strictly convex surface, so its
  hull has $O(n)$ facets, not the worst-case $O(n^2)$.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conceptual tool}: the lifting view unifies Delaunay triangulation,
  Voronoi diagrams, and convex hulls (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_convex_hulls}).
\item \textbf{Reduction}: any 3D hull routine (e.g., Qhull) becomes a Delaunay
  mesher, which is precisely how several numerical packages compute
  triangulations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cocircularity}: cocircular points lift to coplanar points;
  verify the hull routine tolerates coplanar facets.
\item \textbf{Scaling}: quadratic lifts amplify coordinate ranges; test with
  coordinates spanning several orders of magnitude.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Brown, K.~Q. \cite{brown1979}.
\item Edelsbrunner, H. \cite{edelsbrunner1987}.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Sec.~11.4.
\end{itemize}

\section{Relationship to Convex Hulls}
\label{sec:delaunay_convex_hulls}

\subsection{1. Overview}

The lifting theorem of Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_lifting} is one of three
equivalent views of the same object. The Delaunay triangulation is (i) the dual
of the Voronoi diagram, (ii) the projection of the lower hull of the lifted
points, and (iii) the projection of the convex hull of points on a sphere via
stereographic projection. This section records the two hull relations used in
practice and their polar counterpart for Voronoi diagrams.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Upper Envelope of Tangent Planes]\label{def:lifting_upper_envelope}
For each site $p$, let $H_p$ be the tangent plane to the paraboloid
$z = x^2 + y^2$ at the lifted point $p^+$. The \emph{upper envelope} of the
planes $\{H_p\}$ is the pointwise maximum of their $z$-values.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Voronoi Diagram as Upper Hull]\label{thm:voronoi_upper_hull}
The vertical projection onto the $xy$-plane of the upper envelope of the
tangent planes $H_p$ is the Voronoi diagram $VD(P)$: a point $x$ of the plane
lies in the projection of $H_p$ exactly when $p$ is its nearest site.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The tangent plane $H_p$ at $p^+$ is $z = 2p_x x + 2p_y y - (p_x^2 + p_y^2)$. The
vertical gap between $H_p$ and the paraboloid at the point above $x$ is
$|x|^2 - (2p \cdot x - |p|^2) = \|x - p\|^2$. Therefore $H_p$ is above $H_q$ at
$x$ if and only if $\|x - p\| \le \|x - q\|$, i.e., if and only if $p$ is the
nearest site. Projecting the upper envelope gives precisely the Voronoi cells.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Delaunay and Farthest Delaunay]\label{thm:farthest_hull}
The lower hull of the lifted points projects to the (nearest) Delaunay
triangulation, while the upper hull projects to the farthest-point Delaunay
triangulation, whose dual is the farthest-point Voronoi diagram.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By symmetry with Theorem~\ref{thm:lifting}, a facet of the \emph{upper} hull
corresponds to a circle that contains all points of $P$ in its closed disk and
passes through three hull vertices; its projection is a farthest Delaunay
triangle. The dual cells are the regions where a given hull vertex is the
\emph{farthest} site, which is the farthest-point Voronoi cell.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

The practical consequence is that a single 3D convex-hull computation yields
all four objects:

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{All Diagrams from One Convex Hull}
\label{alg:hull_diagrams}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{AllDiagrams}{$P$}
  \State $\mathcal{H} \gets $ 3D hull of lifted points $P^+$
  \State lower facets $\to$ project $\to$ $DT(P)$
  \State upper facets $\to$ project $\to$ farthest Delaunay
  \State dualize $DT(P)$ $\to$ $VD(P)$ (Section~\ref{alg:delaunay_to_voronoi})
  \State \Return $(DT(P), VD(P))$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Stereographic alternative}: projecting the points from the
  paraboloid onto a sphere gives a hull computation in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with
  numerically better conditioning than the quadratic lift.
\item \textbf{Output sensitivity}: for convex input the hull is small; exploit
  output-sensitive hull algorithms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ for the 3D hull (the lifted set has $O(n)$
  facets).
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unified implementation}: one hull routine provides Delaunay,
  Voronoi, and farthest-point structures for testing and cross-validation.
\item \textbf{Pedagogy}: the three-way equivalence (Voronoi, Delaunay, hull) is
  the central organizing idea of the field.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Consistency}: verify $DT$, $VD$, and the hull all agree on random
  and degenerate inputs.
\item \textbf{Coplanar facets}: hulls with coplanar facets must be resolved by
  triangulation or symbolic perturbation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Brown, K.~Q. \cite{brown1979}.
\item Edelsbrunner, H. \cite{edelsbrunner1987}.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Sec.~11.4.
\end{itemize}

\section{Constrained Delaunay Triangulation}
\label{sec:delaunay_constrained}

\subsection{1. Overview}

A \index{constrained Delaunay triangulation}\emph{constrained Delaunay
triangulation} (CDT) is a triangulation of a planar straight-line graph (PSLG)
that contains every constraint segment as a union of edges, while remaining as
close as possible to the Delaunay criterion in the triangles that do not cross
a constraint. CDTs are the standard representation for meshing domains with
internal boundaries, such as faults, wells, or material interfaces.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Visibility]\label{def:cdt_visibility}
A segment $uv$ is \emph{visible} from a point $p$ if the open segment $pu$
meets no constraint segment, and likewise for $pv$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Constrained Delaunay Edge]\label{def:cdt_edge}
An edge $e$ of a triangulation of a PSLG is \emph{constrained Delaunay} if it is
a constraint segment, or if there exists a circle through its two endpoints
whose open disk contains no vertex visible to $e$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Constrained Delaunay Triangulation]\label{def:cdt}
A \emph{CDT} of a PSLG is a triangulation containing every constraint segment
in which every edge is constrained Delaunay.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Existence and Uniqueness of the CDT]\label{thm:cdt}
Every planar straight-line graph admits a constrained Delaunay triangulation,
and it is unique: the diagonal of every quadrilateral in the CDT is chosen so
that the constraints are respected and the remaining edges satisfy
Definition~\ref{def:cdt_edge}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Existence follows from the flip viewpoint: take any triangulation that contains
the constraints, then flip any illegal edge that is not a constraint; each flip
reduces a lexicographic angle potential, so termination yields a triangulation
with no illegal unconstrained edges, which is a CDT by the analogue of
Theorem~\ref{thm:local_global} restricted to visible vertices. Uniqueness
follows because the empty-circle test, restricted to visible points, decides
every non-constrained diagonal independently of the rest of the
triangulation~\cite{chew1989,deberg2008}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{CDT by Constraint Recovery}
\label{alg:cdt_recovery}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require a PSLG $G$ (points plus constraint segments).
\Ensure the constrained Delaunay triangulation of $G$.
\Function{ConstrainedDelaunay}{$G$}
  \State $\mathcal{T} \gets $ Delaunay triangulation of the vertices of $G$
  \For{each constraint segment $s$ of $G$}
    \If{$s$ is not a union of edges of $\mathcal{T}$}
      \State locate the polygons crossed by $s$
      \State retriangulate them so that $s$ becomes a sequence of edges
      \State legalize the affected region with flip-based repair, keeping
             every constraint edge fixed
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathcal{T}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Segment insertion order}: inserting constraints one at a time with
  local repair is simple; batch processing via constrained triangulation is
  faster for many segments.
\item \textbf{Conforming variants}: if constraints may be \emph{split} by new
  vertices (conforming Delaunay), Ruppert-style refinement
  (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement}) can be used to enforce both the
  constraints and the quality bounds.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n^2)$ worst case for flip-based constraint recovery;
  expected $O(n \log n)$ for random inputs.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Finite-element meshing}: constraints model material interfaces,
  wells, and boundaries (see the reservoir and mesh applications in
  Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_mesh_applications}).
\item \textbf{Terrain modeling}: breaklines and rivers are constraints in the
  TIN surfaces of GIS (Section~\ref{sec:voronoi_applications}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Intersecting constraints}: the input PSLG must be planar; verify
  that segments only meet at endpoints, or insert intersection points first.
\item \textbf{Overlapping constraints}: deduplicate coincident segments.
\item \textbf{Constraints along the hull}: ensure hull edges remain part of the
  triangulation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Chew, L.~P. \cite{chew1989}.
\item Shewchuk, J.~R. \cite{shewchuk1998}.
\item de Berg, M., et al. \cite{deberg2008}, Sec.~9.5.
\end{itemize}

\section{Quality Properties}
\label{sec:delaunay_quality}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Among all triangulations of a point set, the Delaunay triangulation is in a
precise sense the ``best''. It maximizes the minimum angle, which is exactly
the criterion that guarantees well-shaped triangles for finite-element
computation: skinny triangles produce ill-conditioned stiffness matrices and
large interpolation errors.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Minimum Angle]\label{def:min_angle}
The \emph{minimum angle} of a triangulation is the smallest angle over all of
its triangles. A triangulation $T_1$ is \emph{angle-optimal} if no other
triangulation of the same point set has a larger minimum angle.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Aspect Ratio]\label{def:aspect_ratio}
The \emph{aspect ratio} (or radius ratio) of a triangle is the ratio of its
circumradius to its inradius; it is a scale-invariant measure of shape quality,
equal to $1$ for equilateral triangles and unbounded for skinny ones.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Maximin Angle Property]\label{thm:maximin}
Among all triangulations of a point set in general position, the Delaunay
triangulation maximizes the minimum angle (and, more strongly, is
lexicographically optimal in the sorted list of angles).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Flipping an illegal edge replaces two angles $\alpha, \beta$ (the angles
opposite the illegal edge) with angles $\alpha', \beta'$ satisfying
$\min(\alpha',\beta') > \min(\alpha,\beta)$: the smallest of the four angles of
the quadrilateral strictly increases. Since Lawson's algorithm
(Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping}) reaches the Delaunay triangulation
by such flips and each flip only improves the minimum angle, no triangulation
can have a larger minimum angle. The lexicographic claim follows by the same
monotonicity on the sorted angle sequence.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Angle Lower Bound]\label{cor:angle_bound}
Every triangle of the Delaunay triangulation of points in general position has
minimum angle at least $30^\circ$ when restricted to its circumcircle's
diameter bound; more relevantly, Delaunay refinement
(Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement}) can push the bound to any prescribed
value by inserting points.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

Quality measures are cheap to evaluate and are used to drive refinement:

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Quality Report}
\label{alg:quality_report}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require a triangulation $\mathcal{T}$ and a target minimum angle $\theta_{\min}$.
\Ensure the list of triangles violating the quality bound.
\Function{ReportSkinnyTriangles}{$\mathcal{T}, \theta_{\min}$}
  \State $\text{violators} \gets []$
  \For{each triangle $T \in \mathcal{T}$}
    \State $\theta_T \gets $ minimum angle of $T$
    \If{$\theta_T < \theta_{\min}$}
      \State append $T$ to $\text{violators}$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\text{violators}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Quality metric}: minimum angle, circumradius-to-shortest-edge
  ratio, and radius ratio are equivalent up to constants for bounded shapes;
  pick the one matched to the solver's error estimate.
\item \textbf{Target angle}: angles above $\approx 30^\circ$ typically require
  more Steiner points; the mesh-size trade-off drives
  Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement}'s parameters.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n)$ to evaluate quality over all triangles; one
  constant-time formula per triangle.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$ for the report.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mesh quality control}: FEM and CFD pipelines validate meshes
  against minimum-angle or aspect-ratio thresholds before solving.
\item \textbf{Refinement driver}: skinny-triangle reports feed the
  circumcenter-insertion loop (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Degenerate triangles}: collinear vertices yield zero angles; the
  report must flag them without division-by-zero.
\item \textbf{Extreme aspect ratios}: verify scale invariance under uniform
  scaling and rotation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Delaunay Refinement}
\label{sec:delaunay_refinement}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Delaunay triangulations of a \emph{point set} can still contain skinny triangles
when the points are badly distributed. \index{Delaunay refinement}\emph{Delaunay
refinement} (Chew~\cite{chew1989}, Ruppert~\cite{ruppert1995}) inserts new
points---circumcenters of skinny triangles---until every triangle satisfies a
quality bound. Ruppert's algorithm and its successors are the workhorses of
2D quality mesh generation (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_mesh_applications}) and
are the reason angle bounds such as ``no angle below $20.7^\circ$'' appear in
every meshing manual.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Skinny Triangle]\label{def:skinny}
A triangle is \emph{skinny} if its circumradius-to-shortest-edge ratio
$\rho = R / s$ exceeds a threshold $B$; equivalently its minimum angle is below
$\arcsin(1 / (2B))$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Encroached Segment]\label{def:encroached}
A segment of the domain boundary (or a constraint) is \emph{encroached} by a
vertex $v$ if $v$ lies inside the circle with the segment as diameter. An
encroached segment must be split at its midpoint before any circumcenter inside
its influence region is inserted.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Ruppert's Algorithm]\label{thm:ruppert}
Let a planar straight-line graph be input with no two segments forming an
angle below a threshold $\phi$ (typically $90^\circ$). Ruppert's refinement
algorithm terminates and produces a mesh in which every triangle has
circumradius-to-shortest-edge ratio at most $B \ge \sqrt{2}$, hence minimum
angle at least $\arcsin(1/(2B))$ (about $20.7^\circ$ for $B = \sqrt{2}$), and
whose size is within a constant factor of the smallest possible mesh satisfying
the bound.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Termination rests on a shortest-edge lemma: inserting the circumcenter of a
skinny triangle (or the midpoint of an encroached segment) always creates edges
of length at least $L$ times the length of the offending short edge, for a fixed
$L = \sin\theta$ with $B \ge \sqrt{2}$. Since every newly inserted point is a
constant fraction away from all existing points, the smallest edge length can
decrease by at most a fixed factor, and after finitely many reductions the
refinement stops. Optimality follows because every inserted circumcenter lies
in a region that must contain a Steiner point in any mesh achieving the bound,
so the count is within a constant factor of optimum~\cite{ruppert1995,shewchuk1998}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Ruppert Delaunay Refinement}
\label{alg:ruppert_refine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require PSLG $G$, ratio bound $B$.
\Ensure a quality Delaunay mesh of $G$.
\Function{RuppertRefine}{$G, B$}
  \State $\mathcal{T} \gets $ conforming Delaunay triangulation of $G$
  \State $Q \gets $ queue of encroached segments and skinny triangles
  \While{$Q$ is not empty}
    \State $x \gets Q.\text{pop}()$ (prefer encroached segments)
    \If{$x$ is an encroached segment}
      \State insert its midpoint; re-triangulate locally; enqueue new
             encroached segments and skinny triangles
    \Else \Comment{$x$ is a skinny triangle}
      \State $c \gets \text{circumcenter}(x)$
      \If{some segment near $c$ is encroached}
        \State split that segment at its midpoint instead
      \Else
        \State insert $c$; re-triangulate locally; enqueue new skinny triangles
      \EndIf
    \EndIf
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $\mathcal{T}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ratio bound $B$}: $B = \sqrt{2}$ gives the classic
  $20.7^\circ$ guarantee; larger $B$ (e.g., $B = 2$) yields fewer points but
  weaker angles; Chew's variant attains $30^\circ$.
\item \textbf{Small input angles}: angles below $60^\circ$ in the input PSLG
  force special handling (segment splitting at graded lengths) because
  circumcenter insertion near acute corners can create arbitrarily short edges.
\item \textbf{Sizing fields}: refinement with a user sizing function
  (graded meshes) replaces the uniform bound $B$ by a local target edge length.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ expected for the refinement loop, where $n$ is
  the output size.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$.
\item \textbf{Output size}: within a constant factor of the optimal mesh
  achieving the same angle bound (Theorem~\ref{thm:ruppert}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Quality mesh generation}: the meshers behind
  Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_mesh_applications} (Triangle, CGAL, TetGen in 2D
  analog form) implement Ruppert's algorithm directly.
\item \textbf{Conforming CDTs}: refinement yields a conforming Delaunay
  triangulation that contains the constraints and satisfies angle bounds,
  combining Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_constrained} with quality guarantees.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Acute input angles}: verify termination when the input contains
  $30^\circ$ corners by checking the segment-splitting path is taken.
\item \textbf{Encroachment chains}: an encroached midpoint can encroach on
  neighbors; the queue must drain without infinite loops (the $B \ge \sqrt{2}$
  condition prevents cascades).
\item \textbf{Verification}: after termination, assert every triangle has ratio
  $\le B$ and every segment is unencroached.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Chew, L.~P. \cite{chew1989}.
\item Ruppert, J. \cite{ruppert1995}.
\item Shewchuk, J.~R. \cite{shewchuk1998}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Higher-Dimensional Delaunay Complexes}
\label{sec:delaunay_higher_dimensional}

\subsection{1. Overview}

All of the theory extends to $\mathbb{R}^d$ with the empty-circumsphere
property replacing the empty circle. The Delaunay complex of a point set in
$\mathbb{R}^d$ is a simplicial complex of $d$-simplices, and its restriction
to simplices whose circumradius is bounded---the \index{alpha shape}\emph{alpha
complex}---is the standard structure for surface reconstruction from point
clouds. Weighted (regular) triangulations generalize the construction to sites
with radii, replacing circles by power regions.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Empty Circumsphere]\label{def:empty_circumsphere}
A simplex $T$ of a point set in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is \emph{Delaunay} if the open
ball bounded by the circumsphere of $T$ contains no point of the set. The
\emph{Delaunay complex} is the collection of all such simplices; its maximal
$d$-simplices tile the convex hull of the point set when the points are in
general position.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Alpha Complex]\label{def:delaunay_alpha_complex}
For a real $\alpha \ge 0$, the \emph{alpha complex} consists of all Delaunay
simplices whose circumradius is at most $\alpha$ (and, recursively, all faces of
those simplices). Varying $\alpha$ from $0$ to $\infty$ produces the
\emph{alpha shape} family interpolating from the empty set to the convex hull.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Regular Triangulation]\label{def:regular_triangulation}
For weighted points $(p_i, r_i)$, the \emph{power distance} of $x$ to $p_i$ is
$\|x - p_i\|^2 - r_i^2$. A \emph{regular triangulation} (weighted Delaunay
triangulation) is defined by empty \emph{power spheres}: no point of the set
lies in the power region of any simplex. Its dual is the power (weighted
Voronoi) diagram.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Delaunay Complex in Higher Dimensions]\label{thm:high_dim_delaunay}
Let $P$ be a set of points in general position in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The Delaunay
complex is a triangulation of the convex hull of $P$. Lifting $p \mapsto
(p, \|p\|^2)$ to the paraboloid in $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ shows that the Delaunay
complex is the projection of the lower hull of the lifted points, exactly as in
two dimensions (Theorem~\ref{thm:lifting}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The determinant form of the in-circle predicate generalizes verbatim to a
$(d+2) \times (d+2)$ determinant whose sign decides whether a point lies inside
a circumsphere. All two-dimensional arguments---cavity repair, flips, lifting,
randomized incremental construction---carry through with the same proofs,
replacing circles by spheres and triangles by $d$-simplices.
\end{proof}

In three dimensions the situation differs in one essential way: the Delaunay
tetrahedralization need not be unique, and no flip sequence suffices to reach
it from an arbitrary tetrahedralization. Moreover the worst-case complexity
grows.

\begin{theorem}[Complexity in Fixed Dimension]\label{thm:high_dim_size}
In $\mathbb{R}^d$ the Delaunay complex has $\Theta(n^{\lceil d/2 \rceil})$
simplices in the worst case; in $\mathbb{R}^3$ it has $O(n^2)$ tetrahedra and
can be constructed by randomized incremental insertion in expected $O(n^2)$
time and $O(n \log n)$ time for well-distributed inputs.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Bowyer--Watson in Higher Dimensions}
\label{alg:delaunay_3d}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require point set $P$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$, enclosing $(d{+}1)$-simplex.
\Ensure the Delaunay complex of $P$.
\Function{InsertDelaunayD}{$P$}
  \State $\mathcal{K} \gets $ single enclosing $(d{+}1)$-simplex
  \For{each $p \in P$}
    \State cavity $\gets \{ T \in \mathcal{K} : p \in \text{circumsphere}(T) \}$
    \State remove the cavity; add the fan of simplices from $p$ to each
           boundary facet of the cavity
  \EndFor
  \State remove simplices incident to the enclosing simplex
  \State \Return $\mathcal{K}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dimension}: the code is generic in $d$ but the practical
  sweet spot is $d \le 4$; beyond that the $\Theta(n^{\lceil d/2\rceil})$ size
  bound makes exact construction prohibitive and approximate methods (spatial
  hashing, approximate NNS) are used instead.
\item \textbf{Weights}: regular triangulations encode anisotropic or
  radius-bearing sites (spheres, molecules); they are the default when inputs
  have sizes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: expected $O(n \log n)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$;
  $\Theta(n^{\lceil d/2\rceil})$ worst case in $\mathbb{R}^d$.
\item \textbf{Space}: the output itself dominates; in $\mathbb{R}^3$ up to
  $O(n^2)$ tetrahedra.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Surface reconstruction}: the alpha complex (at a suitable
  $\alpha$) reconstructs surfaces from scanner output, as used in the reverse
  engineering pipelines of Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_mesh_applications}.
\item \textbf{Tetrahedral meshing}: Delaunay tetrahedralizations of 3D point
  sets (with refinement for quality) are the foundation of TetGen and CGAL
  volume meshing.
\item \textbf{Molecular modeling}: regular triangulations compute
  power/Voronoi decompositions of atom models for solvent-accessible surface
  areas.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cospherical points}: five cospherical points make the
  tetrahedralization non-unique; verify consistent tie-breaking.
\item \textbf{Slivers}: four nearly coplanar points produce a near-degenerate
  tetrahedron; report the smallest radius ratio to check mesh quality.
\item \textbf{Degenerate lower dimensions}: points embedded in a
  lower-dimensional affine subspace (all points coplanar in $\mathbb{R}^3$)
  must be handled by projecting to the subspace first.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Bowyer, A. \cite{bowyer1981}; Watson, D.~F. \cite{watson1981}.
\item Edelsbrunner, H., M\"ucke, E.~P. \cite{edelsbrunner1994}, alpha shapes.
\item Edelsbrunner, H. \cite{edelsbrunner1987}.
\item Okabe, A., et al. \cite{okabe2000}, Ch.~4.
\end{itemize}

\section{Intrinsic Delaunay Triangulation}
\label{sec:delaunay_intrinsic}

The Euclidean Delaunay triangulation is defined through empty circumcircles, a notion that depends on the ambient metric of $\mathbb{R}^2$. On a polyhedral surface $\mathcal{M}$ triangulated by triangles that do not lie in a common plane, this extrinsic test is meaningless: the two triangles sharing an edge lie in different tangent planes, so their circumcircles (as circles in $\mathbb{R}^3$) cannot be compared. An \emph{intrinsic Delaunay triangulation}\index{intrinsic Delaunay triangulation} instead uses only the intrinsic metric of the surface, i.e., the edge lengths of the mesh, to decide legality. This section follows the flip-based construction of Bobenko and Springborn~\cite{bobenko2007} and its practical implementation by Fisher et al.\ \cite{fisher2007}.

\begin{definition}[Intrinsic Delaunay Edge]\label{def:intrinsic_delaunay_edge}
Let $e = (i,j)$ be an interior edge of a triangulated polyhedral surface, shared by triangles $T_1$ and $T_2$, and let $\alpha_e$ and $\beta_e$ be the angles opposite $e$ in $T_1$ and $T_2$, respectively. The edge $e$ is \emph{intrinsically Delaunay}\index{intrinsic Delaunay edge} if
\[
\alpha_e + \beta_e \le \pi.
\]
A triangulation is \emph{intrinsic Delaunay} if every interior edge is intrinsically Delaunay.
\end{definition}

Because each triangle of the surface is intrinsically a Euclidean triangle, the angles $\alpha_e$ and $\beta_e$ are computed from the three edge lengths of the respective triangles using the law of cosines. The criterion therefore depends only on the intrinsic metric, never on how the triangles are embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

\begin{definition}[Cotangent Weight]\label{def:cotangent_weight}
For an interior edge $e$, the \emph{cotangent weight} is $w_e = \cot \alpha_e + \cot \beta_e$, where $\alpha_e, \beta_e$ are the angles opposite $e$. The identity
\[
\cot \alpha_e + \cot \beta_e = \frac{\sin(\alpha_e + \beta_e)}{\sin \alpha_e \sin \beta_e}
\]
shows that $w_e \ge 0$ if and only if $\alpha_e + \beta_e \le \pi$. Thus a triangulation is intrinsic Delaunay if and only if all cotangent weights are non-negative.
\end{definition}

The connection to the Laplace--Beltrami operator motivates the definition. The cotangent Laplacian of the mesh has off-diagonal entries $-\tfrac{1}{2} w_e$ and diagonal entries summing to zero; it is positive semi-definite exactly when the triangulation is intrinsic Delaunay. Negative weights, which can appear in an ordinary triangulation containing obtuse triangles, are responsible for spurious oscillations in discrete harmonic functions and for unstable heat propagation. Intrinsic Delaunay re-triangulation removes this defect at no cost in resolution.

\subsection{Algorithm}

The construction proceeds by flipping illegal edges exactly as in the planar case (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_flips}), but with two differences:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The legality test uses the intrinsic angle sum $\alpha_e + \beta_e$ rather than the planar in-circle predicate.
\item After a flip, the new diagonal $e'$ of the quadrilateral must be given an intrinsic length. The two triangles sharing $e$ are, intrinsically, two Euclidean triangles glued along $e$; unfolding them into a common plane yields a flat (possibly non-convex) quadrilateral, and the new edge length is the length of the other diagonal of this quadrilateral, computed from the four known edge lengths.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Intrinsic Delaunay Flip Algorithm}
\label{alg:intrinsic_delaunay_flip}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{IntrinsicDelaunay}{triangulation}
  \State $queue \gets$ all interior edges of $triangulation$
  \While{$queue$ is not empty}
    \State $e \gets queue.\text{pop}()$
    \State $(\alpha_e, \beta_e) \gets$ angles opposite $e$ in its two incident triangles
    \If{$\alpha_e + \beta_e > \pi$}
      \State $e' \gets$ other diagonal of the quadrilateral formed by the two triangles
      \State $\ell(e') \gets$ intrinsic length of $e'$ from the unfolded quadrilateral
      \State flip $e$ to $e'$
      \For{$f$ in the two newly created edges}
        \State $\text{push}(queue, f)$
      \EndFor
    \EndIf
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $triangulation$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Existence of Intrinsic Delaunay Triangulation]\label{thm:intrinsic_delaunay_exists}
Every triangulation of a polyhedral surface admits an intrinsic Delaunay re-triangulation of the same vertices, obtainable by a finite sequence of edge flips~\cite{bobenko2007,fisher2007}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Each flip replaces an edge whose opposite angles sum to more than $\pi$ by the other diagonal. An angle-sum decrease argument shows that every flip strictly decreases a global potential over the set of triangulations of the intrinsic metric; since the set of triangulations of a fixed vertex set is finite, the process terminates. When the intrinsic Delaunay condition determines every diagonal uniquely, the resulting triangulation is unique; otherwise ties are broken consistently, for example lexicographically over edges.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The intrinsic Delaunay triangulation of a mesh is the dual of the \emph{geodesic Voronoi diagram} of its vertices on the surface, just as the planar Delaunay triangulation is the dual of the planar Voronoi diagram. This explains why the intrinsic construction is the ``correct'' generalization for surface processing.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Properties}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Metric invariance}: The result depends only on edge lengths, so it is invariant under isometric deformations of the surface (bending without stretching).
\item \textbf{Non-negative weights}: All cotangent weights are non-negative, making the cotangent Laplacian positive semi-definite and well-behaved for PDEs, harmonic maps, and the heat method.
\item \textbf{Same vertex set}: The algorithm changes only connectivity, never vertex positions; it is a pure re-tiling of the intrinsic metric.
\item \textbf{Compatibility}: The intrinsic flip criterion coincides with the planar one on flat regions of the mesh, so the algorithm reproduces the usual Delaunay triangulation on planar surfaces.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: Each flip is $O(1)$; the total number of flips is $O(n^2)$ in the worst case and $O(n)$ in typical cases for meshes with reasonable initial quality~\cite{fisher2007}.
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$ for the mesh and the queue of candidate edges.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Applications}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Discrete Laplace--Beltrami}: Guaranteeing positive semi-definiteness of the cotangent operator for spectral analysis and shape signature computation.
\item \textbf{Heat methods}: Stabilizing the heat kernel and geodesic-distance approximation on meshes with obtuse triangles.
\item \textbf{Harmonic and biharmonic fields}: Removing spurious extrema caused by negative cotangent weights in, e.g., parameterization and deformation~\cite{bobenko2007}.
\item \textbf{Discrete exterior calculus}: Restoring the non-negativity of the Hodge-star edge weights discussed in the discrete exterior calculus chapter (Section~\ref{sec:hodge_star}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Applications to Mesh Generation}
\label{sec:delaunay_mesh_applications}

Delaunay meshes are the workhorse of industrial mesh generation because the
empty-circumcircle (empty-circumsphere in 3D) property and the associated
refinement algorithms of Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement} produce
element-quality guarantees that are provably unobtainable with other
triangulation schemes. The following are the principal industrial use cases.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Finite Element Analysis (FEM)}: Delaunay meshes with the
  refinement guarantees of Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement} bound the
  minimum angle of every triangle away from zero, which is necessary for the
  conditioning of the stiffness matrix and the convergence of FEM solvers in
  structural and thermal analysis. The commercial FEA market---\texttt{ANSYS},
  \texttt{Abaqus} (Dassault Syst\`emes), \texttt{COMSOL}, and Altair---sells
  solvers whose mesh generators are exactly this refinement
  scheme~\cite{shewchuk1998}.

\item \textbf{Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)}: Commercial CFD meshers
  (\texttt{ANSYS} Fluent meshing, SIMULIA, \texttt{STAR-CCM+}, and
  \texttt{Pointwise}) generate quality tetrahedral meshes from CAD geometry;
  the empty-sphere property keeps high-aspect-ratio elements near the
  anisotropic inflation layers while preserving the angle bounds that
  stabilize pressure--velocity coupling on aircraft, turbine, and vehicle
  models.

\item \textbf{Reservoir Simulation (Voronoi/PEBI Simulation Grids)}: The
  perpendicular-bisector (PEBI) grids used for reservoir flow simulation are
  Voronoi diagrams, and their construction is driven by the dual Delaunay
  triangulation. Commercial reservoir-modeling suites such as \texttt{Roxar
  RMS} (Emerson) build unstructured simulation grids whose cells honor
  wells, faults, and fracture networks, on which multiphase flow and
  transport solvers run.

\item \textbf{Medical Imaging and Surgical Planning}: Delaunay
  tetrahedralization of segmented CT and MRI volumes produces the volumetric
  meshes used in finite-element modeling of bone, soft tissue, and blood
  flow. Commercial planning platforms such as \texttt{Materialise Mimics}
  sell patient-specific modeling for surgical guides, implants, and
  biomechanical simulation.

\item \textbf{Reverse Engineering and Metrology}: Surface reconstruction
  from laser-scan point clouds builds a Delaunay (or alpha-shape,
  Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_higher_dimensional}) complex of the scanned
  part. Commercial scanning pipelines such as \texttt{PolyWorks}
  (InnovMetric) and \texttt{Geomagic} (3D Systems) ship this reconstruction
  and the resulting mesh to downstream CAD and inspection.

\item \textbf{Geographic Information Systems (GIS)}: Conforming Delaunay
  triangulations of terrain point clouds produce the triangulated irregular
  networks (TINs) that back digital elevation models, visibility analysis,
  and contouring in \texttt{Esri ArcGIS} 3D Analyst and comparable
  commercial GIS; the conforming refinement also generates the meshes for
  watershed and flood modeling.

\item \textbf{Shape and Topology Optimization}: The conforming mesh produced
  by Delaunay refinement with sizing fields adapts element density to the
  stress field, accelerating the density-based optimization loop in
  commercial tools such as \texttt{Altair OptiStruct}, SIMULIA Tosca, and
  \texttt{nTopology} for automotive and aerospace lightweighting.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Industrial-Scale Software}
The algorithms of this chapter are implemented at production scale in the
open-source meshers \texttt{TetGen} (tetrahedralization and constrained
Delaunay), \texttt{CGAL} (2D and 3D Delaunay and regular triangulations),
\texttt{Triangle} (2D CDT and refinement, embedded under license in
commercial GIS and numerical products), \texttt{Gmsh} (unstructured mesh
generation), and \texttt{MMG} (remeshing and metric adaptation), as well as
in commercial tools such as \texttt{ANSYS}, \texttt{COMSOL}, and
\texttt{Pointwise} for CFD. Their shared core is the incremental insertion and
flip algorithm of Sections~\ref{sec:delaunay_incremental} and
\ref{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping}, coupled with the quality refinement
guarantees of Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement}.
