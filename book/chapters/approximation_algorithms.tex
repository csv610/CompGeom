\chapter{Approximation Algorithms in Geometry}
\label{ch:approximation_algorithms}

\section{Why Approximation?}
\label{sec:approx_why}

Many optimization problems that sound easy to state are computationally
intractable. The traveling salesman problem asks for the shortest tour that
visits every given point; the $k$-center problem asks for the $k$ balls of
smallest radius covering a set; $k$-median and $k$-means ask for $k$ centers
minimizing the sum of (squared) distances to the points they serve; and
geometric covering and packing ask for the fewest shapes covering a set, or
the largest number of non-overlapping shapes fitting in a container. Every
one of these problems is NP-hard, and several remain NP-hard even for points
in the plane, so exact polynomial-time algorithms are out of the question
unless the celebrated conjecture $\mathrm{P} \neq \mathrm{NP}$ fails.

This chapter studies the alternative computational geometry offers:
\textbf{approximation algorithms}\index{approximation algorithm} that run in
polynomial time and return solutions provably close to optimal. Two
resources trade off against each other: the running time and the
\emph{approximation factor}, the worst-case ratio between the returned and
the optimal value. At one end, a constant-factor approximation is cheap; at
the other, a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) drives the ratio to
$1 + \varepsilon$ for any fixed $\varepsilon > 0$ at a cost that grows with
$1/\varepsilon$.

Geometry makes approximation easier than it is for arbitrary graphs, for
three reasons. First, Euclidean distances satisfy the
\textbf{triangle inequality}\index{triangle inequality}, which bounds the
cost of shortcuts and local improvements. Second,
\textbf{packing bounds} -- a ball of radius $R$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$ holds only
boundedly many mutually separated points -- keep quadtree-based structures
under control; these power the Arora--Mitchell PTAS, approximate nearest
neighbors, and well-separated pairs. Third, \textbf{small representatives
exist}: a small sample or core-set often suffices for a near-optimal answer.
The systematic theory is Har-Peled's monograph \cite{harpeled2011};
the textbooks \cite{deberg2008,cormen2009} cover the elementary material,
the randomization tools are in Chapter~\ref{ch:randomized_algorithms}, and
the range-counting machinery in Chapter~\ref{ch:range_searching}.

Sections~\ref{sec:approx_intro}--\ref{sec:approx_eps} set up the vocabulary
(approximation ratios, PTAS/FPTAS) and the sampling theorems
($\varepsilon$-nets and $\varepsilon$-approximations).
Sections~\ref{sec:approx_coresets}--\ref{sec:approx_distance_queries}
build the data structures: core-sets, approximate nearest neighbors,
locality-sensitive hashing, well-separated pairs, and spanners.
Sections~\ref{sec:approx_clustering}--\ref{sec:approx_enclosing} apply them
to clustering, $k$-center/$k$-median, and minimum enclosing shapes, and
Section~\ref{sec:approx_high_dimensional} treats high dimensions. The
chapter closes with exercises and a project on approximate nearest-neighbor
search.

\section{Geometric Approximation}
\label{sec:approx_intro}

\begin{definition}[Approximation Ratio]\label{def:approx_ratio}
  An \textbf{$\alpha$-approximation algorithm} for a minimization problem
  returns, on every instance $I$, a feasible solution with value
  $\operatorname{val}(A(I)) \leq \alpha \cdot \operatorname{OPT}(I)$, where
  $\operatorname{OPT}(I)$ is the optimal value and $\alpha \geq 1$ is the
  \textbf{approximation ratio}\index{approximation ratio}. For
  maximization problems the inequality is reversed. An algorithm with ratio
  $1 + \varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$ is a
  \textbf{$(1+\varepsilon)$-approximation}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[PTAS and FPTAS]\label{def:approx_ptas}
  A problem admits a \textbf{polynomial-time approximation scheme} (PTAS)
  if for every fixed $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a polynomial-time algorithm
  computing a $(1 + \varepsilon)$-approximation, the constant of the
  polynomial depending arbitrarily on $\varepsilon$. A \textbf{fully
  polynomial-time approximation scheme} (FPTAS) runs in time polynomial in
  both $n$ and $1/\varepsilon$; a \textbf{randomized} PTAS succeeds with
  probability at least $1/2$ and is boosted by repetition. These are the
  standard definitions of \cite{cormen2009}.
\end{definition}

Two facts bound what is possible. An FPTAS implies the problem is only
weakly NP-hard; strongly NP-hard geometric problems therefore have PTASs at
best, with running time exponential in $1/\varepsilon$. And some problems
resist a PTAS entirely: $k$-center cannot be approximated within
$2 - \delta$ unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$ (Section~\ref{sec:approx_k_center_median}),
so the goal there is a tight constant factor.

\begin{example}[Metric TSP]\label{ex:approx_tsp_ratio}
  In a general metric, the Christofides algorithm (see \cite{cormen2009})
  finds a tour of length at most $3/2$ times optimal: it builds a minimum
  spanning tree, duplicates the odd-degree vertices to get an Eulerian
  multigraph, and shortcuts the Euler tour. The triangle inequality enters
  in the shortcut step. The Euclidean case is far better behaved: by
  Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_arora_ptas} it has a PTAS.
\end{example}

The signature achievement of geometric approximation is the
\textbf{quadtree-based PTAS for Euclidean TSP}, found independently by
Arora \cite{arora1998} and Mitchell \cite{mitchell1999}.

\begin{theorem}[Arora's PTAS for Euclidean TSP]\label{thm:approx_arora_ptas}
  For every fixed integer $c \geq 1$, there is a randomized algorithm that
  computes a Euclidean TSP tour on $n$ points whose length is within a
  factor $1 + 1/c$ of optimal, with probability at least $1/2$, in time
  $O(n\,(\log n)^{O(c)})$. Mitchell's guillotine subdivision method gives a
  deterministic scheme in $n^{O(c)}$ time with a simpler proof.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  The scheme has three steps. (i) \textbf{Quadtree dissection}: enclose the
  points in a square of side $2^L$ with $L = O(\log n)$ and split it into
  four equal quadrants until each cell holds at most one point.
  (ii) \textbf{Portals}: place $m = O(c \log n)$ equally spaced points on
  every cell edge and allow the tour to cross edges only at portals; a
  \emph{patching lemma} -- the triangle inequality plus a packing bound on
  how often a tour crosses a cell boundary -- shows some portals-only tour
  is within $1 + O(1/c)$ of optimal. (iii) \textbf{Dynamic programming}:
  over the quadtree cells, keep for each cell and each portal pairing the
  best set of paths visiting all points inside the cell; with $4m$ portals
  per cell there are $2^{O(m)} = n^{O(c)}$ pairing patterns, giving total
  time $O(n\,(\log n)^{O(c)})$. Repeating over a random shift of the grid
  boosts the probability. The scheme extends to $\mathbb{R}^d$, other norms,
  and Steiner tree and $k$-TSP; see \cite{arora1998,mitchell1999}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  The two ingredients -- a hierarchical decomposition whose cells have
  bounded crossing number, and an exact program over it -- recur throughout
  the chapter: they drive the core-set algorithms of
  Section~\ref{sec:approx_enclosing}, the well-separated pairs of
  Section~\ref{sec:approx_wsd}, and the approximate nearest-neighbor
  structure of Section~\ref{sec:approx_ann}.
\end{remark}

\section{\texorpdfstring{$\varepsilon$}{epsilon}-Approximations}
\label{sec:approx_eps}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Range searching (Chapter~\ref{ch:range_searching}) answers exact counting
queries. $\varepsilon$-nets and $\varepsilon$-approximations approximate
\emph{all} such counts simultaneously with a single small random sample
whose size depends only on the range family, not on $n$. They are the
probabilistic backbone of this chapter: core-sets
(Section~\ref{sec:approx_coresets}), randomized algorithms
(Section~\ref{sec:randomized_eps_nets}), and partition-tree range structures
all rest on the sampling theorems proved here.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Range Space]\label{def:approx_range_space}
  A \textbf{range space} is a pair $(X, \mathcal{R})$ of a finite ground set
  $X$ and a family $\mathcal{R}$ of subsets (the \textbf{ranges}); for point
  sets in $\mathbb{R}^d$ the standard ranges are halfspaces, disks,
  rectangles, and simplices. The \textbf{VC dimension}
  $\operatorname{VCdim}(\mathcal{R})$ is the size of the largest subset of
  $X$ that is \emph{shattered} by $\mathcal{R}$, i.e., whose every subset is
  realized as $R \cap Y$. Halfplanes in the plane have VC dimension $3$;
  axis-parallel rectangles have VC dimension $4$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\varepsilon$-Net and $\varepsilon$-Approximation]\label{def:approx_eps_net}
  A subset $N \subseteq X$ is an \textbf{$\varepsilon$-net} if every range
  $R$ with $|R| \geq \varepsilon |X|$ contains a point of $N$. A subset $A$
  is an \textbf{$\varepsilon$-approximation} if for every
  $R \in \mathcal{R}$,
  \[
    \left|\frac{|A \cap R|}{|A|} - \frac{|R|}{|X|}\right| \leq \varepsilon .
  \]
\end{definition}

An $\varepsilon$-approximation is stronger than an $\varepsilon$-net: a
range with an $\varepsilon$-fraction of the points is counted with
positive empirical measure, hence hit. This is the notion introduced as
Definition~\ref{def:eps_net} in Chapter~\ref{ch:range_searching}: nets serve
\emph{hitting} problems, approximations serve \emph{counting}.

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Haussler--Welzl]\label{thm:approx_eps_net_bound}
  Let $(X, \mathcal{R})$ have VC dimension $d$. A random sample of
  $m = O(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})$ points is an
  $\varepsilon$-net with probability at least $1/2$; an additive
  $O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta})$ raises it to $1 - \delta$.
\end{theorem}

The proof in \cite{hausslerwelzl1987} bounds the number of ``essentially
different'' ranges by $O(m^d)$ via shattering, then applies a union bound:
a range of size $\varepsilon |X|$ is missed with probability at most
$(1 - \varepsilon)^m$.

\begin{theorem}[$\varepsilon$-Approximations by Sampling]\label{thm:approx_eps_approx_bound}
  For VC dimension $d$, a random sample of
  $m = O(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac{d}{\varepsilon})$ is an
  $\varepsilon$-approximation with probability at least $1/2$. Halfspace
  ranges in $\mathbb{R}^d$ admit $\varepsilon$-approximations of size
  $O(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})$.
\end{theorem}

The size is optimal up to logarithmic factors: variance forces
$\Omega(1/\varepsilon^2)$, and the halfspace upper bounds follow from the
discrepancy theory of \cite{matousek1995,matousek1999}; a set of small
discrepancy is exactly a good $\varepsilon$-approximation.

\begin{remark}[Covering and Hitting]\label{rem:approx_hitting}
  An $\varepsilon$-net is a \textbf{hitting set} for the ranges that are
  large relative to $X$. By duality this gives constant-factor
  approximations for many geometric covering problems in fixed dimension.
  Packing is different: geometric bin packing (Chapter~\ref{ch:packing_algorithms},
  Section~\ref{sec:rectangle_packing}) is NP-hard
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:rect_pack_nphard}) yet admits approximation schemes of
  its own; see \cite{deberg2008,harpeled2011}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Random Sampling for $\varepsilon$-Nets}
\label{alg:approx_eps_net_sample}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Range space $(X, \mathcal{R})$ of VC dimension $d$; error $\varepsilon$; confidence $\delta$.
\Ensure An $\varepsilon$-net $N$ of $X$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$.
\Function{NetSample}{$X$, $d$, $\varepsilon$, $\delta$}
  \State $m \gets \lceil \frac{c}{\varepsilon}(d\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon} + \log\frac{1}{\delta})\rceil$
  \State draw $m$ points of $X$ independently and uniformly at random into $N$
  \For{each range $R$ with $|R| \geq \varepsilon |X|$}
    \State verify $R \cap N \neq \emptyset$ \Comment{range-counting step}
  \EndFor
  \If{some large range misses $N$}
    \State \Return \Call{NetSample}{X, d, \varepsilon, \delta} \Comment{resample}
  \EndIf
  \State \Return $N$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Verifying the net property uses the range trees and partition trees of
Chapter~\ref{ch:range_searching}.

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\varepsilon$}: the sample grows like $d/\varepsilon$ (nets)
  or $d/\varepsilon^2$ (approximations); use a net when only a witness is
  needed and an approximation when counts must be accurate.
\item \textbf{VC dimension $d$}: intervals on a line have $d = 2$;
  halfplanes and disks in the plane $d = 3$; simplices in $\mathbb{R}^d$
  grow linearly in $d$ (\cite{hausslerwelzl1987,matousek1999}).
\item \textbf{Confidence}: repetition boosts the guarantee at logarithmic
  cost.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(m)$ to draw the sample, plus a verification pass;
  with logarithmic range counting, $O(m \log^{O(1)} n)$ total.
\item \textbf{Size}: independent of $n$ -- $O(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}
  \log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})$ and $O(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^2}
  \log\frac{d}{\varepsilon})$ respectively. This independence is what makes
  the rest of the chapter nearly input-size-free.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Range counting}: estimate any query count in constant time
  after sampling.
\item \textbf{Hitting sets for covering}: Remark~\ref{rem:approx_hitting}.
\item \textbf{Discrepancy theory}: the deterministic counterpart
  (\cite{matousek1999}).
\item \textbf{Core-sets}: sampling is the cheapest construction of the
  core-sets of Section~\ref{sec:approx_coresets}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item Verify the net property on adversarial ranges just above
  $\varepsilon |X|$ points, and at the exact threshold.
\item Test duplicates (VC dimension can drop) and collinear configurations.
\item For approximations, check the count error is at most $\varepsilon$
  on all tested ranges, not merely on average.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

Haussler and Welzl's $\varepsilon$-net theorem \cite{hausslerwelzl1987},
Matou\v{s}ek's discrepancy constructions \cite{matousek1995,matousek1999},
and the textbook treatments \cite{deberg2008,harpeled2011}.

\section{Coresets}
\label{sec:approx_coresets}

A recurring theme is that a \emph{small representative} of the input
suffices for a near-optimal answer.

\begin{definition}[Core-Set]\label{def:approx_coreset}
  Let $f$ be a cost on point sets and $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ the input. A
  weighted subset $S \subseteq P$ (point $s$ with weight $w(s) \geq 0$) is
  an \textbf{$\varepsilon$-core-set}\index{core-set} for $f$ if for every
  candidate solution $C$,
  \[
    (1 - \varepsilon)\, f_C(P) \;\leq\; f_C(S) \;\leq\;
    (1 + \varepsilon)\, f_C(P),
  \]
  where $f_C$ holds $C$ fixed. Then any near-optimal solution on $S$ is
  near-optimal on $P$.
\end{definition}

Core-sets give a dimension-free speed-up: an exact algorithm -- however slow
-- run on the $m$ points of $S$ yields a $(1 + \varepsilon)$-approximation
for $P$. The two constructions are random sampling
(Section~\ref{sec:approx_eps}) and greedy farthest-point expansion; both
produce sizes bounded in terms of $\varepsilon$ and $d$ but not $n$, which
also enables streaming and distributed algorithms that merge core-sets.

\begin{theorem}[Core-Sets for Minimum Enclosing Balls]\label{thm:approx_meb_coreset_bound}
  Let $P$ have $n$ points and minimum enclosing ball radius $r^*$. For every
  $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$: (i) an $\varepsilon$-core-set of size
  $O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ exists, and this is tight \cite{badoiu2002};
  (ii) the farthest-point procedure of
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_meb_coreset} finds a ball of radius
  $(1 + \varepsilon) r^*$ in time $O(dn/\varepsilon + \varepsilon^{-5})$
  with a core-set of size at most $\lceil 2/\varepsilon \rceil$
  \cite{badoiu2002}; (iii) Kumar, Mitchell, and Yildirim compute a
  $(1 + \varepsilon)$-approximation in time linear in $n$ and $d$ for fixed
  $\varepsilon$, with core-sets of size $O(1/\varepsilon)$
  \cite{kumar2003}.
\end{theorem}

For clustering, Har-Peled and Mazumdar \cite{harpeled2004} showed $k$-means
and $k$-median admit core-sets of size $O(k \varepsilon^{-d} \log n)$ and
hence $(1 + \varepsilon)$-approximations linear in $n$ for fixed
$k, d, \varepsilon$; Section~\ref{sec:approx_k_center_median} develops the
algorithms. The connection to randomization is direct: every core-set here
is either a certified random sample (Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_eps_approx_bound})
or a greedy procedure that terminates after $O(1/\varepsilon)$ rounds
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_meb_coreset}). Section~\ref{sec:approx_high_dimensional}
returns to the striking fact that the ball core-set bound is
\emph{dimension-free}, which is why approximate minimum enclosing balls are
tractable in high dimensions where the exact algorithms of
Chapter~\ref{ch:enclosure_convex_distance} (Section~\ref{sec:welzls_algorithm})
are not.

\begin{remark}
  The word ``coreset'' also names the $\varepsilon$-approximation of a range
  space restricted to a query family (Definition~\ref{def:approx_eps_net});
  the distinction is only which cost is preserved.
\end{remark}

\section{Approximate Nearest Neighbors}
\label{sec:approx_ann}

\subsection{1. Overview}

The nearest-neighbor problem (Definition~\ref{def:nearest_neighbor},
Chapter~\ref{ch:proximity}) asks for the point of a preprocessed set $P$
closest to a query $q$. Exact search is easy in low dimensions -- the
$k$d-tree of Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_structures}
(Section~\ref{sec:kd_tree}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:kd_nn}) answers queries in
$O(\log n)$ expected time -- but degrades exponentially with the dimension,
and in high dimensions exact search suffers the curse of dimensionality
(Section~\ref{sec:highdim_curse}). \textbf{Approximate nearest neighbors}
(ANN)\index{approximate nearest neighbor} relax the answer by a factor
$c \geq 1$ and recover polynomial structure. This section develops the
low-dimensional regime via balanced box decompositions \cite{arya1998};
Section~\ref{sec:approx_lsh} handles the high-dimensional regime.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[$c$-Approximate Nearest Neighbor]\label{def:approx_ann_c}
  For $c \geq 1$, a point $p' \in P$ is a \textbf{$c$-approximate nearest
  neighbor} of query $q$ if $\|q - p'\| \leq c \min_{p \in P}\|q - p\|$. A
  structure supports \textbf{$c$-ANN queries} if it returns such a point
  for every query (cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:highdim_nearest_neighbor}).
\end{definition}

The relaxation makes search \emph{output-sensitive}: the algorithm may stop
once it can prove no unexplored point can beat the current answer by more
than $c$, and cell-based pruning makes that proof cheap.

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Balanced Box Decomposition]\label{thm:approx_ann_complexity}
  The \textbf{balanced box-decomposition (BBD) tree} stores $n$ points in
  $\mathbb{R}^d$ in $O(dn)$ space and $O(dn \log n)$ preprocessing time and
  answers $c$-ANN queries in $O(c^d \log n)$ time \cite{arya1998}; in fixed
  dimension the query time is $O(\log n)$, which is optimal in the worst
  case.
\end{theorem}

The BBD tree is a balanced cousin of the compressed quadtree whose nodes are
\emph{boxes} (axis-parallel boxes with few extra dimensions), with the
property that every node is either a grid cell or decomposes into boundedly
many sub-boxes. Search (Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_ann_quadtree}) keeps a
priority queue over boxes keyed by distance to the query and prunes a box
once its distance exceeds the current answer divided by $c$; packing bounds
limit the number of expanded boxes to $O(c^d)$. This is the same dissection
structure as Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_arora_ptas}, and it repairs the balance
weakness of the $k$d-tree (Theorem~\ref{thm:kd_nn_complexity}).

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{$c$-Approximate Nearest Neighbor via Box Decomposition}
\label{alg:approx_ann_quadtree}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A BBD tree $\mathcal{T}$ over $P$; query point $q$; factor $c \geq 1$.
\Ensure A point $p' \in P$ with $\|q - p'\| \leq c \min_{p \in P}\|q - p\|$.
\Function{ApproxNN}{$\mathcal{T}$, $q$, $c$}
  \State $R \gets +\infty$; $p' \gets$ any point of $P$
  \State priority queue $Q$ of boxes keyed by $\operatorname{dist}(q, \text{box})$; push root
  \While{$Q$ is not empty}
    \State pop box $B$ with minimal $\operatorname{dist}(q, B)$
    \If{$\operatorname{dist}(q, B) > R / c$} \State break \EndIf \Comment{pruning test}
    \If{$B$ is a leaf}
      \For{each $p \in B$} \If{$\|q - p\| < R$} $R \gets \|q - p\|$; $p' \gets p$ \EndIf \EndFor
    \Else \State push the children of $B$ onto $Q$ \EndIf
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $p'$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The pruning test is where the approximation enters: a box at distance
exceeding $R/c$ cannot contain a point closer than $R/c$, good enough since
$R$ is the best found so far.

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$c$}: query time grows like $c^d$; choose the smallest factor
  the application tolerates ($c = 1$ gives no pruning and degenerates to
  exact search).
\item \textbf{Box decomposition}: cap the extra dimensions per node and the
  leaf capacity to balance query and update cost.
\item \textbf{Search order}: best-first by distance minimizes expanded
  boxes; depth-first uses less queue memory.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Preprocessing}: $O(dn \log n)$ time, $O(dn)$ space.
\item \textbf{Query}: $O(c^d \log n)$; the term $c^d$ is why this is a
  \emph{fixed-dimensional} structure -- in high dimensions
  Section~\ref{sec:approx_lsh} is the better tool.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Registration and reconstruction}: closest-point queries feed
  the ICP-style matching of Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_matching}.
\item \textbf{Robotics and graphics}: proximity queries and collision
  acceleration (Chapter~\ref{ch:applications_graphics}).
\item \textbf{Similarity search}: practical systems such as navigable
  small-world graphs \cite{malkov2018} are heuristic cousins of the
  guarantees developed here.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item Compare against brute force on random sets and verify the ratio
  $\|q - p'\| / \|q - p^*\| \leq c$.
\item Adversarial clusters (true neighbor just beyond the first expanded
  box) stress the pruning test; verify termination with exact predicates
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_predicates}).
\item Queries far outside the data must trigger the break condition without
  scanning the tree.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

The BBD tree and its optimal query time are due to Arya, Mount, Netanyahu,
Silverman, and Wu \cite{arya1998}; the quadtree background is in
Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_structures} and the high-dimensional treatment in
\cite{harpeled2011}.

\section{Locality-Sensitive Hashing}
\label{sec:approx_lsh}

\subsection{1. Overview}

In high dimensions the BBD tree's $O(c^d \log n)$ query time of
Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_ann_complexity} is exponential in $d$.
\textbf{Locality-sensitive hashing} (LSH)\index{locality-sensitive hashing}
instead hashes points so that \emph{near} points collide with high
probability and \emph{far} points with low probability, then answers queries
by hashing and scanning colliding buckets. Indyk and Motwani's scheme
\cite{indyk1998} was the first to break the curse of dimensionality in the
worst case with sublinear query time. The engine is the random-projection
intuition of Chapter~\ref{ch:high_dimensional} (Section~\ref{sec:highdim_jl}).

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[$(r, cr, p_1, p_2)$-Sensitive Family]\label{def:approx_lsh_sensitive}
  A family $\mathcal{H}$ of hash functions from a metric space to a range
  $U$ is \textbf{$(r, cr, p_1, p_2)$-sensitive} for $c > 1$, $p_1 > p_2$ if
  for all $p, q$:
  \[
    d(p,q) \leq r \;\Rightarrow\; \Pr_h[h(p) = h(q)] \geq p_1, \qquad
    d(p,q) \geq cr \;\Rightarrow\; \Pr_h[h(p) = h(q)] \leq p_2 .
  \]
  The ratio $\rho = \frac{\ln(1/p_1)}{\ln(1/p_2)}$ controls efficiency.
\end{definition}

Single-coordinate projections give an $(r, cr, 1 - r/d, 1 - cr/d)$-sensitive
family on the Hamming cube; random hyperplanes serve Euclidean space.

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Indyk--Motwani]\label{thm:approx_lsh_quality}
  With $k = \log_{1/p_2} n$ and $L = n^{\rho}$, the structure of
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_lsh} answers $(c, r)$-approximate near-neighbor
  queries in $O(n^{\rho})$ expected time using $O(n^{1+\rho} + dn)$ space,
  with constant probability \cite{indyk1998}.
\end{theorem}

The design amplifies: with $k$ hashes composed, a far point collides with a
given query in one table with probability at most $1/n$, so the union bound
over the $n$ points keeps expected far collisions at $O(1)$ per table; with
$L = n^{\rho}$ independent tables, a near neighbor is missed with
probability at most $(1 - p_1^k)^L \leq 1/2$. This mirrors the union-bound
logic of Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_eps_net_bound}, with a hash function in
place of a range.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Locality-Sensitive Hashing: Index and Query}
\label{alg:approx_lsh}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data set $P$; parameters $k, L$; an $(r, cr, p_1, p_2)$-sensitive family $\mathcal{H}$.
\Ensure An index answering $(c, r)$-approximate near-neighbor queries.
\Function{BuildLSH}{$P$, $k$, $L$}
  \For{$j = 1$ \textbf{to} $L$}
    \State pick $g_j = (h_1, \dots, h_k)$, $h_i \in \mathcal{H}$ independent
    \State build a hash table $T_j$ storing $g_j(p)$ for every $p \in P$
  \EndFor
\EndFunction
\Function{QueryLSH}{$q$}
  \State $S \gets \emptyset$
  \For{$j = 1$ \textbf{to} $L$}
    \State $S \gets S \cup \{p \in P : g_j(p) = g_j(q)\}$
    \If{more than $2L$ points collected} \State break \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return the point of $S$ within distance $cr$ of $q$, or \textbf{null}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$k = \log_{1/p_2} n$}: balances far-point collisions (down
  with $k$) against near-point sensitivity (also down with $k$).
\item \textbf{$L = n^{\rho}$}: gives constant success probability; a
  constant-factor increase boosts it to $1 - \delta$.
\item \textbf{Radius $r$}: run the structure at geometrically growing radii
  $r, 2r, 4r, \dots$ to answer full ANN queries.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Preprocessing}: $O(dnL)$ time to hash, $O(n^{1+\rho} + dn)$
  space.
\item \textbf{Query}: $O(dkL)$ to hash plus $O(n^{\rho})$ expected scan --
  strictly sublinear in $n$ for $\rho < 1$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Similarity search}: near-duplicate detection and retrieval,
  and the shape matching of Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_matching}
  (Section~\ref{sec:matching_hashing}).
\item \textbf{High-dimensional ANN}: with the dimension-reduction pipeline
  of Section~\ref{sec:highdim_nearest_neighbor}.
\item \textbf{Learning}: kernel pipelines where exact search is the
  bottleneck (Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry_data_ml}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item Measure empirical recall as a function of $L$ and check it rises
  toward $1$.
\item Adversarial inputs (points clustered near the query radius) trigger
  the break condition; test the abort path.
\item Test points exactly at radii $r$ and $cr$; the guarantee is
  probabilistic, so verify empirically.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

Indyk and Motwani \cite{indyk1998} introduced LSH and its analysis; the
modern treatment and notation are in \cite{harpeled2011}.

\section{Well-Separated Pair Decomposition}
\label{sec:approx_wsd}

\subsection{1. Overview}

$n$-body simulation, $k$-nearest-neighbor graphs, and spanner construction
need information about all $n^2$ point pairs. Euclidean space admits a
linear-size \textbf{well-separated pair decomposition} (WSPD)\index{well-separated pair decomposition}:
$O(n)$ pairs of subsets, each far apart relative to its size, that together
cover every pair of points. Callahan and Kosaraju introduced the concept
\cite{callahan1995} and used it for $k$-nearest neighbors and $n$-body
potential fields; the construction is quadtree-based.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Well-Separated Pair]\label{def:approx_ws_pair}
  Non-empty sets $A, B \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ are \textbf{$s$-well-separated}
  for $s > 2$ if
  \[
    \max\{\operatorname{diam}(A), \operatorname{diam}(B)\}
    \leq s \cdot \operatorname{dist}(A, B),
  \]
  where $\operatorname{dist}(A, B) = \min_{a \in A, b \in B}\|a - b\|$. A
  \textbf{well-separated pair decomposition} of $P$ with parameter $s$ is a
  set of $s$-well-separated pairs of subsets of $P$ such that every unordered
  point pair of $P$ lies in exactly one pair.
\end{definition}

The contract: for any $a \in A$, $b \in B$, the distance $\|a - b\|$ is
within $1 + 2/s$ of $\operatorname{dist}(A, B)$, so smooth functions of the
distances can be evaluated once per pair.

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Size and Construction of WSPD]\label{thm:approx_wspd_size}
  For every $s > 2$ and every $n$-point set $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, a
  well-separated pair decomposition with parameter $s$ containing
  $O(s^d n)$ pairs exists and is constructed in $O(s^d n \log n)$ time
  \cite{callahan1995}.
\end{theorem}

The construction runs on the \textbf{split tree}, a compressed quadtree
(Section~\ref{sec:quadtree}, Theorem~\ref{thm:qt_subdivision}) whose nodes
may split into children whose cells are pairwise separated. The recursion
\Call{Wspd}{u, v} stops when the two node sets are $s$-well-separated and
otherwise descends into the larger node. Separation forces each recursive
thread to visit $O(1)$ nodes (packing bound), giving $O(s^d n)$ pairs.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Well-Separated Pair Decomposition from a Split Tree}
\label{alg:approx_wspd_build}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A split tree for $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ with root $T$; separation parameter $s > 2$.
\Ensure A set $\mathcal{D}$ of $s$-well-separated pairs covering all pairs of $P$.
\Function{Wspd}{$u$, $v$}
  \If{$u$, $v$ are $s$-well-separated} \State add $(P_u, P_v)$ to $\mathcal{D}$
  \ElsIf{$u$ and $v$ are both leaves} \State add $(P_u, P_v)$ to $\mathcal{D}$
  \ElsIf{$\operatorname{radius}(u) \geq \operatorname{radius}(v)$}
    \For{each child $u'$ of $u$} \State \Call{Wspd}{u', v} \EndFor
  \Else \For{each child $v'$ of $v$} \State \Call{Wspd}{u, v'} \EndFor \EndIf
\EndFunction
\State $\mathcal{D} \gets \emptyset$
\For{each pair $(u, v)$ of children of the root} \State \Call{Wspd}{u, v} \EndFor
\State \Return $\mathcal{D}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$s$}: larger $s$ improves the approximation ($2/s$ error) but
  increases the pair count ($O(s^d n)$); $s = 4$ is the canonical choice.
\item \textbf{Granularity}: single-point leaves keep the recursion shallow;
  skip long chains of degree-one cells.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(s^d n \log n)$; \textbf{size}: $O(s^d n)$ pairs --
  linear in $n$ for constant $s, d$, replacing the quadratic all-pairs scan.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$n$-body problems}: evaluate potentials per well-separated
  pair \cite{callahan1995}.
\item \textbf{$k$-nearest neighbors}: report approximate nearest pairs
  (cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:closest_pair}).
\item \textbf{Spanners}: the application of
  Section~\ref{sec:approx_distance_queries}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item Verify that every point pair appears in exactly one pair of
  $\mathcal{D}$, and spot-check the separation inequality.
\item Clustered and collinear inputs stress the separation test; verify
  termination.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

Callahan and Kosaraju \cite{callahan1995} introduced the WSPD; the quadtree
machinery is in Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_structures} and the spanner
applications in \cite{harpeled2011}.

\index{well-separated pair decomposition}\index{split tree}

\section{Approximate Distance Queries}
\label{sec:approx_distance_queries}

\begin{definition}[Geometric Spanner]\label{def:approx_spanner}
  A graph $G = (P, E)$ is a \textbf{$t$-spanner} of point set $P$ if for all
  $p, q \in P$ the shortest-path length satisfies $d_G(p, q) \leq t \|p - q\|$;
  $t \geq 1$ is the \textbf{stretch factor}\index{stretch factor}. A spanner
  replaces the complete Euclidean graph (quadratic) with $O(n)$ edges that
  preserve all distances up to $t$.
\end{definition}

Spanners are the data structure for \textbf{approximate distance
queries}\index{distance oracle}: preprocess the points once, then answer any
pairwise distance by a shortest-path computation on the small graph.

\begin{theorem}[WSPD Spanner]\label{thm:approx_spanner_stretch}
  Connecting each pair of a well-separated pair decomposition with parameter
  $s \geq 4$ by one edge between arbitrary representatives yields a graph
  with $O(s^d n)$ edges that is a $t$-spanner for
  $t = \frac{s + 4}{s - 4}$ ($t \leq 3$ for $s = 8$)
  \cite{callahan1995,harpeled2011}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  For a point pair $(p, q)$ covered by the well-separated pair
  $(A_i, B_i)$ with representatives $a_i, b_i$, $s$-separation bounds
  $\|p - a_i\|$, $\|a_i - b_i\|$, and $\|b_i - q\|$ each by $O(1/s)\|p - q\|$,
  so the path $p \to a_i \to b_i \to q$ has length at most
  $\frac{s+4}{s-4}\|p - q\|$; the triangle inequality is used once, which is
  why the constant is explicit.
\end{proof}

Once a $t$-spanner is available, approximate shortest paths follow from
Dijkstra's algorithm (Chapter~\ref{ch:distance_paths},
Section~\ref{sec:dijkstra}) in $O(n \log n)$ per query, or from an
\emph{approximate distance oracle} answering in $O(1)$. Refinements require
\emph{bounded-degree} and \emph{light} spanners (degree and total edge
weight constant times the MST weight) while keeping constant stretch; the
classical theory is surveyed in \cite{harpeled2011}.

\index{spanner}

\section{Geometric Clustering}
\label{sec:approx_clustering}

\textbf{Clustering}\index{clustering} partitions a point set into $k$
groups with similar members. The geometric setting makes similarity a
distance objective, and the objective determines both the algorithm and its
guarantee. The three classical objectives are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{$k$-center}: cover $P$ by $k$ balls of minimum radius;
  \item \textbf{$k$-median}: minimize the sum of point-to-nearest-center
    distances;
  \item \textbf{$k$-means}: minimize the sum of \emph{squared}
    point-to-center distances.
\end{enumerate}
All three are NP-hard, even in the plane \cite{gonzalez1985}, and all three
are workhorses of data analysis, so approximation is essential. Formal
definitions and algorithms are in Section~\ref{sec:approx_k_center_median}.

The objectives differ in their treatment of outliers and cluster sizes.
$k$-center is the most conservative: a single far point sets the radius, so
it is robust to cluster shape but sensitive to noise. $k$-median averages,
so a small well-separated cluster hardly moves the optimum. $k$-means
squares, amplifying large clusters and aligning with variance-based models;
it is the objective of Lloyd's algorithm
(Section~\ref{sec:approx_k_center_median}) that dominates practice
(Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry_data_ml}, Section~\ref{sec:data_ml_clustering}).

The algorithmic landscape mirrors the objectives. For $k$-center a greedy
farthest-point rule achieves the tight factor $2$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_gonzalez_bound}). For $k$-median and $k$-means,
\emph{Lloyd-style} iteration converges to a local optimum, and core-sets
(Section~\ref{sec:approx_coresets}) reduce the input to
$O(k \varepsilon^{-d} \log n)$ points, yielding $(1 + \varepsilon)$-approximations
linear in $n$ for fixed $k, d, \varepsilon$ \cite{harpeled2004}. Clustering
is thus the cleanest demonstration that the three tools of this chapter --
sampling, core-sets, and greedy/local search -- compose into end-to-end
guarantees. The number $k$ of clusters is part of the input; the guarantees
hold for every $k$, and the application chooses it.

\index{clustering}\index{k-center}\index{k-median}\index{k-means}

\section{\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k}-Center and \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k}-Median Problems}
\label{sec:approx_k_center_median}

\subsection{1. Overview}

This section develops approximations for the objectives of
Section~\ref{sec:approx_clustering}. The centerpiece is the farthest-point
algorithm of Gonzalez \cite{gonzalez1985}, which achieves the optimal factor
$2$ for $k$-center. For $k$-median and $k$-means, a constant factor below
that style of guarantee is impossible, but Lloyd-style iteration
\cite{lloyd1982} plus core-sets deliver $(1 + \varepsilon)$-approximations
in fixed dimension \cite{harpeled2004}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[$k$-Center]\label{def:approx_kcenter}
  Given $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and $k$, find $k$ centers $C$ minimizing the
  \textbf{radius} $\max_{p \in P}\operatorname{dist}(p, C)$, where
  $\operatorname{dist}(p, C) = \min_{c \in C}\|p - c\|$; the optimum is $r^*$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$k$-Median and $k$-Means]\label{def:approx_kmedian}
  The \textbf{$k$-median} problem minimizes
  $\sum_{p \in P}\operatorname{dist}(p, C)$ over all $k$-point center sets
  $C$; \textbf{$k$-means} minimizes $\sum_{p \in P}\operatorname{dist}(p, C)^2$.
  Both are NP-hard \cite{gonzalez1985}.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

The farthest-point rule picks the first center arbitrarily and each further
center as the point of $P$ farthest from the current centers.

\begin{theorem}[Gonzalez's $2$-Approximation]\label{thm:approx_gonzalez_bound}
  The farthest-point algorithm
  (Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_gonzalez_kcenter}) computes $k$ centers of
  radius at most $2 r^*$ in $O(nk)$ time. No polynomial-time algorithm
  achieves ratio below $2$ unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$
  \cite{gonzalez1985}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  If the returned radius exceeded $2 r^*$, the farthest point would be at
  distance more than $2 r^*$ from all chosen centers, so by induction the
  algorithm would have found $k + 1$ points with mutual distances exceeding
  $2 r^*$. But the optimal solution covers $P$ with $k$ balls of radius
  $r^*$, each of diameter at most $2 r^*$ by the triangle inequality, so two
  such points cannot share a ball: they need $k + 1$ balls, a contradiction.
  The lower bound reduces planar dominating set, which is NP-complete; see
  \cite{gonzalez1985}.
\end{proof}

For $k$-median and $k$-means, Lloyd's algorithm
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_lloyd}) alternates assigning points to the
nearest center and moving each center to the centroid of its assigned
points.

\begin{theorem}[Monotonicity of Lloyd's Algorithm]\label{thm:approx_lloyd_monotone}
  Every iteration of Lloyd's algorithm
  (Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_lloyd}) does not increase the $k$-means
  objective, and the center sequence converges to a fixed point that can be
  arbitrarily worse than the global optimum \cite{lloyd1982}.
\end{theorem}

Core-sets upgrade the heuristic into an approximation: reducing to
$O(k \varepsilon^{-d} \log n)$ weighted points and searching over candidate
centers gives a $(1 + \varepsilon)$-approximation in time linear in $n$ for
fixed $k, d, \varepsilon$ \cite{harpeled2004}. In practice the best of both
worlds is achieved by seeding Lloyd's algorithm with the Gonzalez centers.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Farthest-Point $k$-Center (Gonzalez)}
\label{alg:approx_gonzalez_kcenter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Point set $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$; integer $k \geq 1$.
\Ensure A set $C$ of at most $k$ centers with radius at most $2 r^*$.
\Function{GonzalezKCenter}{$P$, $k$}
  \State pick arbitrary $p_0 \in P$; $C \gets \{p_0\}$
  \For{$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $k - 1$}
    \State let $p$ be a point of $P$ maximizing $\min_{c \in C}\|p - c\|$
    \State $C \gets C \cup \{p\}$
  \EndFor
  \State assign each $p \in P$ to its nearest center in $C$
  \State \Return $C$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Lloyd's Algorithm ($k$-Means Heuristic)}
\label{alg:approx_lloyd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Point set $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$; integer $k \geq 1$; initial centers $C$.
\Ensure A locally optimal set of $k$ centers for the $k$-means objective.
\Function{Lloyd}{$P$, $C$}
  \Repeat
    \State $C' \gets C$
    \For{each $p \in P$} assign $p$ to the nearest center of $C'$ \EndFor
    \For{each non-empty cluster $P_i$} $c_i \gets \frac{1}{|P_i|}\sum_{p \in P_i} p$ \EndFor
  \Until{no center changes}
  \State \Return $C$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$k$}: fixed by the application
  (Section~\ref{sec:approx_clustering}); the guarantees hold for all $k$.
\item \textbf{Seeding}: Gonzalez centers make good seeds for
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_lloyd}; randomized seeding rules reduce the
  dependence on the first arbitrary center.
\item \textbf{Termination}: Lloyd can cycle in degenerate cases; fix a
  tolerance and a maximum iteration count.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gonzalez}: $O(nk)$ time, $O(n)$ space.
\item \textbf{Lloyd}: $O(nkt)$ for $t$ iterations; $t$ is not polynomially
  bounded in the worst case but is small in practice.
\item \textbf{Core-set schemes}: $O(n)$ time plus a term exponential in
  $k$, $d$, and $1/\varepsilon$ \cite{harpeled2004}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Facility location and quantization}: $k$-center models
  emergency-response placement; $k$-means is the Lloyd--Max quantizer of
  signal processing \cite{lloyd1982}.
\item \textbf{Data analysis}: the default clustering engine of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry_data_ml} (Section~\ref{sec:data_ml_clustering}).
\item \textbf{Preprocessing}: Gonzalez centers double as metric nets and
  coarse summaries in many pipelines.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item Compare the Gonzalez radius with the optimum (exact for small $n$) and
  verify the factor $2$.
\item Lloyd can empty clusters; fix a re-seeding convention. Test
  $k \geq n$ and coincident points as defined cases.
\item Verify monotone objective decrease at every Lloyd iteration
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_lloyd_monotone}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

Gonzalez's farthest-point algorithm \cite{gonzalez1985}, Lloyd's
quantization algorithm \cite{lloyd1982}, and the core-set based
$(1 + \varepsilon)$-approximations of Har-Peled and Mazumdar
\cite{harpeled2004}; the textbook treatment is \cite{deberg2008,harpeled2011}.

\section{Minimum Enclosing Shapes}
\label{sec:approx_enclosing}

\begin{definition}[Minimum Enclosing Ball]\label{def:approx_meb}
  The \textbf{minimum enclosing ball} (MEB) of $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ is
  the ball of minimum radius containing $P$; in the plane it is the smallest
  enclosing circle (SEC) of Definition~\ref{def:sec}
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:enclosure_convex_distance}). Its radius is the
  $1$-center value $r^*$.
\end{definition}

The MEB is the $k = 1$ case of $k$-center
(Definition~\ref{def:approx_kcenter}), and it is exceptional: it can be
solved exactly in expected linear time by Welzl's randomized incremental
algorithm (Chapter~\ref{ch:enclosure_convex_distance},
Section~\ref{sec:welzls_algorithm}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:welzl},
Theorem~\ref{thm:welzl_linear}) \cite{welzl1991,megiddo1983}. The catch is
dimension: the exact algorithm's constant grows exponentially in $d$
(the ball has up to $d + 1$ support points), so beyond a few dozen
dimensions the problem is solved \emph{approximately} by the core-sets of
Section~\ref{sec:approx_coresets}, whose size is independent of $n$ and $d$.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Core-Set Based $(1 + \varepsilon)$-Approximate MEB}
\label{alg:approx_meb_coreset}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Point set $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$; error $\varepsilon > 0$.
\Ensure Center $c$ and radius $r$ with $P \subseteq B(c, (1 + \varepsilon) r)$ and $r \leq (1 + \varepsilon) r^*$.
\Function{MEBCoreset}{$P$, $\varepsilon$}
  \State pick arbitrary $c_1 \in P$; $S \gets \emptyset$; $t \gets 1$
  \While{$P \nsubseteq B(c_t, (1 + \varepsilon) r_t)$}
    \State let $p_t$ be the point of $P$ farthest from $c_t$; $r_t \gets \|p_t - c_t\|$
    \State $S \gets S \cup \{p_t\}$
    \State $(c_{t+1}, r_{t+1}) \gets$ exact MEB of $S$ \Comment{via Welzl; $|S| \leq 2/\varepsilon$}
    \State $t \gets t + 1$
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $(c_t, r_t)$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The loop is the farthest-point expansion of
Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_meb_coreset_bound}: each round adds the point that
best witnesses the current error and recomputes the exact ball on the small
sample (cheap, since $|S| \leq \lceil 2/\varepsilon \rceil$), terminating
once the inflated sample ball contains $P$. Termination is geometric: while
the loop continues, every added point grows the sample ball's radius by a
constant factor, so only $O(1/\varepsilon)$ points are ever added. Badoiu
and Clarkson proved the size and time bounds \cite{badoiu2002}; Kumar,
Mitchell, and Yildirim sharpened the running time to linear in $n$ and $d$
for fixed $\varepsilon$ \cite{kumar2003}.

\begin{remark}
  The algorithm exploits the support-point theorem behind Welzl's exact
  algorithm: the optimal ball is determined by at most $d + 1$ points on its
  boundary. Approximating the MEB is thus a case study in how an exact
  structural theorem converts into an approximation algorithm with mild
  loss.
\end{remark}

\index{minimum enclosing ball}\index{smallest enclosing circle}

\section{High-Dimensional Problems}
\label{sec:approx_high_dimensional}

In high dimensions the low-dimensional structures of this chapter fail in
the worst case: quadtree methods carry factors $c^d$ or $s^d$, and the BBD
tree's $O(c^d \log n)$ query time is exponential in $d$. The curse of
dimensionality (Section~\ref{sec:highdim_curse}) is real. Three ideas
survive it, and they are the three pillars built here:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Dimension reduction}: the Johnson--Lindenstrauss lemma
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:high_dimensional}, Section~\ref{sec:highdim_jl})
    embeds $n$ points into $O(\log n)$ dimensions while preserving distances
    within $1 + \varepsilon$, and its random projections are the same ones
    that power LSH (Section~\ref{sec:approx_lsh}).
  \item \textbf{Core-sets}: the MEB admits core-sets whose size is
    independent of $d$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_meb_coreset_bound}), so
    Section~\ref{sec:approx_enclosing} runs in time linear in $d$. The
    solution can be summarized without the dimension, even though no space
    decomposition can tame $\mathbb{R}^d$; the discrepancy bounds of
    \cite{matousek1999} supply the matching sampling theory.
  \item \textbf{Sublinear query structures}: LSH answers approximate
    nearest-neighbor queries in time $n^{\rho}$ for $\rho < 1$ independent
    of $d$, and the pipeline of
    Section~\ref{sec:highdim_dimensionality_reduction} feeds reduced data
    into Section~\ref{sec:approx_ann};
    Section~\ref{sec:highdim_nearest_neighbor} surveys the interplay.
\end{enumerate}

The lesson is a hierarchy. Problems with geometric structure (balls,
halfspaces) have dimension-free core-set guarantees; problems with metric
structure (nearest neighbors, distance queries) have sublinear-time
solutions via projections and hashing; and purely combinatorial problems
(covering, packing; Chapter~\ref{ch:packing_algorithms}) let the dimension
enter the approximation ratio itself. The practical high-dimensional
systems -- the navigable small-world graphs of \cite{malkov2018} -- blend
these ideas with engineering and remain heuristic; the theory of this
chapter is the vocabulary in which their guarantees are stated.

\index{curse of dimensionality}\index{dimension reduction}

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:approx_exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Prove Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_gonzalez_bound} in full: show the
    chosen $k$ centers have pairwise distances exceeding the returned radius
    if it exceeds $2 r^*$, and conclude by the triangle inequality that the
    optimal balls cannot separate them.
  \item Argue that no polynomial-time algorithm approximates $k$-center
    within a factor below $2$: show a $(2 - \delta)$-approximation would
    decide the planar dominating-set problem exactly (see \cite{gonzalez1985}).
  \item Exhibit a range space (points on a line, intervals as ranges) where
    an $\varepsilon$-net of size $\Omega(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
    \log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})$ is necessary, and describe the worst-case
    configuration.
  \item Show that an $\varepsilon$-approximation is an $\varepsilon$-net:
    prove that if $A$ is an $\varepsilon$-approximation of $(X,
    \mathcal{R})$, then every range with $|R| \geq \varepsilon |X|$ contains
    a point of $A$.
  \item For Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_meb_coreset}, prove that while the
    loop continues, the sample radius grows by a constant factor per round,
    and conclude $|S| \leq \lceil 2/\varepsilon \rceil$ (the bound of
    Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_meb_coreset_bound}).
  \item Derive the stretch bound of
    Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_spanner_stretch}: for an $s$-well-separated pair
    with $s \geq 4$, bound $\|p - a_i\|$, $\|a_i - b_i\|$, and
    $\|b_i - q\|$ in terms of $\|p - q\|$ and assemble the path
    $p \to a_i \to b_i \to q$ to get $(s + 4)/(s - 4)$.
  \item Implement Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_ann_quadtree} and
    Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_lsh} on the same random point sets; plot the
    empirical approximation factor and query time against $d = 2, 4, 8, 16$
    and identify where LSH overtakes the tree.
  \item Use a random sample of size $m = O(1/\varepsilon^2)$
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:approx_eps_approx_bound}) to estimate the fraction of
    $n$ points in the unit square inside a fixed disk; compare the empirical
    error with the bound of Definition~\ref{def:approx_eps_net} over $1000$
    trials.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Project: Approximate Nearest-Neighbor Search}
\label{sec:approx_project}

Build a practical approximate nearest-neighbor search system and compare the
guarantees of this chapter with engineering reality.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Input}: generate random point sets
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:sampling}, Section~\ref{sec:uniform_random_points}) in
    dimensions $d = 2, 4, 8, 16$ with $n = 10^4$ to $10^6$, and load a real
    feature-vector data set if available.
  \item \textbf{Exact baseline}: implement brute-force nearest neighbors to
    compute ground truth for $100$ queries; this is the yardstick for ratio
    and recall.
  \item \textbf{BBD tree}: implement the balanced box decomposition and the
    $c$-approximate query of Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_ann_quadtree}; verify
    the ratio bound on every query.
  \item \textbf{LSH}: implement Algorithm~\ref{alg:approx_lsh} with random
    hyperplane projections; tune $k$ and $L$ and record the
    recall-versus-query-time curve.
  \item \textbf{Comparison}: for each dimension report average
    approximation factor, 95th percentile, recall, and query time for both
    structures, and locate the crossover dimension.
  \item \textbf{Engineering}: compare against a practical library (e.g.,
    the navigable small-world graphs of \cite{malkov2018}) on the
    high-dimensional data set.
  \item \textbf{Verification}: plot the empirical tradeoff against
    Theorems~\ref{thm:approx_ann_complexity} and \ref{thm:approx_lsh_quality},
    and explain discrepancies in terms of $c$, $k$, $L$, and bucket size.
\end{enumerate}
The project exercises the sampling, space-decomposition, and hashing
machinery of this chapter and of Chapters~\ref{ch:spatial_structures} and
\ref{ch:high_dimensional}, and it surfaces the gap between worst-case
guarantees and practical behavior that motivates the field.
