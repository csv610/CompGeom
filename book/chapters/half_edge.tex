\section{Half-Edge Representation}
\label{sec:halfedge_representation}

Almost every algorithm in this book operates on geometry that is
combinatorial as well as numeric: a planar subdivision, a polygon with
holes, or a polyhedral surface is a collection of vertices joined by edges
into faces, and the algorithms repeatedly ask \emph{adjacency questions}
--- which face lies across this edge, which edges bound this face, which
edges meet at this vertex. The simplest representation, the
\index{indexed face set}\textbf{indexed face set} (a list of vertex
coordinates plus, for each face, the indices of its vertices), answers none
of these questions directly: finding the face on the other side of an edge
requires scanning every face, and walking around a vertex requires
reassembling the incident edges from all faces that mention the vertex. An
indexed face set is a good \emph{input format}; it is a poor
\emph{working structure} for algorithms that traverse the mesh.

\begin{definition}[Planar Subdivision]\label{def:hed_subdivision}
A \textbf{planar subdivision} is a partition of a connected region of the
plane (or of the entire plane) into vertices, edges, and faces by a
straight-line planar embedding of a planar graph: edges meet only at
vertices, and each bounded face is a polygonal region bounded by a cycle of
edges. A \textbf{polyhedral surface} is the analogous 2-manifold embedded
in $\mathbb{R}^3$, built from planar polygonal faces.
\end{definition}

The \index{doubly connected edge list}\textbf{doubly connected edge list}
(DCEL), introduced in the planar-subdivision chapter of de Berg et al.
\cite{deberg2008}, and the \index{half-edge}\textbf{half-edge} structure of
the polygon-mesh literature \cite{botsch2010} are the same object under two
names. The central idea is to abandon the undirected edge and store, for
every edge, two directed \textbf{half-edges}\index{half-edge}, one for each
side of the edge. Each half-edge owns a pointer to the face on its side and
to the half-edge that precedes and follows it around that face. With this
single change, every adjacency query becomes either $O(1)$ or a short walk,
and local topological edits --- splitting an edge, inserting a vertex,
merging two faces --- become $O(1)$ pointer surgery rather than global
reconstruction.

The half-edge structure appears throughout this book. It is the substrate
on which polygon booleans (Chapter~\ref{ch:polygon_ops}), mesh-analysis
validity checks (Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}), Delaunay edge flips and
insertion legalization (Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}), walking
point location (Chapter~\ref{ch:point_location}), and the topological
counting of Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology} are built. This chapter
develops the representation itself: its elements
(Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_elements}), its operators
(Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_operators}), its treatment of boundaries and
manifolds (Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_boundary}), the adjacency and
traversal queries it supports (Sections~\ref{sec:halfedge_adjacency} and
\ref{sec:halfedge_traversal}), its relation to the winged-edge, quad-edge,
and half-face structures (Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_comparison}), its
storage cost (Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_complexity}), its construction from
unstructured input (Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_construction}), and its role
in mesh-processing applications (Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_applications}).

\section{Vertices, Half-Edges, and Faces}
\label{sec:halfedge_elements}

\begin{definition}[Half-Edge]\label{def:hed_halfedge}
A \textbf{half-edge} is a directed edge of the subdivision. It records a
start vertex (its \emph{origin}), the face on its left (its \emph{incident
face}), and references to the next and previous half-edges in the boundary
cycle of that face. Each undirected edge of the subdivision is realized by
exactly two half-edges, one for each of its two sides.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[DCEL / Half-Edge Structure]\label{def:hed_dcel}
A \textbf{doubly connected edge list} (equivalently a \textbf{half-edge
structure}) over a planar subdivision or polyhedral surface consists of:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a set of \textbf{vertices}, each storing coordinates and one
    incident half-edge;
  \item a set of \textbf{half-edges}, each storing an origin vertex, a twin
    half-edge, a next half-edge, a prev half-edge, and an incident face;
  \item a set of \textbf{faces}, each storing one boundary half-edge and,
    for a face with holes, the extra boundary cycles.
\end{itemize}
The half-edges of a face are linked by \texttt{next} into one closed cycle
per boundary component.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Boundary Cycle]\label{def:hed_boundary_cycle}
The \textbf{boundary cycle} of a face $f$ is the cyclic sequence of
half-edges reached by repeatedly applying \texttt{next}, starting from any
half-edge of $f$. For a face without holes this is a single cycle; the
length of the cycle is the \textbf{face degree} of $f$, written $|f|$.
\end{definition}

The orientation convention fixes the geometry-to-topology link: in the
plane, half-edges run counterclockwise around bounded faces and clockwise
around the unbounded outer face, so that the interior of every face lies
consistently to the left (or right) of its directed boundary. On a closed
polyhedral surface the same rule orients every face so that the outward
normal is consistently on one side (see Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_boundary}
and Chapter~\ref{ch:polyhedral_geometry}).

The half-edge data is redundant, and the redundancy is the structure's
invariant: it can be checked after every edit, exactly as the adjacency
invariants of a graph can be checked via the Handshaking Lemma
\cite{diestel2017}.

\begin{theorem}[Half-Edge Incidence Identity]\label{thm:hed_incidence}
Let $H$ be the number of half-edges of a subdivision with $E$ edges,
$V$ vertices, and faces $F$ (including the unbounded face in the planar
case). Then
\[
H = 2E = \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = \sum_{f \in F} |f| ,
\]
where $\deg(v)$ counts the number of half-edges whose origin is $v$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each undirected edge is realized by exactly two half-edges, one per side,
so $H = 2E$. Each half-edge has exactly one origin vertex, so summing the
incident-half-edge count over all vertices counts every half-edge exactly
once: $H = \sum_v \deg(v)$. Similarly each half-edge is traversed by exactly
one face (it lies on the boundary of its incident face), and the face
degrees count exactly the half-edges of that face, so $H = \sum_f |f|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A Single Triangle]\label{ex:hed_triangle}
The subdivision of a triangle has $V = 3$ vertices, $E = 3$ edges, and
$F = 2$ faces (the bounded triangle and the unbounded outer face). Its
$H = 2E = 6$ half-edges split into three pairs: the three CCW half-edges
$(v_0, v_1), (v_1, v_2), (v_2, v_0)$ bound the triangle, and their twins
bound the outer face clockwise. Hence $\sum_v \deg(v) = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6$ and
$\sum_f |f| = 3 + 3 = 6$, matching Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_incidence}.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
For a closed polyhedral surface (a sphere-like mesh, no boundary), every
edge has two sides and $H = 2E$ still holds, but there is no unbounded
face. The incidence identity is dimension-independent: it is the
combinatorial core of the Euler-characteristic machinery of
Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}.
\end{remark}

\section{Twin, Next, and Prev Operators}
\label{sec:halfedge_operators}

The three core operators are what make the half-edge structure a
\emph{navigational} structure rather than a static list.

\begin{definition}[Twin Operator]\label{def:hed_twin}
The \textbf{twin} operator $\operatorname{twin}(h)$ returns the half-edge
of the same undirected edge that runs in the opposite direction and lies on
the other side of the edge. It is an involution: $\operatorname{twin}(h) =
h'$ if and only if $\operatorname{twin}(h') = h$, and
$\operatorname{twin}(\operatorname{twin}(h)) = h$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Next and Prev Operators]\label{def:hed_next_prev}
The \textbf{next} operator $\operatorname{next}(h)$ returns the half-edge
that follows $h$ in the boundary cycle of its face, and the \textbf{prev}
operator $\operatorname{prev}(h)$ returns the half-edge that precedes it.
Both are defined within a face: $\operatorname{next}$ and
$\operatorname{prev}$ are inverse to each other on the same face cycle.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Inverse on Face Cycles]\label{lem:hed_inverse}
For every half-edge $h$,
$\operatorname{prev}(h) = \operatorname{next}^{-1}(h)$; in particular
$\operatorname{next}(\operatorname{prev}(h)) = h$ and
$\operatorname{prev}(\operatorname{next}(h)) = h$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By definition both $\operatorname{next}$ and $\operatorname{prev}$ restrict
to cyclic successor and predecessor permutations of the same face boundary
cycle, and the successor permutation's inverse is the predecessor
permutation.
\end{proof}

Two derived queries are used constantly and are worth naming. The
\index{origin vertex}\textbf{origin} function $\operatorname{org}(h)$
returns the start vertex of $h$; the \index{target vertex}\textbf{target}
is $\operatorname{org}(\operatorname{twin}(h))$, the vertex the half-edge
points to. The \index{incident face}\textbf{incident face}
$\operatorname{face}(h)$ returns the face whose boundary contains $h$. Every
adjacency primitive in Sections~\ref{sec:halfedge_adjacency} and
\ref{sec:halfedge_traversal} is a short composition of
$\operatorname{twin}$, $\operatorname{next}$, $\operatorname{prev}$, and the
origin/face accessors.

The two compositions that matter most are those that cross between the
\emph{face} world and the \emph{vertex} world. A face cycle is traced by
iterating $\operatorname{next}$; a \index{vertex star}\textbf{vertex star}
--- the set of faces or edges incident to a vertex --- is traced by the
composition $\operatorname{next} \circ \operatorname{twin}$, which walks
from one incident face of $v$ to the next one around $v$.

\begin{theorem}[Cycle Structure]\label{thm:hed_cycles}
The $\operatorname{next}$ cycles partition the half-edges, one cycle for
each boundary component of each face. The cycles of
$\operatorname{next} \circ \operatorname{twin}$ partition the half-edges
around vertices: each cycle visits exactly the half-edges whose origin is
a fixed vertex, once each.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every half-edge has exactly one $\operatorname{next}$ and one
$\operatorname{prev}$, and the two are inverses
(Lemma~\ref{lem:hed_inverse}), so the functional graph of
$\operatorname{next}$ decomposes into disjoint cycles; since each half-edge
lies on exactly one face boundary, these are the face cycles. For the
second claim, fix $v$ and consider the map $h \mapsto
\operatorname{next}(\operatorname{twin}(h))$ on the set $S_v$ of half-edges
with origin $v$. If $h$ has origin $v$, then $\operatorname{twin}(h)$ has
origin $\operatorname{org}(h)$'s neighbor $w$, and
$\operatorname{next}(\operatorname{twin}(h))$ is the half-edge of the next
face around $v$ that shares $v$; its origin is again $v$, so the map sends
$S_v$ into itself, and it is a bijection on the finite set $S_v$, hence a
permutation whose cycles visit every half-edge of $S_v$. Distinct vertices
have disjoint origin sets, so the cycles partition all half-edges.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Guibas and Stolfi \cite{guibas1985} formalized this algebra for their
\index{quad-edge}\textbf{quad-edge} structure, in which the operators
form a group algebra on each geometric edge; the half-edge structure is
exactly the restriction of the quad-edge algebra to the primal side of a
subdivision. We return to this comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_comparison}.
\end{remark}

\section{Boundary and Manifold Handling}
\label{sec:halfedge_boundary}

Most real meshes --- a scanned surface, a finite-element domain, a terrain
--- are not closed polyhedra: they have a \index{boundary}boundary. The
half-edge structure must represent boundaries explicitly, because an edge
that is incident to only one face has no second half-edge to pair with.

\begin{definition}[Boundary Edge and Boundary Half-Edge]\label{def:hed_boundary_edge}
A \textbf{boundary edge} is an edge incident to exactly one face. The
half-edge of a boundary edge that belongs to the incident face is a
\textbf{boundary half-edge}; it has no twin among the half-edges of the
other side, because no face exists there.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Manifold Edge and Manifold Vertex]\label{def:hed_manifold}
An edge is \textbf{edge-manifold} if it is incident to at most two faces; a
vertex is \textbf{vertex-manifold} if its incident faces form a single
connected fan (a topological disk, or half-disk at the boundary) rather
than two or more cones meeting only at the vertex. A mesh is a
\index{manifold mesh}\textbf{manifold mesh} if every edge and vertex is
manifold. Non-manifold edges (three or more incident faces) cannot be
represented faithfully by a structure in which every edge has exactly two
sides.
\end{definition}

The boundary behavior is forced by the twin involution:

\begin{theorem}[Twin Boundary Dichotomy]\label{thm:hed_twin_boundary}
Let $h$ be a half-edge of a manifold mesh. If $h$ is an interior half-edge,
then $\operatorname{twin}(h)$ is a distinct half-edge belonging to the
unique other face on the edge. If $h$ is a boundary half-edge, then no such
half-edge exists, and implementations either leave
$\operatorname{twin}(h)$ null or link it to a synthetic
\index{dummy half-edge}\textbf{dummy boundary half-edge}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By edge-manifoldness each edge has at most two incident faces. An interior
half-edge's edge has exactly two incident faces, so it has exactly one
partner half-edge on the other side, which is its twin. A boundary
half-edge's edge has exactly one incident face, so no partner exists.
\end{proof}

Boundary half-edges are not isolated: they chain together.

\begin{theorem}[Boundary Cycles]\label{thm:hed_boundary_loops}
In a manifold mesh with boundary, the boundary half-edges that are not
paired (i.e., those with null or dummy twins) decompose into a unique set
of disjoint closed chains, each oriented consistently, one chain per
\index{boundary component}\textbf{boundary component} of the mesh.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each boundary half-edge has exactly one $\operatorname{next}$, which is
again a boundary half-edge: walking forward along the boundary keeps the
single incident face on one side, and the successor half-edge of that face
can only leave the boundary when the boundary makes a closed turn, which by
definition continues the chain. Since there are finitely many boundary
half-edges and every boundary vertex has exactly one incoming and one
outgoing boundary half-edge (vertex-manifoldness), the chains are disjoint
cycles. Each cycle bounds one connected component of the boundary, and
distinct cycles cannot belong to the same boundary component because the
components are disjoint sets of vertices and edges.
\end{proof}

The boundary cycle count $b$ enters the \index{Euler characteristic}Euler
characteristic, the fundamental numerical invariant of a subdivision
(Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_euler} in Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_complexity}). It
also drives the boundary-detection routines of
Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}: with a half-edge structure, an edge is a
boundary edge exactly when its twin is null (or dummy), a test that costs
$O(1)$ and needs no hashing.

\begin{example}[Square with a Hole]\label{ex:hed_boundary}
A square region with a square hole is a subdivision with two boundary
components --- the outer perimeter and the hole's perimeter --- so $b = 2$,
and every edge on either cycle is a boundary edge by
Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_boundary_loops}. The region's single face is bounded
by two cycles: its outer boundary runs counterclockwise and the hole's
boundary runs clockwise, so the face interior lies consistently on one side
of both; the structure stores both cycles in the face record
(Definition~\ref{def:hed_dcel}).
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Two degenerate situations defeat the manifold assumptions:
\index{T-junction}\textbf{T-junctions} (a vertex lying on the interior of
an edge of another face) and \index{pinched vertex}\textbf{pinched}
(non-manifold) vertices where two fans meet only at the vertex. Both must
be detected and repaired before a half-edge structure is built; the
verification algorithms of Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis} and the
manifold concepts of Section~\ref{sec:math_topology} provide the tests.
\end{remark}

\section{Adjacency and Neighborhood Queries}
\label{sec:halfedge_adjacency}

\subsection{1. Overview}

The half-edge structure exists to answer \index{adjacency query}adjacency
queries. This section catalogues the standard queries a mesh algorithm
asks, states their costs, and gives the pointer expressions that answer
them. All of them reduce to a constant number of operator applications plus
one cyclic walk, which is why mesh-analysis and mesh-editing algorithms are
built on this structure \cite{deberg2008,botsch2010}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Adjacency Query]\label{def:hed_adjacency_query}
An \textbf{adjacency query} takes one element of the subdivision (vertex,
edge, half-edge, or face) and returns a related element or a list of
related elements, such as ``the face across edge $e$'' or ``all edges
incident to vertex $v$''.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

The cost of a query is governed by Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_cycles}: constant
compositions of $\operatorname{twin}$, $\operatorname{next}$, and
$\operatorname{prev}$ step from one element to a unique neighbor in $O(1)$,
while enumeration queries walk an entire cycle and cost the cycle length.

\begin{theorem}[Query Complexity]\label{thm:hed_query}
Each of the single-element adjacency queries (face across an edge, face of
a half-edge, origin and target of a half-edge, twin, next, prev, boundary
test) costs $O(1)$. Each enumeration query (faces or edges around a vertex,
edges or vertices of a face, faces around a face) costs $O(1)$ per returned
element, hence $O(\deg(v))$ or $O(|f|)$ in total.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The single-element queries apply one or two of the operators defined in
Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_operators}, each an $O(1)$ pointer dereference;
the boundary test reads the twin field once. An enumeration query iterates
the relevant cycle of Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_cycles}, performing $O(1)$ work
per element and visiting each element of the star or face exactly once, for
$O(\deg(v))$ or $O(|f|)$ work.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

The neighborhood queries are one-line compositions of the operators; the
following is the canonical enumeration of the faces incident to a vertex,
using the cycle of Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_cycles}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Enumerate Faces Around a Vertex}
\label{alg:hed_neighbors}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A half-edge structure and a vertex $v$.
\Ensure The list of faces incident to $v$.
\Function{IncidentFaces}{$v$}
  \State $h \gets v.\textit{halfedge}$ \Comment{any half-edge with origin $v$}
  \State $h_0 \gets h$; $\mathit{faces} \gets [\,]$
  \Loop
    \State $\mathit{faces}.\text{append}(\operatorname{face}(h))$
    \State $h \gets \operatorname{twin}(\operatorname{prev}(h))$
    \State \textbf{if} $h = h_0$ \textbf{then break}
  \EndLoop
  \State \Return $\mathit{faces}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}
The step $h \leftarrow \operatorname{twin}(\operatorname{prev}(h))$ rotates
the half-edge to the next face around $v$; the equivalent step
$h \leftarrow \operatorname{next}(\operatorname{twin}(h))$ rotates in the
opposite direction. Both visit every incident face exactly once by
Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_cycles}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Origin pointer}: storing the origin vertex per half-edge
    makes $\operatorname{org}$ free; a variant that stores only the
    \emph{target} vertex changes the derived expressions but not their cost.
    Store whichever the dominant algorithms read most.
  \item \textbf{Null versus dummy twins}: a null twin halves the boundary
    test to one comparison but breaks code that assumes
    $\operatorname{twin}(h)$ is always valid; dummy boundary half-edges
    keep the twin involution total at the price of $2b$ extra half-edge
    records (one per boundary half-edge) and the explicit handling of the
    dummy's operators.
  \item \textbf{Prev pointer}: $\operatorname{prev}$ can be derived from
    $\operatorname{next}$ by storing the face cycles in circular arrays, but
    a direct pointer doubles the storage of the operator fields and halves
    the walking distance; most library implementations store both
    \cite{botsch2010,ericson2004}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-query time}: $O(1)$ for single-element queries,
    $O(\deg(v))$ or $O(|f|)$ for enumerations (Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_query}).
  \item \textbf{Space}: the operator fields dominate; see the detailed
    accounting in Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_complexity}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Point location by walking} (Chapter~\ref{ch:point_location})
    advances from triangle to triangle by repeatedly answering ``which face
    is across this edge?'' in $O(1)$.
  \item \textbf{Delaunay legalization} (Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations})
    tests an edge's two incident faces and relinks the four half-edges of a
    quadrilateral, both $O(1)$.
  \item \textbf{Mesh analysis} (Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}) computes
    valence, boundary loops, and orientability by enumerating the vertex
    stars and face cycles of this section.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Invariant audit}: after building any half-edge structure,
    verify $\operatorname{twin}(\operatorname{twin}(h)) = h$,
    $\operatorname{next}(\operatorname{prev}(h)) = h$, and the counts of
    Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_incidence}.
  \item \textbf{Star enumeration}: for every vertex, assert that
    IncidentFaces (Algorithm~\ref{alg:hed_neighbors}) returns each incident
    face exactly once and terminates.
  \item \textbf{Boundary vertices}: on an open disk, boundary vertices must
    yield the half-disk fan; the traversal must not leave the structure
    through a null twin.
  \item \textbf{Isolated and non-manifold cases}: isolated vertices have an
    empty star; non-manifold vertices break the single-cycle property and
    must be rejected at construction time
    (Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_construction}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

The DCEL treatment follows de Berg et al. \cite{deberg2008}; the pointer
expressions, the boundary conventions, and the storage trade-offs follow
Botsch et al. \cite{botsch2010} and the data-structure overview of Ericson
\cite{ericson2004}. The graph-theoretic counting arguments use
Diestel \cite{diestel2017} and the dictionary organization of
Cormen et al. \cite{cormen2009}.

\section{Traversal Operations}
\label{sec:halfedge_traversal}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Traversal is the algorithmic use of the navigation operators: walking the
boundary of a face, walking the star of a vertex, and walking a boundary
cycle. A traversal is any algorithm that visits the elements of a cycle of
Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_cycles} in order. Traversals are the loops inside the
mesh-analysis routines of Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis} --- valence
counting, boundary detection, orientability propagation --- and the
per-vertex stencils of mesh processing \cite{botsch2010}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Face Walk]\label{def:hed_face_walk}
A \textbf{face walk} from a half-edge $h$ visits the half-edges
$h, \operatorname{next}(h), \operatorname{next}^2(h), \dots$ until it
returns to $h$. It visits exactly the boundary cycle of
$\operatorname{face}(h)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Vertex Walk]\label{def:hed_vertex_walk}
A \textbf{vertex walk} from a half-edge $h$ with origin $v$ visits the
half-edges $h, \operatorname{next}(\operatorname{twin}(h)),
\operatorname{next}(\operatorname{twin}(\operatorname{next}(\operatorname{twin}(h)))), \dots$
until it returns to $h$; by Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_cycles} this visits every
half-edge with origin $v$ exactly once.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Cycle Cover]\label{thm:hed_cycle_cover}
Every half-edge is visited by exactly one face walk and exactly one vertex
walk, so the walks partition the half-edges into $\sum_f b_f$ face cycles
($b_f$ boundary components of face $f$) and $V$ vertex cycles.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
This is the combinatorial content of Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_cycles}
restated: the next-permutation decomposes the half-edges into the face
cycles, and the $\operatorname{next} \circ \operatorname{twin}$ permutation
decomposes them into the vertex cycles. Each half-edge appears in exactly
one cycle of each type.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Walk a Face Boundary}
\label{alg:hed_face_traversal}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A half-edge $h$.
\Ensure The half-edge sequence of the boundary cycle of $\operatorname{face}(h)$.
\Function{WalkFace}{$h$}
  \State $h_0 \gets h$; $\mathit{cycle} \gets [\,]$
  \Loop
    \State $\mathit{cycle}.\text{append}(h)$
    \State $h \gets \operatorname{next}(h)$
    \State \textbf{if} $h = h_0$ \textbf{then break}
  \EndLoop
  \State \Return $\mathit{cycle}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Walk Around a Vertex}
\label{alg:hed_vertex_traversal}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A half-edge $h$ and a flag $\mathit{emit\_faces}$.
\Ensure The incident faces (or incident edges) of $\operatorname{org}(h)$ in cyclic order.
\Function{WalkVertex}{$h$, $\mathit{emit\_faces}$}
  \State $h_0 \gets h$; $\mathit{cycle} \gets [\,]$
  \Loop
    \State append $\operatorname{face}(h)$ if $\mathit{emit\_faces}$, else append $h$
    \State $h \gets \operatorname{twin}(\operatorname{prev}(h))$
    \State \textbf{if} $h = h_0$ \textbf{then break}
  \EndLoop
  \State \Return $\mathit{cycle}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{remark}
To walk the boundary cycles of an open mesh, start at any unvisited
boundary half-edge (twin null or dummy) and apply
Algorithm~\ref{alg:hed_face_traversal}; the returned cycle is exactly one
boundary component (Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_boundary_loops}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Direction}: walking with $\operatorname{next}$ traverses a
    face in the stored orientation; the opposite orientation is obtained by
    swapping $\operatorname{next}$ and $\operatorname{prev}$.
  \item \textbf{Termination test}: comparing against the start half-edge is
    the only safe termination test; counting steps is a bug source when
    faces have varying degrees.
  \item \textbf{Boundary walk seeding}: boundary walks must be seeded from
    a boundary half-edge; seeds are cheaply found by keeping a single
    boundary half-edge per component (Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_boundary}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Face walk}: $O(|f|)$ steps, $O(1)$ per step.
  \item \textbf{Vertex walk}: $O(\deg(v))$ steps, $O(1)$ per step.
  \item \textbf{Full traversal} of all faces or all vertices: $O(H) =
    O(V + E)$, because the walks partition the half-edges
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_cycle_cover}) and each half-edge is touched $O(1)$
    times.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Valence and degree computation}: the vertex walk of
    Algorithm~\ref{alg:hed_vertex_traversal} implements the degree loop of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}.
  \item \textbf{Boundary detection and hole filling}: boundary walks
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_boundary_loops}) enumerate the loops to be filled
    or parameterized.
  \item \textbf{Orientation propagation}: the face walk carries the
    orientation consistency check of Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_boundary}
    across adjacent faces.
  \item \textbf{Geodesic and area queries}: walking the faces around a
    vertex accumulates the angle defect used in discrete curvature
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cycle closure}: assert every walk returns to its start
    half-edge; a walk that escapes indicates broken
    $\operatorname{next}$/$\operatorname{twin}$ links.
  \item \textbf{Partition test}: run all walks and verify that every
    half-edge is visited exactly once (Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_cycle_cover}).
  \item \textbf{Boundary}: on an open mesh, a vertex walk must stop at a
    boundary half-edge and must visit each boundary face once.
  \item \textbf{Edge cases}: a 2-gon (two half-edges, degenerate face), a
    lone edge, and a mesh of one triangle are minimal inputs on which the
    termination test must still fire.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

The traversal primitives and their use in mesh algorithms are described in
Botsch et al. \cite{botsch2010}; the walking queries are the workhorses of
the triangulation walk in Chapter~\ref{ch:point_location} and the
Delaunay insertion of Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}.

\section{Comparison with Other Representations}
\label{sec:halfedge_comparison}

The half-edge structure is one point on a spectrum of mesh representations
differing in which adjacencies are stored explicitly and whether duality is
exposed \cite{ericson2004,botsch2010}.

\begin{description}
  \item[Indexed face set] Vertices plus per-face index lists. Minimum
    storage and the universal file format, but no adjacency: every
    neighbor query costs a scan, and local edits require global rebuilds.
    It is the \emph{input} format for the construction pipeline of
    Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_construction}.
  \item[Winged edge]\index{winged-edge} The precursor of the half-edge
    structure, from the solid-modeling literature. Every undirected edge
    stores the two faces it borders and four half-edge links (the
    successors and predecessors of both halves). It answers the same
    queries as the half-edge structure but with more fields per edge and
    without the symmetric twin/next pairing; the half-edge structure is
    essentially a pointer-slimmed winged edge.
  \item[Half-edge / DCEL]\index{half-edge} This chapter's structure: two
    half-edges per edge, each with origin, twin, next, prev, and face.
    Exactly the adjacency needed by the planar algorithms of this book, at
    roughly two to three times the storage of an indexed face set.
  \item[Quad edge]\index{quad-edge} The structure of Guibas and Stolfi
    \cite{guibas1985} stores each geometric edge with \emph{four} directed
    edges: the two primal half-edges and the two dual half-edges crossing
    the edge. Its algebra (operators $\operatorname{rot}$,
    $\operatorname{sym}$, $\operatorname{flip}$, $\operatorname{next}$)
    handles the Voronoi--Delaunay duality uniformly, supports subdivisions
    of arbitrary topology (including non-manifold edge incidences via
    edge rings), and degrades gracefully to a half-edge structure when
    only the primal is needed. It is the representation of choice when an
    algorithm must navigate the dual subdivision, as in
    Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}.
  \item[Half-face]\index{half-face} The 3D analogue for volumetric cell
    complexes. A face of a 3D mesh is shared by up to two cells; a
    \textbf{half-face} is the restriction of a face to one incident
    volume, and the twin relation pairs half-faces across interior faces.
    Tetrahedral meshes implement the same idea more directly as four
    neighbor pointers per tetrahedron. Volumetric half-edge
    generalizations (the radial-edge family) additionally support
    non-manifold boundaries by cycling the faces around each edge.
\end{description}

\begin{remark}[Choosing a Representation]\label{rem:hed_choice}
As a rule of thumb: use an indexed face set for I/O and archival, the
half-edge structure for 2D subdivisions and polyhedral \emph{surfaces} on
which the algorithms of Chapters
\ref{ch:polygon_ops}, \ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}, and
\ref{ch:mesh_analysis} run, the quad-edge structure when primal--dual
duality is part of the algorithm, and half-faces or tetrahedral adjacency
for volumetric meshes (Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}). The structures
are freely convertible in linear time in either direction
(Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_construction} gives the hard direction).
\end{remark}

\index{quad-edge}\index{winged-edge}\index{half-face}\index{radial-edge}

\section{Incidence and Storage Complexity}
\label{sec:halfedge_complexity}

A half-edge structure stores one record per vertex, per half-edge, and per
face. With the fields of Definition~\ref{def:hed_dcel}, a half-edge holds
the twin, next, and prev pointers, the origin vertex, and the incident
face; a vertex holds its coordinates and one incident half-edge; a face
holds one boundary half-edge (plus one per extra boundary cycle). The
counts are exact:

\begin{theorem}[Storage Counts]\label{thm:hed_storage}
For a subdivision with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges, and $F$ faces, the
half-edge structure stores exactly $H = 2E$ half-edge records, $V$ vertex
records, and $F$ face records. The total storage is $O(V + E + F)$ and, for
a fixed record size, $O(V + E)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every undirected edge contributes exactly two half-edges, one per side
(Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_twin_boundary}); no other half-edges exist. Vertices
and faces are stored one per element of the subdivision by
Definition~\ref{def:hed_dcel}. For a connected planar subdivision
$E = O(V)$ by the planar Euler formula of Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_euler}, and
in general $E \leq 3V$ for planar triangulations (and for surface meshes of
bounded genus), so the bound follows.
\end{proof}

In a triangular mesh the numbers are explicit: $H = 2E$, and by the
Handshaking Lemma for triangle meshes \cite{diestel2017},
$3F = 2E$, so $E \approx 3V$ and $H \approx 6V$ for large closed meshes. The
half-edge structure therefore costs roughly $6$ half-edge records plus $1$
vertex record per vertex, against $3$ face records and $1$ vertex record
for an indexed face set: a constant factor, not an asymptotic change. This
is the standard price of $O(1)$ adjacency
\cite{botsch2010,ericson2004}.

The counting invariants of Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_incidence} are the mesh's
\textbf{topological checksum}: any inconsistency in the pointers changes
one of the sums, so the identity
\[
\sum_{v} \deg(v) = 2E = \sum_{f} |f|
\]
catches almost every corrupted structure. The deepest such invariant is
the Euler characteristic.

\begin{theorem}[Euler--Poincar\'e Formula]\label{thm:hed_euler}
Let $M$ be a connected 2-manifold mesh with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges,
$F$ faces, $b$ boundary components, and genus $g$ (see
Section~\ref{sec:math_topology} and Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}).
Then
\[
V - E + F = 2 - 2g - b .
\]
In particular, a closed surface of genus $g$ has $V - E + F = 2 - 2g$, and
a disk has $V - E + F = 1$ \cite{euler1758,poincare1895}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Sketch. Euler's classical result \cite{euler1758} gives $V - E + F = 2$
for convex polyhedra, which are closed surfaces of genus $0$. Removing a
disk (opening one boundary component) reduces $F$ by 1, giving the $-b$
term; attaching a handle raises $g$ by 1 and reduces $V - E + F$ by 2, by
triangulating the added tube with $V'$ new vertices and checking
$V' - E' + F' = 0$. A full proof is the induction presented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis} (Theorem~\ref{thm:euler_poincare}) and the
algebraic-topology formulation of Section~\ref{sec:math_topology}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Planar Subdivisions]
For a connected planar subdivision one conventionally counts the unbounded
face as an extra face, which adds exactly 1 to $F$ and yields the classical
$V - E + F = 2$ of Euler's planar formula \cite{deberg2008}. The two
conventions differ by exactly the outer face: the surface version above
gives $V - E + F = 2 - b$ for a disk-like region with $b$ boundary
components, and adding the outer face turns this into
$V - E + F = 2$. Keep the convention fixed when checking a mesh.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Checking a Mesh]
A closed tetrahedron mesh has $V = 4$, $E = 6$, $F = 4$, hence
$V - E + F = 2$, matching $g = 0$, $b = 0$. An open triangulated disk with
$V = 6$, $E = 9$, $F = 4$ satisfies $V - E + F = 1 = 2 - b$, so $b = 1$:
exactly one boundary component. If an edit corrupts the mesh, one of these
counts changes and the invariant test fails; this is why
Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis} runs the Euler test as a validity check.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Because $V - E + F$ is a \index{topological invariant}topological
invariant, it is constant across \emph{remeshing}: retriangulating a
surface without changing its topology preserves $V - E + F$, which is how
topology-preservation is tested in simplification and remeshing pipelines
(Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis} and Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}).
\end{remark}

\section{Construction from Polygon Soup}
\label{sec:halfedge_construction}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Real inputs --- an STL file, an OBJ export, the output of a
reconstruction algorithm --- arrive as an \index{polygon soup}\textbf{polygon
soup}: an unordered list of oriented polygon loops with no shared
structure. Building a half-edge structure from such a soup is a two-step
process: \textbf{vertex welding} merges coincident coordinates into shared
vertex records, and \textbf{edge pairing} matches the half-edges of each
face to the half-edges of its neighbors \cite{botsch2010,deberg2008}. The
same pipeline converts an indexed face set into the working structure used
by every other chapter.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Polygon Soup]\label{def:hed_polygon_soup}
A \textbf{polygon soup} is a list of faces, each given by an oriented
sequence of point coordinates (or of indices into an unshared coordinate
list). There is no requirement that vertices, edges, or faces be shared
between any two faces.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Vertex Welding]\label{def:hed_welding}
\textbf{Vertex welding} replaces the points of a polygon soup by a set of
\index{welded vertex}welded vertex records: two soup points map to the same
vertex if their distance is at most a tolerance $\epsilon$ (in practice,
below the coordinate quantization of the file format).
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

The correctness condition for the pairing step is exact:

\begin{theorem}[Pairing Condition]\label{thm:hed_pairing}
Let the faces of a soup be consistently oriented. The unordered vertex
pairs $\{u,v\}$ of the soup's edges are the undirected edges of a manifold
mesh if and only if every pair is used exactly twice. In that case each
bucket of size two contains one half-edge from each of the two adjacent
faces, and these two half-edges are each other's twins. An edge used once
is a boundary edge; an edge used three or more times is non-manifold and
cannot be represented.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A manifold mesh has every edge incident to exactly one or two faces
(Definition~\ref{def:hed_manifold}). Each incidence of face $f$ with edge
$\{u,v\}$ contributes one half-edge of that face over $\{u,v\}$, so an edge
appears in the soup once per incident face: once for a boundary edge, twice
for an interior edge. Hence the bucket size of $\{u,v\}$ equals the number
of incident faces. Two occurrences with the same face would duplicate a
half-edge and are impossible for a well-formed face. If the faces are
consistently oriented, the two half-edges of a size-two bucket traverse
$\{u,v\}$ in opposite directions and are twins. Conversely, bucket sizes
outside $\{1,2\}$ witness a boundary-less non-manifold edge or a duplicated
face.
\end{proof}

Consistency of orientation is required for the conclusion; a soup with
mixed orientations still welds and pairs, but the extracted faces have
inconsistent normals, which the orientability pass of
Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis} then repairs.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Weld Polygon-Soup Vertices}
\label{alg:hed_vertex_weld}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A list $P$ of points and a tolerance $\epsilon > 0$.
\Ensure A map from soup points to welded vertex indices.
\Function{WeldVertices}{$P$, $\epsilon$}
  \State $G \gets$ uniform grid of cell size $\epsilon$; $\mathit{map} \gets [\,]$; $k \gets 0$
  \For{each point $p \in P$}
    \State $c \gets$ grid cell of $p$; $\mathit{found} \gets \text{None}$
    \For{each vertex $q$ in $c$ or in the 8 neighboring cells}
      \If{$\|p - q\| \leq \epsilon$}
        \State $\mathit{found} \gets \text{index}(q)$; \textbf{break}
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \If{$\mathit{found} \neq \text{None}$}
      \State $\mathit{map}.\text{append}(\mathit{found})$
    \Else
      \State insert $p$ as vertex $k$; $\mathit{map}.\text{append}(k)$; $k \gets k + 1$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathit{map}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Build Half-Edge Structure from a Polygon Soup}
\label{alg:hed_build_dcel}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Welded, consistently oriented faces, each an index cycle.
\Ensure A valid half-edge structure.
\Function{BuildDCEL}{$\mathit{faces}$}
  \State create one half-edge per directed face edge; set origin and face fields
  \State $\mathit{buckets} \gets$ hash map on unordered vertex pairs
  \For{each half-edge $h$ from $u$ to $v$}
    \State $\mathit{buckets}[\{u,v\}].\text{append}(h)$
  \EndFor
  \For{each bucket $\{h_i, h_j\}$}
    \If{$\lvert \mathit{bucket} \rvert = 2$}
      \State $\operatorname{twin}(h_i) \gets h_j$; $\operatorname{twin}(h_j) \gets h_i$
    \ElsIf{$\lvert \mathit{bucket} \rvert = 1$}
      \State leave the twin null (boundary half-edge)
    \Else
      \State \textbf{error}: non-manifold edge $\{u,v\}$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \For{each face}
    \State link its half-edges cyclically with $\operatorname{next}$ and $\operatorname{prev}$
  \EndFor
  \State extract boundary cycles and append a dummy boundary half-edge per cycle \Comment{optional}
  \State \Return the structure
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Welding tolerance}: choose $\epsilon$ slightly larger than
    the coordinate quantization (e.g., $10^{-6}$ relative units for
    single-precision STL), but small enough not to merge genuinely
    distinct vertices. Too large a tolerance collapses thin features.
  \item \textbf{Bucket key}: canonicalize each edge as
    $(\min(u,v), \max(u,v))$ so both orientations hash to the same bucket;
    a key on the oriented pair would fail to pair twins.
  \item \textbf{Boundary half-edges}: keep twins null and fix the boundary
    convention before any traversal (Sections
    \ref{sec:halfedge_boundary} and \ref{sec:halfedge_traversal}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Welding}: $O(n)$ expected time and space with the uniform
    grid of Algorithm~\ref{alg:hed_vertex_weld}, $O(n \log n)$ with a
    balanced-tree variant; each point touches a constant number of cells
    for fixed $\epsilon$.
  \item \textbf{Pairing}: $O(n)$ expected, dominated by one hash insert and
    one lookup per half-edge.
  \item \textbf{Total}: $O(n)$ expected time and space for a soup with $n$
    directed edges; the output satisfies the storage bounds of
    Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_storage}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{File loading}: STL, OBJ, and PLY importers run exactly this
    pipeline \cite{botsch2010,ericson2004}.
  \item \textbf{Scan reconstruction}: the triangle soup produced by
    surface-reconstruction algorithms is welded and paired before any
    analysis (Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation} consumes such meshes).
  \item \textbf{Repair}: the pairing stage reports non-manifold edges and
    inconsistent orientations, which is the first repair pass of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Closed mesh}: a welded tetrahedron or sphere must produce
    zero boundary half-edges and satisfy
    $V - E + F = 2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_euler}).
  \item \textbf{Open mesh}: a single triangle must yield one boundary cycle
    of three half-edges and $V - E + F = 1$.
  \item \textbf{Duplicate vertices}: faces whose coordinates are identical
    to 12 decimals must weld to a single shared vertex record.
  \item \textbf{Coordinate noise}: perturb the coordinates by $10^{-5}$
    before welding and verify the tolerance merges them back.
  \item \textbf{Non-manifold soup}: an edge shared by three faces must
    raise the error branch of Algorithm~\ref{alg:hed_build_dcel}.
  \item \textbf{Duplicate faces}: two identical triangles use each edge
    twice but generate two half-edge pairs with the same face on both
    sides; the structure must flag or reject this.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

The welding-and-pairing pipeline follows Botsch et al. \cite{botsch2010}
and the file-import discussion of Ericson \cite{ericson2004}; the
underlying hashing and graph-construction techniques are standard
dictionary algorithms \cite{cormen2009}. The DCEL construction for planar
subdivisions is described by de Berg et al. \cite{deberg2008}.

\section{Applications in Mesh Processing}
\label{sec:halfedge_applications}

The half-edge structure pays for its storage in the local, $O(1)$
topological edits called \index{Euler operation}\textbf{Euler operations}.
These are the primitives from which every topology-changing mesh edit ---
extruding, bridging, deleting a handle, subdividing --- is assembled
\cite{botsch2010,deberg2008}. The classical set, drawn from the
solid-modeling tradition, includes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Make vertex, face, solid (MVFS)}: creates a new component
    with one vertex and one face.
  \item \textbf{Make edge, vertex (MEV)}: adds an edge from a vertex to a
    new vertex on a face.
  \item \textbf{Make edge, face (MEF)}: adds an edge inside a face,
    splitting it into two faces.
  \item The inverse operations \textbf{Kill edge, face (KEF)}, \textbf{Kill
    edge, vertex (KEV)}, and \textbf{Kill vertex, face, solid (KVFS)}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{theorem}[Euler Operations Preserve the Invariant]\label{thm:hed_euler_ops}
Each of MEV, MEF, KEV, and KEF changes $(V, E, F)$ by one of
$(1,1,0)$, $(0,1,1)$, $(-1,-1,0)$, $(0,-1,-1)$, and therefore leaves
$V - E + F$ unchanged. Every connected orientable manifold mesh can be
built from a single MVFS by a sequence of MEV and MEF operations, and
every edit that preserves the Euler characteristic of
Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_euler} is a sequence of these operations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The count changes follow directly from the operation definitions. For
generation: a connected mesh's dual graph is connected, so its faces can be
ordered so that each new face shares an edge with a previous one; adding
faces one at a time by MEF (after placing the first face's vertices with
MEV) constructs the mesh. Conversely, any sequence of the four operations
preserves $V - E + F$ by direct inspection of the increments, and since
these operations span the local relinking moves (edge split, edge collapse,
face split, edge flip, vertex split), any topology-preserving edit
decomposes into them.
\end{proof}

The pointer surgery for an edge split, for example, creates two new
half-edges, reassigns two twin pointers and the $\operatorname{next}$/
$\operatorname{prev}$ links of four neighbors, and never touches any other
half-edge: $O(1)$ work with no global reindexing. This locality is what
makes interactive \index{mesh editing}mesh editing --- the vertex-painting,
extrusion, and Boolean workflows of modern modeling tools --- practical on
million-face meshes \cite{botsch2010,ericson2004}.

\textbf{Boolean operations} on polyhedra (union, intersection,
difference) combine the classification of Chapter~\ref{ch:polygon_ops}
with half-edge surgery: intersecting faces are split along the
intersection polygons, the faces inside each operand are classified, and
the surviving pieces are stitched by pairing twin half-edges at the cut
\cite{ericson2004}. The result is reassembled by the construction of
Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_construction} run on the subdivided soup, and
its validity is verified with the Euler test
(Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_euler}) and the manifold checks of
Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}.

\begin{example}[Edge Flip in Delaunay Legalization]\label{ex:hed_flip}
In a Delaunay triangulation, the legality test of
Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations} examines the two faces on either
side of an interior edge; when the edge is illegal, it is replaced by the
other diagonal of the quadrilateral. In half-edge terms the flip re-links
four half-edges' $\operatorname{next}$/$\operatorname{prev}$ pointers and
the two twin pairs: four pointer assignments per half-edge, $O(1)$ total,
which is why incremental Delaunay insertion (Algorithm~\ref{alg:hed_build_dcel}
supplies the structure it runs on) achieves expected $O(\log n)$ per point.
\end{example}

Beyond booleans and editing, the half-edge structure is the substrate of
the traversal-heavy algorithms throughout this book and its companion
chapters:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mesh analysis} (Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}): connected
    components, boundary detection, orientability, and manifold
    verification are exactly the walks and twin tests of Sections
    \ref{sec:halfedge_adjacency}--\ref{sec:halfedge_traversal}.
  \item \textbf{Polygon operations} (Chapter~\ref{ch:polygon_ops}): polygon
    booleans and clipping resample the boundary cycles
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_boundary_loops}) of the input subdivisions.
  \item \textbf{Delaunay triangulations} (Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}):
    point insertion, legalization flips, and the walking point location of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:point_location} are half-edge walks.
  \item \textbf{Computational topology} (Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}):
    the Euler characteristic, Betti numbers, and homology computations read
    the incidence information encoded by the twin and face links, and
    Section~\ref{sec:math_topology} supplies the manifold background that
    the boundary machinery of Section~\ref{sec:halfedge_boundary} assumes.
  \item \textbf{Mesh generation} (Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}): the
    output of a conforming mesher is delivered as a half-edge (or
    index-based) structure with the exact adjacency that refinement and
    adaptation need.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:halfedge_exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Draw the half-edge structure of a square subdivided into two
    triangles by a diagonal. List all ten half-edges (five undirected
    edges, two half-edges each) with their origin, twin, next, and prev
    links, and verify $\sum_v \deg(v) = 2E = \sum_f |f|$
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_incidence}).
  \item Prove that in a half-edge structure, $\operatorname{org}(h) =
    \operatorname{org}(\operatorname{twin}(\operatorname{next}(h)))$
    holds for every half-edge $h$, and interpret the equality in words.
  \item Show that a face walk that counts steps instead of testing for the
    start half-edge can loop forever, and give a concrete small mesh where
    this happens.
  \item Let $T$ be a triangulated connected surface with $V$ vertices,
    $F$ triangular faces, $E$ edges, $B$ boundary edges, and $b$ boundary
    components. Prove $3F = 2E - B$ (each triangle contributes three
    directed edge incidences; interior edges are counted twice, boundary
    edges once) and combine it with Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_euler} to derive
    $F = 2V - 4 + 2b - B$. Check the formula on a square with one interior
    vertex ($V = 5$, $B = 4$, $b = 1$) and on a plain square.
  \item Design a linear-time algorithm, using
    Algorithm~\ref{alg:hed_face_traversal}, that lists the boundary
    components of an open mesh from the null-twin half-edges alone, and
    prove it visits every boundary edge exactly once
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:hed_boundary_loops}).
  \item Convert the indexed face set of a tetrahedron into a half-edge
    structure by hand, following Algorithm~\ref{alg:hed_build_dcel}, and
    confirm that every twin is paired and that $V - E + F = 2$.
  \item Implement vertex welding with tolerance $\epsilon$ on a soup of
    noisy coordinates, then apply the Euler test
    ($V - E + F$) to the output and explain which failures the test can
    and cannot detect.
  \item Give the half-edge pointer assignments for an edge flip in a
    triangulated quadrilateral, as in Example~\ref{ex:hed_flip}, and verify
    that only the four half-edges of the two faces change their links.
\end{enumerate}
