\section{Shape Representation}
\label{sec:shape_representation}

A \textbf{shape}\index{shape} in this chapter is a surface: a compact
connected two-dimensional manifold embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$, or, for terrain
modeling, a height function over a planar region. \textbf{Shape
representation}\index{shape representation} describes such a surface with a
data structure supporting the queries and algorithms of the surrounding
chapters: rendering, intersection, simplification, parameterization,
simulation, and --- in the reverse direction --- reconstruction from
measurements. Representations fall into two families. \textbf{Discrete
representations} store a finite set of primitives: a point cloud stores an
unorganized set of samples with no connectivity, while a mesh stores a
piecewise-linear surface on a simplicial complex
(Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_simplicial} in Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}).
\textbf{Continuous representations} store a mathematical description: a
parametric patch, an implicit equation $f(x, y, z) = 0$, a signed distance
function (Section~\ref{sec:shape_sdf}), or a level set of a scalar field
(Section~\ref{sec:shape_level_sets}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Point clouds}\index{point cloud} are the raw output of 3D
    scanning and photogrammetry; they carry no topology, which must be
    re-discovered (Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_point_clouds}).
  \item \textbf{Meshes} add topology: a triangle mesh is a 2D simplicial
    complex whose underlying space approximates the surface, stored with the
    half-edge structure of Chapter~\ref{ch:half_edge} and validated by
    Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}.
  \item \textbf{Parametric representations}
    (Section~\ref{sec:shape_representation_implicit}) map a planar domain onto
    the surface; they are native to CAD but do not easily guarantee
    watertightness.
  \item \textbf{Implicit representations} (Sections~\ref{sec:shape_sdf} and
    \ref{sec:shape_poisson_reconstruction}) describe the surface as the zero
    set of a function; they are watertight by construction and make
    inside/outside and boolean operations trivial.
\end{itemize}

The choice follows the operations that come after it: a point cloud suffices
for display and nearest-neighbor queries but not for finite-element assembly;
an implicit function answers inside/outside queries and supports boolean CSG
but resists direct manipulation; a mesh supports refinement, smoothing, and
simulation but must be kept manifold and intersection-free. Because real
acquisitions are point clouds, the central task of this chapter is the
conversion from point data to a faithful, topologically correct surface.

\section{Implicit and Parametric Representations}
\label{sec:shape_representation_implicit}

A \textbf{parametric surface}\index{parametric surface} is the image
$x(D)$ of a map $x \colon D \to \mathbb{R}^3$ from a planar parameter domain
$D$ (typically a set of patches). An \textbf{implicit surface}\index{implicit
surface} is the zero set of a function $f \colon \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$,
$S = \{ x : f(x) = 0 \}$. The two are dual in a practical sense. A parametric
surface is rendered and meshed directly (sample $D$ on a grid and connect the
samples), and its points are enumerated cheaply; but the union or intersection
of two patches requires numerical root finding, and inside/outside is
ill-defined without an orientation convention. An implicit surface gives the
test $f(x) \lessgtr 0$ for free, its boolean union is simply
$\min(f_1, f_2)$ and its intersection $\max(f_1, f_2)$, and watertightness is
automatic; the cost is that meshing requires a polygonization such as the
marching cubes algorithm of Section~\ref{sec:shape_level_sets}
\cite{lorensen1987}.

CAD systems work overwhelmingly with parametric patches: tensor-product
B\'ezier surfaces and their rational extension, non-uniform rational B-splines
(NURBS)\index{NURBS}, are the standard primitives of the industry
\cite{farin2002,piegl1997}. A NURBS surface of degree $(p, q)$ is
\[
x(u,v) = \frac{\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij} P_{ij} N_{i,p}(u) N_{j,q}(v)}
                {\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij} N_{i,p}(u) N_{j,q}(v)},
\]
where $P_{ij}$ are control points, $w_{ij}$ weights, and $N_{i,p}$ the
B-spline basis functions. The parametrization is the main asset: a designer
controls the shape through a coarse control polygon, and exact refinement
(knot insertion) produces dense meshes. The main liability is that a NURBS
model is watertight only by explicit agreement of the boundary curves of
neighboring patches.

Implicit representations arise naturally from measurement rather than design:
the signed distance functions of Section~\ref{sec:shape_sdf} and the indicator
functions of Section~\ref{sec:shape_poisson_reconstruction} are both obtained
from point clouds by fitting a function to the data, and both are regular
enough (Lipschitz, often smooth) to be numerically well behaved; isosurface
extraction (Section~\ref{sec:shape_level_sets}) turns them into meshes. The
two families meet in parameterization: a triangle mesh has no intrinsic
parameter domain, and Chapter~\ref{ch:surface_geometry} constructs one by
mapping the mesh to a planar domain, after which patch-based methods apply.

\section{Reconstruction from Point Clouds}
\label{sec:shape_reconstruction_point_clouds}

\subsection{The Problem}

The \textbf{surface reconstruction problem}\index{surface reconstruction}
is: given a finite point set $P \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ sampled from an unknown
surface $S$, compute a mesh $\hat{S}$ topologically and geometrically close to
$S$. The problem is ill posed --- a finite sample is consistent with infinitely
many surfaces --- so any solution must be guided by an a priori model: that $S$
is smooth and that $P$ samples it densely enough relative to its features. The
sampling conditions quantify density in terms of the geometry of $S$, so that a
sphere and a thin tube of similar sampling density receive similar treatments.

\begin{definition}[Local Feature Size]\label{def:shape_lfs}
  Let $S$ be a surface and let $M$ be its medial axis
  (Definition~\ref{def:medial_axis} in Chapter~\ref{ch:surface_geometry}). The
  \textbf{local feature size} of $S$ at $x \in S$ is the distance from $x$ to
  $M$: $\operatorname{lfs}(x) = \operatorname{dist}(x, M)$. It is a positive
  continuous function on $S$ and is $1$-Lipschitz: $\operatorname{lfs}(x) \le
  \operatorname{lfs}(y) + \operatorname{dist}(x, y)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\varepsilon$-Sample]\label{def:shape_epsilon_sample}
  A point set $P \subset S$ is an \textbf{$\varepsilon$-sample} of $S$ if every
  $x \in S$ is within distance $\varepsilon \cdot \operatorname{lfs}(x)$ of a
  point of $P$, i.e. $\sup_{x \in S} \min_{p \in P}
  \operatorname{dist}(x, p) / \operatorname{lfs}(x) \le \varepsilon$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$r$-Sample]\label{def:shape_r_sample}
  A point set $P \subset S$ is an \textbf{$r$-sample} of $S$ if every $x \in S$
  is within distance $r \cdot \operatorname{lfs}(p)$ of the sample $p$ nearest
  to $x$: $\sup_{x \in S} \min_{p \in P} \operatorname{dist}(x, p) /
  \operatorname{lfs}(p) \le r$.
\end{definition}

By the Lipschitz property the two conditions are equivalent up to a factor
$1 + O(\varepsilon)$: an $\varepsilon$-sample is an $r$-sample for
$r = \varepsilon / (1 - \varepsilon)$, and conversely. Both require more points
where the surface is thin or curved (small $\operatorname{lfs}$) and fewer on
flat regions --- the same feature-adaptive philosophy as the size-field meshing
of Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_quality} --- in contrast to the uniform-density
samples of the discrepancy theory of \cite{matousek1999}.

\subsection{Voronoi-Based Sampling Guarantees}

The sampling conditions translate into strong statements about the Voronoi
diagram of $P$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:voronoi_diagrams}) and its dual Delaunay
complex (Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}). The key object is the
restricted Delaunay complex, whose dual Voronoi faces meet the surface.

\begin{definition}[Restricted Delaunay Complex]\label{def:shape_restricted_delaunay}
  A Delaunay simplex $\sigma$ of $P$ belongs to the \textbf{restricted Delaunay
  complex} $\operatorname{Del}(P, S)$ if its dual Voronoi face $V(\sigma)$
  intersects $S$. It is a subcomplex of the Delaunay complex of $P$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Sampling Guarantee]\label{thm:shape_sampling_guarantee}
  Let $S$ be a compact smooth surface and $P$ an $\varepsilon$-sample of $S$.
  There is a constant $\varepsilon_0$ such that for every $\varepsilon \le
  \varepsilon_0$ the restricted Delaunay complex $\operatorname{Del}(P, S)$ is
  a two-dimensional manifold complex homeomorphic to $S$ and within Hausdorff
  distance $O(\varepsilon \cdot \max_S \operatorname{lfs})$ of $S$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  The proof has three steps. First, the poles of $P$
  (Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_surface}) approximate the surface
  normals: the two farthest Voronoi vertices of each cell lie on opposite sides
  of $S$ at distance $\Omega(\operatorname{lfs})$ from it. Second, every simplex
  of $\operatorname{Del}(P, S)$ has circumradius $O(\varepsilon \cdot
  \operatorname{lfs})$, so its vertices lie in a thickening of $S$ of that
  width, strictly between the two sheets of the medial axis. Third, projecting
  the restricted triangles onto $S$ along the normals is a homeomorphism for
  $\varepsilon$ below $\varepsilon_0$; the classical proofs establish
  $\varepsilon_0 = 0.06$ for the crust and cocone filters
  \cite{amentabern1999,amenta2000}.
\end{proof}

The theorem is the foundation of all Delaunay-based reconstruction: the surface
is \emph{already present} as a subcomplex of the Delaunay complex, and the
algorithms of Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_surface} differ only in
how they select it. The same argument with the $r$-sample condition gives the
provably good surface meshing theory of Boissonnat and Oudot
\cite{boissonnat2005}: for $r \le 0.09$ the restricted Delaunay complex of an
$r$-sample is homeomorphic to the surface and refinable with quality
guarantees (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement}, \cite{shewchuk2002}).

\subsection{Delaunay-Based Meshing Approaches}

The Voronoi and Delaunay structures organize reconstruction into a four-stage
pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Triangulation}: compute the Delaunay complex of $P$, a
    tetrahedralization built by randomized incremental insertion in expected
    $O(n \log n)$ time (worst-case size $\Theta(n^2)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$,
    Theorem~\ref{thm:high_dim_size}).
  \item \textbf{Filtering}: select the triangles plausibly on $S$ --- by
    empty-sphere radius (alpha complex, Definition~\ref{def:delaunay_alpha_complex}),
    pole orientation (crust), cocone angle, or the ball-pivoting empty-ball rule
    (Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_surface}).
  \item \textbf{Cleaning}: remove non-manifold edges, delete small spurious
    components, and fill holes, using the topological checks of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}.
  \item \textbf{Refinement}: re-mesh the output with the guaranteed-quality
    Delaunay refinement of Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement} \cite{shewchuk2002}.
\end{enumerate}

For terrain data the pipeline simplifies to two dimensions: samples of a height
function $z = h(x, y)$ are projected to the plane, the 2D Delaunay triangulation
of the projection is the triangulated irregular network (TIN) --- the natural
choice by the maximin angle property (Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations})
--- and rivers and breaklines enter as constraints of the constrained Delaunay
triangulation, as in Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}.

\subsection{Complexity}

Reconstruction is dominated by the Delaunay tetrahedralization of stage~1:
expected $O(n \log n)$ time and $O(n)$ space for well-distributed samples, and
$O(n^2)$ worst case in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:high_dim_size});
filtering, cleaning, and hole filling are linear in the $O(n)$ triangles. Noise
and outliers are handled only partially by the filter-based methods of the next
section; the least-squares methods of Sections~\ref{sec:shape_sdf} and
\ref{sec:shape_poisson_reconstruction} are the robust alternatives.

\section{Surface Reconstruction}
\label{sec:shape_reconstruction_surface}

\subsection{1. Overview}

This section develops the four classical Delaunay-based algorithms that turn
Theorem~\ref{thm:shape_sampling_guarantee} into working software. The
\emph{alpha-shape} family \cite{edelsbrunner1983alpha,edelsbrunner1994}
selects triangles by an empty-sphere radius; the \emph{crust}
\cite{amenta1998,amentabern1999} selects triangles through the poles of the
Voronoi cells, whose directions approximate the surface normals; the
\emph{cocone} \cite{amenta2000} sharpens the crust filter to a thin cone
around the pole direction and adds manifold checks that produce a watertight
output; and the \emph{ball-pivoting algorithm} \cite{bernardini1999} grows a
mesh directly from the points, without building the Delaunay complex, by
pivoting a ball of fixed radius around the front. All four share the
filter-and-clean pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_point_clouds}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Pole and Pole Vector]\label{def:shape_pole}
  A \textbf{pole} of a sample $p$ is a vertex of the Voronoi cell $V(p)$
  farthest from $p$; when $V(p)$ is unbounded the pole is taken on the far side
  of the surface, at distance $\ge \operatorname{lfs}(p)$ from $p$. The
  \textbf{pole vector} $v_p = p^{+} - p$, where $p^{+}$ is the pole, approximates
  the inward normal of $S$ at $p$ up to an angle $O(\varepsilon)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Cocone]\label{def:shape_cocone}
  Let $\theta \in (0, \pi/2)$, typically $\theta = \arcsin(\varepsilon)$. The
  \textbf{cocone} of $p$ with pole vector $v_p$ is the subset of the Voronoi
  cell
  \[
  C(p) = \left\{ x \in V(p) : \frac{(x - p) \cdot v_p}{\| x - p \|}
          \ge \sin \theta \right\},
  \]
  a thin double cone around the pole direction containing the surface near $p$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Alpha Shape]\label{def:shape_alpha_shape}
  The \textbf{alpha shape} of $P$ at $\alpha \ge 0$ is the union of the
  simplices of the alpha complex at $\alpha$
  (Definition~\ref{def:delaunay_alpha_complex}): those Delaunay simplices whose
  smallest empty circumsphere has radius at most $\alpha$, with all their
  faces. As $\alpha$ grows from $0$ to $\infty$ the alpha shape interpolates
  from the point set to the convex hull of $P$ \cite{edelsbrunner1983alpha};
  the family is studied in Section~\ref{sec:alpha_shapes} of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:surface_modeling}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Empty-Ball Property]\label{def:shape_empty_ball}
  A ball of radius $\rho$ through three points of $P$ is \emph{empty} if its
  interior contains no point of $P$. The ball-pivoting algorithm accepts a
  triangle only if some empty ball of radius $\rho$ passes through its three
  vertices.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

\begin{theorem}[Crust and Cocone Guarantee]\label{thm:shape_crust_guarantee}
  Let $S$ be a smooth surface and $P$ an $\varepsilon$-sample of $S$ with
  $\varepsilon \le 0.06$. The crust of $P$ \cite{amentabern1999} and the cocone
  of $P$ \cite{amenta2000} each contain a set of triangles forming a surface
  homeomorphic to $S$ and within Hausdorff distance $O(\varepsilon \cdot
  \operatorname{lfs})$ of $S$. The cocone output is additionally watertight,
  and every cocone triangle has circumradius $O(\varepsilon \cdot
  \operatorname{lfs})$ and makes an angle $O(\varepsilon)$ with the tangent
  plane of $S$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  By Theorem~\ref{thm:shape_sampling_guarantee} the restricted Delaunay complex
  $\operatorname{Del}(P, S)$ is homeomorphic to $S$. For the crust one shows
  that a Delaunay triangle of $P \cup \{ \text{poles} \}$ with all three
  vertices in $P$ is a triangle of the restricted complex, and conversely. For
  the cocone one shows the circumcenter of every restricted triangle lies in
  $C(p)$ for each of its vertices, because the pole direction makes an angle
  $O(\varepsilon)$ with the normal. The umbrella property (every interior
  vertex has one cycle of incident triangles) then yields the homeomorphism and
  the watertightness \cite{amenta2000,dey2007}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Ball-Pivoting Correctness]\label{thm:shape_ball_pivoting_correctness}
  Let $S$ be a watertight surface and $P$ a sample of $S$ such that (i) no
  feature of $S$ of diameter smaller than $\rho$ separates two of its points
  (the ball of radius $\rho$ never crosses the medial axis) and (ii) the ball
  can always be pivoted from any two adjacent samples to a third sample. Then
  the ball-pivoting algorithm \cite{bernardini1999} produces a triangle mesh
  homeomorphic to $S$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  Condition (i) keeps the pivoted ball from ``jumping'' across a thin feature,
  so the front advances without self-intersection; condition (ii) keeps the
  front from stalling in gaps. Under both, each step adds a triangle whose
  interior contains no other point of $P$, the front propagates over all of $S$
  by connectivity, and the output is a triangulation of the same topological
  type as $S$ \cite{bernardini1999}.
\end{proof}

The alpha-shape family has a corresponding guarantee in the $r$-sample model:
for $r \le 0.09$ some value of $\alpha$ makes the alpha complex contain exactly
the restricted Delaunay complex, so the alpha shape reconstructs the surface
\cite{boissonnat2005}. In practice $\alpha$ is chosen by inspection: too small
fragments the surface, too large fills the concavities of the convex hull.

\emph{Noise and outliers.} The guarantees assume an ideal sample lying on $S$;
real scans perturb each point with noise and add outliers away from $S$.
Delaunay-based filters are fragile under both: an outlier is a legitimate
Delaunay vertex whose triangles may survive the filter, and noise inflates
circumradii, breaking the empty-sphere conditions. The remedies are
pre-processing (project each point onto the local tangent plane fitted to its
nearest neighbors; prune outliers by nearest-neighbor statistics) and
post-processing (the implicit least-squares methods of
Sections~\ref{sec:shape_sdf} and \ref{sec:shape_poisson_reconstruction} smooth
by construction). Among the Delaunay methods the cocone is the most robust,
because its manifold checks remove outlier-induced non-manifold structures
\cite{amenta2000,dey2007}.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Crust (Amenta--Bern)}
\label{alg:shape_crust}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require an $\varepsilon$-sample $P$ of a smooth surface $S$.
\Ensure a triangle mesh homeomorphic to $S$.
\Function{Crust}{$P$}
  \State compute the Voronoi diagram of $P$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:voronoi_diagrams})
  \For{each sample $p \in P$}
    \State $p^{+} \gets$ pole of $V(p)$, the farthest Voronoi vertex of $V(p)$
  \EndFor
  \State $P' \gets P \cup \{ p^{+} : p \in P \}$
  \State compute the Delaunay triangulation $DT(P')$
  \State keep every triangle of $DT(P')$ whose three vertices all lie in $P$
  \State remove small connected components of the retained triangles
  \State \Return the retained triangles
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The 2D analogue of Algorithm~\ref{alg:shape_crust}, which reconstructs a planar
curve, keeps the edges of $DT(P \cup \text{poles})$ whose endpoints both lie in
$P$ and provably recovers the curve \cite{amenta1998}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Cocone (Amenta--Choi--Dey--Leekha)}
\label{alg:shape_cocone}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require an $\varepsilon$-sample $P$ of a smooth surface $S$, angle $\theta = \arcsin(\varepsilon)$.
\Ensure a watertight triangle mesh homeomorphic to $S$.
\Function{Cocone}{$P$, $\theta$}
  \State compute the Voronoi diagram of $P$
  \For{each sample $p \in P$}
    \State $p^{+} \gets$ pole of $V(p)$; \quad $v_p \gets$ unit pole vector
    \State $C(p) \gets \{ x \in V(p) : (x - p) \cdot v_p \ge \sin\theta \, \| x - p \| \}$
  \EndFor
  \State keep every Delaunay triangle whose circumcenter lies in $C(p)$ for some vertex $p$
  \State remove non-manifold edges (umbrella property)
  \State fill small holes left by the filter
  \State \Return the cleaned triangle mesh
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Ball-Pivoting Algorithm (Bernardini et al.)}
\label{alg:shape_ball_pivoting}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require a point set $P$, ball radius $\rho$, a spatial grid for neighbor queries.
\Ensure a triangle mesh of the surface sampled by $P$.
\Function{BallPivot}{$P$, $\rho$}
  \State $G \gets$ uniform grid over $P$ for neighbor queries
  \State find a seed triangle $(a,b,c)$ whose three points admit an empty ball of radius $\rho$
  \State $\text{front} \gets$ the three oriented edges of the seed triangle
  \While{front is not empty}
    \State pop an oriented edge $(a,b)$ from the front
    \State pivot: rotate the ball of radius $\rho$ through $a$ and $b$ around
           the edge $(a,b)$ until it touches a point of $P$
    \If{a point $c$ is touched and the ball through $a,b,c$ is empty}
      \State add triangle $(a,b,c)$ to the mesh
      \State push the two new oriented edges of $(a,b,c)$ onto the front
    \Else
      \State mark $(a,b)$ as a boundary edge of the mesh
    \EndIf
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return the triangle mesh
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Alpha}: must exceed the sample spacing (so triangles span real
    patches) but stay below the feature size (so the shape does not bridge
    concavities); sweep $\alpha$ and stop at the smallest value for which the
    output is a manifold of the expected genus
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}).
  \item \textbf{Ball radius $\rho$}: bounded below by the maximum distance
    between adjacent samples (or the front stalls) and above by the minimum
    feature size (or the ball jumps across a thin feature). Run several passes
    with increasing $\rho$ to fill holes.
  \item \textbf{Cocone angle $\theta$}: the theory calls for
    $\theta = \arcsin(\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon$ estimated from the
    maximum sample gap relative to the feature size; widen $\theta$ slightly to
    tolerate noise.
  \item \textbf{Cleaning thresholds}: the sizes of removed components and of
    filled holes trade recall (more true surface) against precision (fewer
    artifacts).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Crust and cocone}: dominated by the Delaunay tetrahedralization
    (expected $O(n \log n)$, worst case $O(n^2)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$,
    Theorem~\ref{thm:high_dim_size}); pole computation and filtering are linear
    passes over the $O(n)$ cells and triangles.
  \item \textbf{Ball pivoting}: with a grid giving $O(1)$ expected neighbor
    queries, each pivot inspects $O(1)$ candidates and the total is $O(n)$ for
    a surface of $n$ samples \cite{bernardini1999}.
  \item \textbf{Space}: $O(n)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reverse engineering}: scanned parts are reconstructed with the
    crust, cocone, or ball pivoting, then refined and smoothed for CAD; this is
    the front end of metrology and of the surface reconstruction of
    Section~\ref{sec:shape_applications}.
  \item \textbf{Scientific computing}: reconstructed surfaces seed the
    mesh-generation pipeline of Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}, whose
    refinement (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement}) produces quality meshes
    for simulation.
  \item \textbf{Multiscale analysis}: the alpha shape provides a one-parameter
    family of reconstructions, the reference for evaluating the other filters.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Synthetic verification}: sample a sphere and a torus
    analytically, reconstruct, and verify homeomorphism via the Euler
    characteristic and Betti numbers (Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology})
    and the Hausdorff error against the known surface.
  \item \textbf{Non-uniform sampling}: density varying by two orders of
    magnitude defeats ball pivoting (one $\rho$ cannot fit all) but is handled
    by the feature-adaptive filters; verify the cocone and crust degrade only
    where the $\varepsilon$-condition is violated.
  \item \textbf{Boundary, open surfaces, and sharp features}: the crust and
    ball pivoting assume a closed surface, so on open patches boundary edges
    must be reported, not filled as holes; near edges of zero local feature
    size the $\varepsilon$-sample model fails, and the feature edges must
    survive rather than be rounded away.
  \item \textbf{Noise and outliers}: add Gaussian noise and outliers, and
    verify that normal estimation and outlier pruning keep the reconstruction
    valid; nearly flat regions should produce no surviving slivers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Signed Distance Functions}
\label{sec:shape_sdf}

\begin{definition}[Signed Distance Function]\label{def:shape_sdf}
  Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ be a compact region with boundary
  $\partial\Omega = S$. The \textbf{signed distance function} of $\Omega$ is
  \[
  \phi(x) = \begin{cases} \operatorname{dist}(x, S) & \text{if } x \in \Omega, \\
                         -\operatorname{dist}(x, S) & \text{if } x \notin \Omega. \end{cases}
  \]
  The surface is the zero level set $\phi^{-1}(0)$; the interior of $\Omega$
  is $\phi > 0$ and the exterior $\phi < 0$ (the sign convention varies).
\end{definition}

The signed distance function\index{signed distance function} is the canonical
implicit representation because of three properties. It is $1$-Lipschitz and
satisfies the \textbf{Eikonal equation}\index{Eikonal equation}
$|\nabla \phi| = 1$ almost everywhere, so its gradients are unit vectors along
the shortest paths to the surface. It is a solid representation: the sign test
$\phi(x) > 0$ answers inside/outside queries in $O(1)$ time and boolean
operations are $\min$ and $\max$. And the distance field itself is the data
many applications need --- collision detection, closest-point queries, and the
front propagation of Section~\ref{sec:shape_level_sets} all run on $\phi$.

Signed distance functions are computed from every input type:

\begin{description}
  \item[From a mesh] The distance to a triangle mesh is the minimum over
    vertices, edges, and faces; the fast marching method of
    Section~\ref{sec:shape_level_sets} propagates it through a voxel grid in
    $O(N \log N)$ time for $N$ voxels, with the sign decided once per voxel by
    a ray-crossing or winding-number test.
  \item[From a grid] A binary volume (a segmented CT or MRI scan, or a
    rasterized solid) is converted to a signed distance by a linear-time
    Euclidean distance transform over the grid.
  \item[From a point cloud] Oriented points $p_i$ with normals $n_i$ define a
    local tangent-plane field; $\phi$ is the signed distance to the plane
    through the nearest sample (moving least squares), the bridge between the
    raw scans of Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_point_clouds} and the
    implicit world.
\end{description}

Once $\phi$ is available, a mesh is extracted as the isosurface $\phi^{-1}(0)$
by marching cubes \cite{lorensen1987}; conversely, signed distance fields are
computed from geometry and stored on a grid or octree to answer physical
queries (penetration, proximity, closest point) directly --- the standard
interface between CAD geometry and the rendering, robotics, and simulation
stacks of this book.

\section{Level Sets and Front Propagation}
\label{sec:shape_level_sets}

\begin{definition}[Level Set]\label{def:shape_level_set}
  For a function $\phi \colon \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ and a value $c$, the
  \textbf{level set} of $\phi$ at $c$ is $L_c = \{ x : \phi(x) = c \}$. When
  $\phi$ is a signed distance function, the level sets $L_c$, $c \ne 0$, are
  parallel surfaces to $S = L_0$.
\end{definition}

\index{level set}\textbf{Level-set methods} represent the surface as a level set
of an evolving function and move the surface by evolving the function, not by
moving points on it \cite{osher2003}. The evolution is governed by the
\textbf{level-set equation}\index{level-set equation}
\[
\phi_t + F\,|\nabla \phi| = 0,
\]
where $F$ is a speed function in the normal direction, solved numerically with
upwind finite differences that keep the front sharp. The advantage over
explicit surface evolution is topological: a level set can split and merge (a
contracting figure-eight curve splits into two components), which a
parametrization cannot do without re-meshing. Level-set evolution drives shape
carving, curvature-driven fairing ($F = -\kappa$), and segmentation of medical
volumes \cite{osher2003,sethian1996}.

When the speed $F$ is everywhere of one sign, the front moves monotonically and
the arrival time $T(x)$ satisfies the \emph{Eikonal equation}
$|\nabla T| = 1/F$ with $T = 0$ on the initial front --- a static problem
solved once by the fast marching method \cite{sethian1996}.

\begin{theorem}[Fast Marching]\label{thm:shape_fast_marching}
  Let $\Gamma$ be an initial front and $F > 0$ a speed on a grid of $N$ points.
  The fast marching method computes the arrival time of $|\nabla T| = 1/F$ at
  every grid point in $O(N \log N)$ time using a heap-ordered front.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  The method maintains a heap of candidate points ordered by current arrival
  time. The smallest candidate $x$ is accepted: by monotonicity of the front no
  later candidate can lower its time. The accepted point updates its grid
  neighbors by solving the local Eikonal equation with a first-order upwind
  scheme. Each of the $N$ points is accepted once and updated a constant number
  of times, giving $N$ heap operations at $O(\log N)$ each.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Arrival Time as Distance]
  With speed $F \equiv 1$, the arrival time $T(x)$ is exactly the Euclidean
  distance from $x$ to $\Gamma$; fast marching with $F = 1$ is how
  Section~\ref{sec:shape_sdf} propagates distances into a volume grid, and the
  same scheme on a surface mesh yields geodesic distances.
\end{example}

The level-set and fast marching ideas connect the whole chapter: the signed
distance functions of Section~\ref{sec:shape_sdf} are the $F = 1$
arrival-time fields; their isosurfaces are extracted by \textbf{marching
cubes} \cite{lorensen1987}, which constructs surface triangles in each grid
cell for the 15 configurations of how a level set crosses the cell and resolves
their topological ambiguities; and the Poisson reconstruction of
Section~\ref{sec:shape_poisson_reconstruction} produces a field whose isosurface
is the output mesh.

\index{fast marching}\index{marching cubes}

\section{Poisson Surface Reconstruction}
\label{sec:shape_poisson_reconstruction}

\subsection{1. Overview}

\index{Poisson reconstruction}Poisson surface reconstruction \cite{kazhdan2006}
is an implicit method: it turns an oriented point cloud into a watertight
surface by solving a global Poisson equation rather than by filtering a
Delaunay complex. The input is a set of points $P$ with approximate outward
normals $N$; the output is a smooth indicator function whose isosurface
approximates the surface. Because the method fits one smooth field to
\emph{all} the data, it is far more robust to noise than the Delaunay-based
filters of Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_surface}, and it fills holes
and produces watertight output automatically --- the de facto standard for
reconstructing watertight models from real scans.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Indicator Function]\label{def:shape_indicator}
  For a surface $S = \partial\Omega$, the \textbf{indicator function} of
  $\Omega$ is $\chi_{\Omega}(x) = 1$ for $x \in \Omega$ and $0$ otherwise.
  Reconstructing $S$ is equivalent to reconstructing the indicator: $S$ is the
  level set $\chi = \tau$ for a threshold $\tau$ between the two sides.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Oriented Point Set]\label{def:shape_oriented_points}
  An \textbf{oriented point set} is a set of pairs $(p_i, n_i)$ where $p_i$ is
  a sample and $n_i$ a unit vector approximating the outward normal of $S$ at
  $p_i$. Normals are estimated by principal component analysis of the $k$
  nearest neighbors (the smallest eigenvector of the covariance matrix), with
  signs made globally consistent.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

The method exploits a distributional identity. For a smooth surface $S$ with
outward normal $n$ and surface measure $dA$,
\[
\nabla \chi_{\Omega} = -n(x)\,\delta_S(x),
\]
where $\delta_S$ is the surface Dirac measure: the gradient of the indicator
is a vector field supported on $S$ and pointing inward. The oriented samples
$(p_i, n_i)$ discretize this field; convolving with a smoothing kernel $F$ on
the octree of the samples gives the vector field
$V(x) = \sum_i n_i\,F(x - p_i)$, and the identity
$\nabla \chi * F = V$ says the smoothed indicator solves a Poisson equation.

\begin{theorem}[Poisson Reconstruction]\label{thm:shape_poisson}
  Let $(P, N)$ be an oriented point set sampling a smooth surface $S$, and let
  $V = \sum_i n_i F(x - p_i)$ be the induced vector field for a smoothing
  kernel $F$. Then the indicator function $\chi$ of $S$ satisfies, in the
  sense of distributions,
  \[
  \Delta \chi = \nabla \cdot V,
  \]
  and solving this equation and extracting the isosurface at the average of
  $\chi$ at the sample points yields a surface approximating $S$ to within the
  smoothing scale of $F$ \cite{kazhdan2006}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  Taking the divergence of both sides of $\nabla(\chi * F) = V$ gives
  $\Delta(\chi * F) = \nabla \cdot V$. Convolution commutes with
  differentiation and $F$ approximates the identity, so $\chi * F$ is a
  smoothed indicator function whose isosurface at the threshold
  $\tau = \chi * F(p_i)$ --- the mean value at the samples --- lies within one
  smoothing radius of $S$; the threshold interpolates the value of $\chi$
  across $S$ because the samples lie on the boundary.
\end{proof}

The practical system \cite{kazhdan2006} replaces the uniform grid by an
octree: samples and normals live in the leaves, the kernel is a trilinear hat
function scaled per cell, and the Poisson equation is solved by a multigrid
solver on the tree. The later \emph{screened} variant adds the constraint
$\chi(p_i) = 0$ at the samples, keeping the surface near the data; both share
the theory above.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Poisson Surface Reconstruction}
\label{alg:shape_poisson_reconstruction}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require oriented points $(p_i, n_i)$, octree depth $d$.
\Ensure a watertight triangle mesh approximating the sampled surface.
\Function{PoissonReconstruct}{$(P, N)$, $d$}
  \State build an octree $\mathcal{O}$ of depth $d$, subdivided so each sample lies in a distinct leaf
  \State $F \gets$ trilinear hat function on the octree cells
  \State $V(x) \gets \sum_i n_i \, F(x - p_i)$ \Comment{smoothed normal field}
  \State solve $\Delta \chi = \nabla \cdot V$ on $\mathcal{O}$ by a multigrid solver
  \State $\tau \gets \frac{1}{|P|} \sum_i \chi(p_i)$
  \State extract the isosurface $\{ x : \chi(x) = \tau \}$ by marching cubes on $\mathcal{O}$
  \State \Return the extracted triangle mesh
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Octree depth $d$}: controls resolution. Doubling $d$ halves the
    cell size; the practical range is $d = 8$ to $12$ for typical scans, chosen
    so the smallest cell matches the sample spacing. Larger $d$ captures more
    detail but amplifies noise and memory use.
  \item \textbf{Isovalue threshold}: the default $\tau$, the mean of $\chi$ at
    the samples, is robust; it can be adjusted when the sampling is biased.
  \item \textbf{Normal estimation}: the $k$-nearest-neighbor radius for normal
    estimation trades sharpness against robustness; $k$ grows with the noise
    level.
  \item \textbf{Smoothing/screening}: the screened variant's weight controls
    fidelity to the data versus smoothness; raise it for dense scans, lower it
    for sparse or very noisy data.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Time}: $O(N)$ for $N$ octree nodes with the multigrid solver of
    \cite{kazhdan2006}, where $N$ is proportional to the number of samples at a
    fixed depth; isosurface extraction is linear in the number of output
    triangles.
  \item \textbf{Space}: $O(N)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Watertight reconstruction}: turning laser and photogrammetric
    scans of objects, buildings, and terrain into closed models for 3D
    printing, inspection, and rendering.
  \item \textbf{Noisy data}: the smooth least-squares fit tolerates noise that
    destroys the Delaunay-based filters of
    Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_surface}; it is the method of choice
    when the point set is not an $\varepsilon$-sample.
  \item \textbf{Implicit interface}: the field output plugs directly into the
    signed distance framework of Section~\ref{sec:shape_sdf} and the
    learning-based pipelines of Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry_data_ml}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Watertightness}: verify the output is closed (every edge shared
    by exactly two triangles) with the Euler characteristic of the expected
    genus, using Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}.
  \item \textbf{Error measurement}: for synthetic surfaces, report the
    Hausdorff distance to the true surface; the error should decrease with
    depth until the sample spacing is resolved.
  \item \textbf{Normals orientation}: inconsistent normal signs give a
    self-canceling field and an empty or inverted reconstruction; test with a
    scan of known orientation.
  \item \textbf{Depth sensitivity}: a depth sweep should stabilize above a
    minimum depth; instability at high depth indicates noise amplification.
  \item \textbf{Holes and sparse regions}: large gaps should still reconstruct
    watertight; verify the surface across a gap is smooth with no cusps.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}

\section{Applications}
\label{sec:shape_applications}

\subsection{Scanning and Reverse Engineering}

\textbf{3D scanning} systems --- laser triangulation, structured light,
time-of-flight lidar, and photogrammetry --- produce point clouds whose
density, accuracy, and coverage vary widely. The reconstruction pipeline of
Sections~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_point_clouds} and
\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_surface} is their common back end: overlapping
scans are registered into one frame (the methods of \cite{besl1992,chen1992}),
outliers are pruned, normals are estimated, and a surface is fitted. Industrial
reverse engineering then converts the mesh into a parametric CAD model using
the patch machinery of Section~\ref{sec:shape_representation_implicit}. The
algorithm follows the data: ball pivoting \cite{bernardini1999} is fastest and
fits uniformly sampled mechanical parts; the cocone \cite{amenta2000} and the
crust \cite{amentabern1999} reconstruct provably correct surfaces from
feature-adaptive samples; Poisson reconstruction \cite{kazhdan2006} is the
robust default for noisy or incomplete scans.

\subsection{Surface Reconstruction}

In medical imaging, segmented CT and MRI volumes are converted to signed
distance functions (Section~\ref{sec:shape_sdf}) and polygonized by marching
cubes \cite{lorensen1987} into the anatomical surfaces used for planning,
simulation, and implant design; the level-set machinery of
Section~\ref{sec:shape_level_sets} drives the segmentation itself. Tomographic
and lidar acquisitions of fossils, terrain, and biological specimens follow the
same pipeline: the sampling condition of
Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_point_clouds} tells the operator the
density needed to resolve a feature of a given size, and the topological
checks of Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology} certify the output.

\subsection{Terrain Modeling}

Airborne lidar produces dense height samples of the ground and of buildings.
Terrain reconstruction is the two-dimensional specialization: the samples are
projected to the plane, outliers (vegetation, vehicles) are removed, and the
Delaunay triangulation of the projection becomes the triangulated irregular
network (TIN) backing digital elevation models, contouring, and visibility
analysis \cite{deberg2008}. Breaklines and rivers enter as constraints of the
constrained Delaunay triangulation, and the meshing machinery of
Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation} refines the TIN for flood and watershed
simulation; see also Section~\ref{sec:voronoi_applications}.

\subsection{Practical Tradeoffs}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fidelity versus watertightness}: the Delaunay-based filters
    follow the data closely but leave holes and artifacts; the implicit methods
    fill holes at the cost of smoothing sharp features. Watertight output is
    mandatory for 3D printing, CFD, and FEM.
  \item \textbf{Speed}: ball pivoting is $O(n)$ expected and fastest on uniform
    scans; the Delaunay pipeline costs $O(n \log n)$ expected with $O(n^2)$
    worst case (Theorem~\ref{thm:high_dim_size}); Poisson reconstruction is
    linear in the octree size with a larger constant.
  \item \textbf{Robustness}: the implicit least-squares methods
    (Sections~\ref{sec:shape_sdf} and \ref{sec:shape_poisson_reconstruction})
    degrade gracefully under noise; the Delaunay methods degrade quickly unless
    the noise is removed first.
  \item \textbf{Representation downstream}: reconstructed meshes feed the
    simulation stacks of Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}, distance fields
    feed the collision and robotics algorithms, and all three standard input
    representations feed the geometry-aware machine-learning pipelines of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry_data_ml}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:shape_exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Let $S$ be a surface and $P$ an $\varepsilon$-sample of $S$. Prove
    from the Lipschitz property of the local feature size
    (Definition~\ref{def:shape_lfs}) that $P$ is an $r$-sample for
    $r = \varepsilon / (1 - \varepsilon)$, and give an example where the two
    conditions differ by a constant factor.
  \item Compute the medial axis and the local feature size of a rectangle
    (compare Example~\ref{ex:medial_axis_rectangle}) and of an annulus, and
    describe the distribution of sample density an $\varepsilon$-sample must
    have at the corners, on the long edges, and near the inner hole.
  \item For samples of the unit circle, compute the Voronoi diagram, the poles
    of each cell, and the pole vectors by hand; verify that each pole vector
    points to the center and that the 2D crust
    (Algorithm~\ref{alg:shape_crust} restricted to edges) recovers the convex
    hull of the samples.
  \item Simulate the ball-pivoting algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg:shape_ball_pivoting})
    by hand on eight points evenly spaced around a circle, with a ball radius
    equal to the chord length, and count the pivoting steps; repeat with a
    smaller ball and explain where the front stalls.
  \item Let $P$ be an $\varepsilon$-sample and $\theta = \arcsin(\varepsilon)$
    the cocone angle. Show that the cocone $C(p)$ of
    Definition~\ref{def:shape_cocone} contains the ball of radius
    $\varepsilon \cdot \operatorname{lfs}(p)$ centered at $p$, and explain why
    this guarantees that the circumcenter of every triangle of the restricted
    Delaunay complex lies in the cocone of its vertices.
  \item Implement the signed distance function of a triangle mesh on a voxel
    grid (Section~\ref{sec:shape_sdf}) using fast marching
    (Theorem~\ref{thm:shape_fast_marching}), and verify the Eikonal condition
    $|\nabla \phi| = 1$ away from the surface.
  \item Implement the level-set evolution $\phi_t + F|\nabla \phi| = 0$ for
    $F \equiv 1$ (distance) and for $F = -\kappa$ (mean-curvature flow), and
    verify that a non-convex curve splits into two components under the second
    flow, demonstrating why level sets handle topology change.
  \item Reconstruct the same synthetic point cloud of a sphere, a torus, and a
    bumpy surface with (i) the alpha complex, (ii) the cocone, and (iii)
    Poisson reconstruction; add Gaussian noise of increasing amplitude and
    report for each method the watertightness, the genus, and the Hausdorff
    distance to the true surface.
\end{enumerate}
