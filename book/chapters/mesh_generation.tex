\chapter{Mesh Generation}
\label{ch:mesh_generation}

\section{Computational Meshes}
\label{sec:meshgen_intro}

A \textbf{computational mesh}\index{mesh} is a discretization of a geometric
domain into simple cells --- triangles and quadrilaterals in the plane,
tetrahedra, hexahedra, and prisms in space --- on which numerical methods
(finite elements, finite volumes, boundary elements) compute approximate
solutions of partial differential equations. Mesh generation is the
algorithmic discipline that constructs such meshes automatically from a
geometric description of the domain: a polygon, a polyhedron, or a CAD
boundary representation, possibly with prescribed mesh sizes near features
and a quality requirement on the cells.

The quality of a mesh directly controls the accuracy and stability of the
numerical solution: poorly shaped cells (very small angles, slivers) inflate
the discretization error and the condition number of the assembled linear
system. Mesh generation therefore balances three competing goals:
\textbf{conformity} (the mesh reproduces the domain boundary and any
prescribed features exactly), \textbf{quality} (every cell satisfies a
geometric quality bound, e.g., a minimum angle), and \textbf{efficiency}
(the number of cells is as small as the size field allows). These goals,
their algorithmic resolutions, and their analysis are the subject of this
chapter.

Two families of methods dominate practice. \textbf{Delaunay-based} methods
(Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}) build the mesh as a Delaunay
triangulation refined by inserting points at circumcenters of bad cells;
they dominate the triangulated meshes of scientific computing
(Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_refinement}). \textbf{Advancing-front} methods
grow the mesh inward from the boundary (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_advancing_front}).
Both are supported by the mesh-analysis machinery of
Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}, which verifies the validity and quality of
the output.

\section{Simplicial Complexes}
\label{sec:meshgen_simplicial}

\begin{definition}[Simplicial Complex]\label{def:meshgen_simplicial_complex}
  A \textbf{simplicial complex} $K$ is a finite collection of simplices
  (vertices, edges, triangles, tetrahedra) such that (i) every face of a
  simplex of $K$ is in $K$, and (ii) the intersection of any two simplices
  of $K$ is a face of both. The \textbf{underlying space} $|K|$ is the
  union of the simplices.
\end{definition}

Simplicial complexes are the abstract combinatorial skeleton of a mesh: a
triangular mesh is a 2D simplicial complex whose underlying space
approximates the domain, and a tetrahedral mesh is a 3D simplicial complex.
The axioms guarantee that cells meet only in shared faces --- there are no
hanging vertices or cracked edges --- which is exactly the topological
validity that finite-element assembly requires. Two meshes are related by
\textbf{refinement} if one is obtained from the other by subdividing
simplices; a \textbf{conforming} refinement keeps the complex a
simplicial complex after every subdivision, which is what makes adaptive
meshing (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_adaptive}) possible.

The half-edge data structure of Chapter~\ref{ch:half_edge} stores a 2D
simplicial complex with adjacency queries in $O(1)$; the combinatorial
machinery of Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology} (Euler characteristic,
Betti numbers) checks that a mesh is a manifold of the intended genus.
These validity checks are run as part of mesh generation's testing phase
(Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_project}).

\index{simplicial complex}\index{underlying space}

\section{Triangular and Tetrahedral Meshes}
\label{sec:meshgen_tri_tet}

The two most common mesh types are the \textbf{triangular mesh} (2D, or a
surface triangulation in 3D) and the \textbf{tetrahedral mesh} (3D
volumetric). A triangular mesh of a polygonal domain conforms to the domain
if every boundary edge of the domain is a union of mesh edges; a
tetrahedral mesh of a polyhedron conforms if the boundary triangles exactly
cover the domain's faces. The mesh is \textbf{simplicial} if all cells are
simplices, which is the case here; quadrilateral and hexahedral meshes
require the different machinery of structured and block-structured methods
and are not treated in this chapter.

The generation pipeline is the same for both dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Input}: a domain described by its boundary (a polygon with
    holes, a CAD boundary representation, or a point cloud with boundary
    information);
  \item \textbf{Initial triangulation}: a coarse conforming triangulation of
    the domain, typically a constrained Delaunay triangulation of the
    boundary (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_constrained});
  \item \textbf{Size field}: a function that prescribes the desired cell
    size at each point, derived from feature sizes, curvature, or a
    prescribed grading (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_adaptive});
  \item \textbf{Refinement}: inserting points until every cell satisfies
    the size and quality requirements (Sections~\ref{sec:meshgen_refinement}
    --\ref{sec:meshgen_chew});
  \item \textbf{Optimization}: smoothing and edge flips to improve quality
    beyond the guarantee (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_optimization});
  \item \textbf{Output}: the mesh in a standard format (e.g., Gmsh's MSH
    format, or the half-edge/triangle-index structures of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:half_edge}).
\end{enumerate}
The Delaunay triangulation of Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_delaunay} is the
central building block of the most widely used pipeline, implemented in
production meshers such as TetGen \cite{si2015} and Gmsh \cite{geuzaine2009}.

\index{triangular mesh}\index{tetrahedral mesh}

\section{Mesh Quality Measures}
\label{sec:meshgen_quality}

The \textbf{quality} of a triangle or tetrahedron measures how far the cell
is from equilateral; the numerical stability of finite-element assembly
degrades with poor quality. The standard measures are:

\begin{description}
  \item[Minimum angle] For a triangle, the minimum interior angle. The
    fundamental bound of this chapter is that every angle is at least
    $30^\circ$ (Delaunay refinement with the smallest-angle rule), because
    the discretization error in the finite-element method is inversely
    proportional to $\sin(\text{minimal angle})$.
  \item[Aspect ratio] The ratio of the circumradius to the inradius; $2$
    for an equilateral triangle and $\infty$ for a degenerate one. A bound
    on the aspect ratio is equivalent to a bound on the minimum angle.
  \item[Radius--edge ratio] The ratio $\rho = R / \ell_{\min}$ of the
    circumradius $R$ to the shortest edge $\ell_{\min}$. A triangle with
    $\rho$ bounded by $\rho^*$ has all angles bounded away from $0$ and
    $180^\circ$; this is the quantity that Ruppert's algorithm controls
    directly (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_ruppert}).
  \item[Sliver measure (3D)] A tetrahedron whose four vertices are nearly
    coplanar but whose edges are long; slivers have small volume-to-edge
    ratios and pass the angle tests that fail triangles. Sliver exudation
    removes them by vertex perturbation (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_optimization}).
\end{description}

The relationships among measures matter for algorithm design: bounding
$\rho$ bounds all angles for triangles, but in 3D no single edge-based
ratio bounds the sliver shape, which is why tetrahedral Delaunay refinement
has weaker guarantees than the 2D theory. Quality is measured and reported
by the mesh-analysis routines of Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}, which
compute histograms of these measures over the mesh.

\index{aspect ratio}\index{radius-edge ratio}\index{sliver}

\section{Delaunay Mesh Generation}
\label{sec:meshgen_delaunay}

Delaunay mesh generation builds the mesh as (a constrained)
\textbf{Delaunay triangulation}\index{Delaunay triangulation} of the domain
and its inserted points. Recall from Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}
that a triangulation is Delaunay if every triangle's circumcircle contains
no point of the triangulation in its interior (the \emph{empty-circumcircle
property}, Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_empty_circumcircle}); equivalently, no
edge is \emph{illegal} and flipping illegal edges terminates at the Delaunay
triangulation (Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping}). The Delaunay
triangulation maximizes the minimum angle over all triangulations of the
same point set, which makes it the natural starting point for quality mesh
generation: it is already the best possible on the given points, and
refinement only improves it further.

Mesh generation uses the Delaunay machinery in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{As the initial mesh}: the boundary of the domain is
    triangulated by a constrained Delaunay triangulation
    (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_constrained});
  \item \textbf{As the refinement engine}: inserting a new point into a
    Delaunay triangulation requires locating its enclosing triangle (by the
    walking methods of Section~\ref{sec:pointloc_triangulations} or the
    history DAG of Section~\ref{sec:pointloc_search_dags}), splitting the
    triangle, and legalizing edges by flips. Each insertion is a local
    operation, so the mesh is updated in expected $O(\log n)$ time.
\end{itemize}
The theory of Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_randomized} (expected $O(n\log n)$
construction) and Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_refinement} (angle guarantees)
therefore transfers directly to mesh generation, and the practical
implementations in TetGen \cite{si2015} and Gmsh \cite{geuzaine2009} are
direct descendants of these algorithms.

\section{Constrained Delaunay Meshing}
\label{sec:meshgen_constrained}

A mesh must reproduce the domain's boundary exactly, but an ordinary
Delaunay triangulation of the boundary vertices can contain edges that
cross the boundary or lie outside the domain. The \textbf{constrained
Delaunay triangulation (CDT)}\index{constrained Delaunay triangulation}
fixes this: a set of constraint edges (the domain boundary and any
prescribed internal features such as material interfaces) is forced into
the triangulation, and every triangle must have the empty-circumcircle
property \emph{relative to the constraints}: its circumcircle contains no
vertex that is \emph{visible} from the triangle's interior, where the line
of sight must not cross a constraint. This is the definition developed in
Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_constrained}.

The CDT of the boundary provides the initial conforming mesh from which
refinement proceeds. The constrained edges are the domain's boundary
segments, and the refinement process (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_refinement})
never removes them: points are inserted only in the interior of cells (or
on constrained edges in a way that preserves the boundary, by splitting the
edge into two constrained pieces). Guaranteeing that the boundary is
reproduced \emph{exactly} requires exact arithmetic on the input
coordinates; floating-point snap is not acceptable, and the robust
predicates of Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_predicates} are used throughout.

For a polyhedron, the 3D constrained Delaunay triangulation is
considerably harder than the 2D CDT because the boundary is a surface
triangulation and the empty-circumsphere condition is more delicate; the
practical resolution used by TetGen \cite{si2015} is to start from a
boundary conforming Delaunay triangulation of the input's vertices and then
enforce the boundary faces, using Shewchuk's boundary-conforming algorithms
\cite{si2015}.

\index{constrained Delaunay triangulation}

\section{Delaunay Refinement}
\label{sec:meshgen_refinement}

\subsection{1. Overview}

\textbf{Delaunay refinement}\index{Delaunay refinement} is the process of
improving a Delaunay triangulation by inserting a new point at the
\textbf{circumcenter} of any cell that fails the size or quality
requirements, and legalizing the affected cells. The two classical
algorithms are Chew's (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_chew}) and Ruppert's
(Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_ruppert}), analyzed in the survey of Shewchuk
\cite{shewchuk2002}; together they show that a Delaunay refinement process
that inserts circumcenters of cells whose radius--edge ratio exceeds a
threshold terminates and produces a mesh whose minimum angle is bounded by
a constant --- $30^\circ$ being the canonical bound.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Skinny Triangle]\label{def:meshgen_skinny}
  A triangle is \textbf{skinny} if its radius--edge ratio $\rho = R/\ell_{\min}$
  exceeds a threshold $\rho^*$ (equivalently, if some angle is too small).
  Its \textbf{circumcenter} is a candidate insertion point.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

The power of circumcenter insertion is a locality argument. Inserting a
point at the circumcenter of a skinny triangle subdivides that triangle into
three smaller triangles, each with radius--edge ratio at most that of the
parent (the circumradius halves while the shortest edge stays bounded
below). The danger is that the insertion point lies \emph{outside} the
domain (it is a boundary or a split of a constrained edge), in which case
the point is inserted on the midpoint of the offending edge instead. The
termination and quality proofs control the accumulation of short edges:
Ruppert's algorithm \cite{ruppert1995} shows that if the threshold is
$\rho^* < 2$ and every boundary segment is \emph{encroached} (its
diametral circle contains another vertex), then the process terminates and
the output mesh has no angle smaller than $\arcsin(\sqrt{3}/(2\rho^*))$
--- at least $20.7^\circ$ for $\rho^* = 2$, and $30^\circ$ for the variant
that refines skinny and encroached cells together.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Delaunay Refinement (Ruppert--Chew style)}
\label{alg:meshgen_refinement}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A constrained Delaunay triangulation of the domain, quality threshold $\rho^*$.
\Ensure A conforming mesh with no triangle of radius--edge ratio $> \rho^*$.
\Function{DelaunayRefine}{$\mathcal{T}$, $\rho^*$}
  \State $Q \gets$ priority queue of skinny triangles and encroached boundary segments
  \While{$Q$ is not empty}
    \State pop the highest-priority item
    \If{it is an encroached segment $e$}
      \State insert the midpoint of $e$; split $e$ into two constrained pieces; legalize
    \ElsIf{the circumcenter $c$ of skinny triangle $t$ encroaches a segment $e$}
      \State insert the midpoint of $e$ instead of $c$; legalize
    \Else
      \State insert $c$; legalize the affected edges by flips
    \EndIf
    \State re-evaluate the quality of all affected cells and update $Q$
  \EndWhile
  \State \Return $\mathcal{T}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Threshold $\rho^*$}: smaller $\rho^*$ gives better angles but
  more cells; the practical sweet spot is $\rho^* = 2$ (angle bound
  $20.7^\circ$) to $2\sqrt{2}$.
\item \textbf{Priority order}: processing encroached segments before
  circumcenters (Chew's ``split the worst first'') is required for the
  termination proof.
\item \textbf{Size field}: refinement stops when cells satisfy both the
  quality bound and the size field of Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_adaptive};
  the two criteria are combined into one priority.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ expected for a mesh of $n$ cells, since
  each point insertion is an expected-$O(\log n)$ Delaunay update.
\item \textbf{Size}: for a domain whose features are resolved, the number
  of cells is proportional to the size-field integral; the constants depend
  on the angle bound.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Finite-element meshing}: the default engine of production
  triangular and tetrahedral meshers (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_fem}).
\item \textbf{Guaranteed-quality requirements}: applications that must bound
  the worst element (e.g., FEM error control, computational fluid dynamics
  meshes) use Delaunay refinement precisely because of its angle guarantee.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Angle check}: after refinement, verify numerically that no
  angle is below the promised bound, using the quality measures of
  Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_quality}.
\item \textbf{Encroachment}: deliberately construct an input where a
  circumcenter encroaches a boundary segment and verify the midpoint split.
\item \textbf{Small features}: boundaries with features closer than the
  requested size must be resolved to their feature size first.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

The refinement algorithms are due to Chew \cite{chew1989} and Ruppert
\cite{ruppert1995}; the complete analysis and engineering guidance are in
Shewchuk's survey \cite{shewchuk2002} and thesis \cite{shewchuk1998}, and
the Delaunay background in Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}.

\section{Ruppert's Algorithm}
\label{sec:meshgen_ruppert}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Ruppert's algorithm \cite{ruppert1995} is the canonical Delaunay refinement
method with a provable angle guarantee. It maintains a constrained Delaunay
triangulation and refines it by inserting circumcenters of skinny triangles
and midpoints of \emph{encroached} boundary segments, in a priority order
that guarantees termination. The output has all angles at least a fixed
bound (about $20.7^\circ$ for the standard parameter) and respects the
domain boundary exactly.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Encroachment]\label{def:meshgen_encroachment}
  A boundary segment $e$ is \textbf{encroached} if some vertex of the
  triangulation lies in the interior of the circle with diameter $e$.
  Inserting a vertex into the circle with diameter $e$ can make the Delaunay
  triangulation lose $e$ as an edge, so encroached segments must be split at
  their midpoint first.
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

The correctness rests on two lemmas proved in \cite{ruppert1995,
shewchuk2002}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Termination}: every inserted point is at distance at least
    $\ell_{\min} \cdot \rho^* / (\rho^* + 1)$ from every existing vertex
    (a separation bound), so the refinement inserts only finitely many
    points and the mesh size is bounded in terms of the smallest feature.
  \item \textbf{Quality}: after termination, no triangle has radius--edge
    ratio exceeding $\rho^*$; for $\rho^* = 2$ this implies no angle
    smaller than $\arcsin(\sqrt{3}/4) \approx 20.7^\circ$. The
    ``smallest angle'' refinement variant (inserting the circumcenter with
    the rule that the new triangle angles are at least $30^\circ$) gives
    the $30^\circ$ bound.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

The algorithm is the specialization of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:meshgen_refinement} with the priority rule ``always
split an encroached segment before inserting any circumcenter'', i.e., the
Chew--Ruppert order in which a skinny triangle is processed only if its
circumcenter is not encroaching a segment.

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Angle bound}: the angle guarantee improves with the choice of
  $\rho^*$; the theory is cleanest at $\rho^* = 2$.
\item \textbf{Size field}: Ruppert's algorithm was originally analyzed
  without a size field; combining it with the graded size field of
  Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_adaptive} preserves the angle bound and the
  linear size behavior.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ expected, dominated by the Delaunay
  insertions.
\item \textbf{Size}: $O(n)$ cells, with constants that grow as the angle
  bound tightens.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Guaranteed-quality 2D meshing}: the reference algorithm
  implemented in Triangle and in the planar meshers of Gmsh
  \cite{geuzaine2009,shewchuk2002}.
\item \textbf{Conforming boundary meshes}: the exact boundary reproduction
  makes it the tool for FEM and CFD preprocessing (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_fem}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Angle histogram}: assert the promised minimum angle over
  thousands of random domains.
\item \textbf{Boundary reproduction}: verify every input boundary segment is
  a union of output edges.
\item \textbf{Encroachment regression}: construct domains whose boundary
  segments are longer than the local feature and verify midpoint splitting.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

Ruppert \cite{ruppert1995} introduced the algorithm; Shewchuk
\cite{shewchuk2002} presents the complete proof of termination and the
angle bounds, and the engineering details \cite{shewchuk1998}.

\section{Chew's Algorithm}
\label{sec:meshgen_chew}

\subsection{1. Overview}

Chew's algorithm \cite{chew1989} is the earliest Delaunay refinement method
with a quality guarantee. It maintains a constrained Delaunay triangulation
and repeatedly splits the \textbf{worst} triangle: the triangle with the
largest circumradius--shortest-edge ratio is refined by inserting its
circumcenter. Chew proved that this process converges to a mesh in which
every angle is at least $30^\circ$ when a careful tie-breaking is used; the
algorithm is the prototype of the priority-queue refinement implemented in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:meshgen_refinement}.

\subsection{2. Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Worst Triangle]\label{def:meshgen_worst}
  The \textbf{worst triangle} of a triangulation is the triangle whose
  radius--edge ratio is maximal. Chew's algorithm always refines the worst
  triangle, resolving ties by a fixed rule (e.g., by edge length).
\end{definition}

\subsection{3. Theory}

The worst-first rule is what makes the analysis simple: refining the
worst triangle at every step guarantees that no triangle ever becomes worse
than the current worst, so the worst ratio decreases monotonically toward
the bound. Chew's proof shows the process terminates with no angle below
$30^\circ$ on inputs with well-separated features; Ruppert's refinement
(Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_ruppert}) extended the guarantee to arbitrary
inputs by adding the encroachment rule.

\subsection{4. Pseudocode}

The pseudocode is Algorithm~\ref{alg:meshgen_refinement} restricted to the
single rule ``pop the worst triangle $t$; insert its circumcenter unless it
encroaches a segment, in which case split the segment''.

\subsection{5. Parameter Selection}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Worst-first priority}: the priority is the radius--edge
  ratio; the deterministic tie-break matters for reproducibility.
\item \textbf{Degenerate resolution}: when the circumcenter is ill-defined
  (nearly collinear cells), refine by the longest edge instead.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Time}: $O(n \log n)$ expected with a heap-based priority
  queue of $O(n)$ triangles.
\item \textbf{Size}: linear in the output mesh size.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Theoretic basis}: the ``worst-first'' scheme is the template
  for guaranteed-quality refinement in two and three dimensions.
\item \textbf{Implementation default}: many meshers use worst-first with
  the $30^\circ$ target as the practical default refinement policy.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{8. Testing Methods and Edge Cases}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Bound verification}: assert the $30^\circ$ angle bound on
  structured and unstructured test domains.
\item \textbf{Infinite loop guard}: verify that the priority queue drains on
  domains with tiny features (the separation bound of
  Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_ruppert} is what guarantees termination).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{9. References}

Chew's original result \cite{chew1989} and its placement in the refinement
theory by Shewchuk \cite{shewchuk2002}.

\section{Advancing-Front Methods}
\label{sec:meshgen_advancing_front}

\textbf{Advancing-front methods}\index{advancing-front} generate the mesh
by growing cells inward from the domain boundary: the boundary is first
discretized into a set of front edges, and new triangles (or tetrahedra)
are created by connecting front vertices, one cell at a time, with the
front advancing into the domain until it collapses. The method is the
main non-Delaunay approach and is favored in certain settings:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Boundary fidelity}: cells start at the boundary, so the
    mesh matches the input surface precisely and can align cells with
    anisotropic directions on the boundary.
  \item \textbf{Anisotropy}: the local cell shapes are controlled directly
    by the front construction, which suits anisotropic boundary-layer
    meshes in computational fluid dynamics.
  \item \textbf{No global structure}: no global Delaunay machinery is
    needed, which simplifies implementations for domains with many
    disconnected boundary components.
\end{itemize}

Its weaknesses are equally well known. The quality guarantee is local and
empirical rather than provable; the front can ``collapse'' poorly near
internal boundaries; and the element count is not controlled by a size
field as cleanly as in Delaunay refinement. In practice the two families
are often combined: an advancing-front method generates the boundary layer
and the coarse interior, and a Delaunay refinement pass cleans up the
interior cells (as in Gmsh's default pipeline \cite{geuzaine2009}).
Simpler but robust alternatives for many applications are the
distmesh-style physically motivated generators \cite{persson2004}, which
relax an initial triangulation under forces derived from the desired cell
size, and the Delaunay refinement methods of Sections
\ref{sec:meshgen_refinement}--\ref{sec:meshgen_chew}.

\index{advancing front}

\section{Adaptive Meshing}
\label{sec:meshgen_adaptive}

\textbf{Adaptive meshing}\index{adaptive meshing} generates a mesh whose
cell size varies over the domain according to a \textbf{size field}: small
cells where the solution (or the geometry) changes rapidly, large cells
where it is smooth. The size field is prescribed either a priori from
geometric features (curvature, thin regions, feature edges) or a posteriori
from a computed error estimator of the numerical solution --- the
loop that iterates ``solve, estimate error, adapt mesh, solve again''
is the \textbf{adaptive finite-element method}.

The two standard adaptation mechanisms are:
\begin{description}
  \item[$h$-refinement] Subdividing cells where the size field demands
    smaller cells; the subdivision must be \textbf{conforming} (the result
    remains a simplicial complex, Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_simplicial}),
    which is enforced by propagating subdivisions across faces and, for
    tetrahedra, by the standard (newest-vertex or longest-edge) refinement
    templates.
  \item[Regeneration] Rebuilding the mesh with the new size field, which is
    how the Delaunay refinement methods of
    Sections~\ref{sec:meshgen_refinement}--\ref{sec:meshgen_chew} adapt:
    the size field enters as a second stopping criterion, and the insertion
    of circumcenters proceeds until every cell satisfies both the quality
    and the size bounds.
\end{description}

The efficiency guarantee of adaptive Delaunay refinement is that the final
mesh size is bounded by the size-field integral (a consequence of the
separation bound), so the mesh does not overshoot the requested sizes even
under rapid grading. Verifying that the computed mesh respects the size
field is part of the testing protocol of Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_project}.

\index{adaptive meshing}\index{size field}\index{h-refinement}

\section{Mesh Optimization}
\label{sec:meshgen_optimization}

The refinement methods of Sections~\ref{sec:meshgen_refinement}--\ref{sec:meshgen_chew}
guarantee a worst-case angle bound but not the best achievable quality.
\textbf{Mesh optimization}\index{mesh optimization} improves the mesh
beyond the guarantee by local operations that preserve the conforming
boundary:

\begin{description}
  \item[Edge flips] In 2D, an interior edge shared by two triangles is
    flipped to the other diagonal when the flip increases the minimum angle
    (the Delaunay legality test of Section~\ref{sec:delaunay_edge_flipping});
    repeatedly flipping illegal edges converges to the Delaunay
    triangulation.
  \item[Laplacian smoothing] Each interior vertex is moved to the average
    of its neighbors' positions; this usually improves the angles, but it
    can invert cells, so the move is accepted only if it improves a quality
    measure (constrained smoothing).
  \item[Optimization-based smoothing] Each vertex is moved to the position
    maximizing the minimum angle (or minimizing the maximum
    radius--edge ratio) over its incident cells, solved by a small local
    nonlinear optimization; this is the strongest local smoother.
  \item[Sliver exudation (3D)] Slivers (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_quality})
    are removed by perturbing their vertices slightly, keeping the
    Delaunay condition while destroying the near-coplanarity; sliver
    exudation is what turns a merely angle-bounded tetrahedral mesh into a
    numerically acceptable one \cite{si2015}.
\end{description}

Optimization is a postprocess: its moves must never violate the domain
boundary (boundary vertices are fixed), must never invert a cell, and must
be idempotent for reproducibility. The quality measures of
Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_quality} and the validity checks of
Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis} are the acceptance criteria.

\section{Finite-Element Applications}
\label{sec:meshgen_fem}

The meshes built in this chapter are the discretization layer of the
\textbf{finite-element method}\index{finite element method} (FEM) and the
finite-volume and finite-difference methods. The FEM on a triangular mesh
approximates a PDE solution by continuous piecewise-linear functions on the
triangles; the accuracy of the approximation depends on the mesh through
two mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \textbf{interpolation error} is bounded in terms of the
    circumradius of each cell and the second derivatives of the true
    solution, so small cells (the size field) control accuracy;
  \item the \textbf{stability} is controlled by the minimal angle, which
    appears in the constants of the error bounds and in the condition
    number of the stiffness matrix, so the angle guarantee of
    Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_refinement} controls robustness.
\end{itemize}
This is the precise reason mesh generation and mesh analysis
(Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}) are inseparable: the quality measures of
Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_quality} are exactly the quantities that FEM
analysis cites, and the adaptive loop of Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_adaptive}
drives the accuracy of industrial simulations. The production meshers used
in FEM workflows --- Gmsh \cite{geuzaine2009} for mesh generation and
visualization, and TetGen \cite{si2015} for guaranteed-quality tetrahedral
meshes --- implement the algorithms of this chapter, and their interfaces
are the de facto standards for exchanging meshes with solvers.

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:meshgen_exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Prove that the minimum angle of a triangle is a decreasing function
    of its radius--edge ratio, and give the exact relation.
  \item Show that inserting the circumcenter of a triangle subdivides it
    into three triangles whose radius--edge ratios are at most the parent's.
  \item Define encroachment and prove that inserting a point strictly
    inside the diametral circle of a segment can destroy the segment's
    presence in the Delaunay triangulation.
  \item Simulate Ruppert's algorithm by hand on a right triangle whose
    hypotenuse is a boundary segment longer than the other edges, and
    verify the termination rule.
  \item Compare the angle guarantees of Ruppert's and Chew's algorithms and
    state which parameter choices produce a $30^\circ$ bound.
  \item Explain why the 3D Delaunay refinement analysis fails for slivers
    and how sliver exudation repairs the problem.
  \item Implement a conforming adaptive $h$-refinement for a 2D mesh with a
    prescribed size field, and verify the conforming property (no hanging
    vertices) after each step.
  \item Run a mesh-quality histogram (Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}) on
    the output of Algorithm~\ref{alg:meshgen_refinement} for a random
    polygon and confirm the angle bound numerically.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Project: Adaptive Mesh Generator}
\label{sec:meshgen_project}

Build an adaptive triangular mesh generator for planar domains:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Input}: read a simple polygon with holes and a size field
    (a function of position, e.g., $h(x,y)$ small near features).
  \item \textbf{Initial mesh}: compute the constrained Delaunay
    triangulation of the boundary (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_constrained}).
  \item \textbf{Refinement}: implement
    Algorithm~\ref{alg:meshgen_refinement} with a priority queue over
    radius--edge ratio and size violation, inserting circumcenters and
    splitting encroached boundary segments.
  \item \textbf{Quality controls}: verify every angle against the promised
    bound and every edge length against the size field, and report the
    histograms using the quality measures of Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_quality}.
  \item \textbf{Optimization}: add constrained Laplacian smoothing
    (Section~\ref{sec:meshgen_optimization}) and measure the quality
    improvement.
  \item \textbf{Verification}: for a known domain (square, annulus),
    check the cell count against the size-field integral and check the mesh
    with the validity routines of Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}.
  \item \textbf{Visualization}: draw the mesh, color cells by radius--edge
    ratio, and animate the refinement steps.
\end{enumerate}
The project mirrors the pipeline of Gmsh and TetGen at small scale and
exercises the Delaunay, point-location, and mesh-analysis machinery of the
corresponding chapters.
