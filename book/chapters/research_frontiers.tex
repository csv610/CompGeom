\chapter{Research Frontiers}
\label{ch:research_frontiers}

This chapter surveys the active frontiers of computational geometry: the areas
where the questions are open, the models are being renegotiated, and the
textbook guarantees no longer apply directly. The common thread is that real
data is massive, imprecise, moving, or generated by a learned model, while
real hardware is hierarchical and real users demand certified answers.

\section{Robust and Certified Geometry}
\label{sec:frontiers_robust}

Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_predicates} develops the machinery of exact
predicates, adaptive arithmetic, and simulation of simplicity that turns
geometric algorithms into reliable software. The frontier begins where that
chapter stops: extending certification beyond single predicates, and coping
with input that is itself uncertain.

A robust \emph{predicate} certifies a sign; a robust \emph{algorithm} certifies
a combinatorial structure. Even with exact orientation and in-circle tests, an
incremental algorithm can produce an inconsistent triangulation if its
tie-breaking rules are not aligned across predicates, and a kinetic or dynamic
algorithm can loop if a failure event is mishandled. Kettner, Mehlhorn, Pion,
Schirra, and Yap collected small regression inputs---such as a convex-hull
routine whose output topology changes under rotation---that now serve as a
standard test suite for libraries such as CGAL~\cite{kettner2008}.

\begin{definition}[Exact Geometric Computation]\label{def:frontiers_egc}
The \emph{exact geometric computation} (EGC) paradigm separates predicate
evaluation from object construction: every predicate is computed exactly, and a
constructed object such as an intersection point is represented by an exact
expression evaluated to a certified precision on demand~\cite{yap1997,halperin2004}.
\end{definition}

EGC converts robustness into a cost question: how many bits must an expression
evaluation carry so that downstream predicates remain exact? Precision-driven
evaluation answers this by evaluating each expression to successively higher
precision until a requested error bound is met (Section~\ref{sec:exact_arithmetic}).

\index{imprecise data}\index{uncertainty}\index{certified computation}A second
line of work treats \emph{data imprecision}: the input is not a set of points
but a set of regions, each guaranteed to contain one unknown true point.

\begin{definition}[Imprecise Point]\label{def:frontiers_imprecise_point}
An \emph{imprecise point} is a connected region $\mathcal{R} \subset
\mathbb{R}^d$ (for example an axis-aligned square, a disk, or a segment) known
to contain the true point, whose exact location is unspecified. The set of all
inputs obtained by choosing one point from each region is the
\emph{uncertainty set}.
\end{definition}

Given imprecise points, one studies the \emph{possible} values of geometric
measures. For the diameter, width, closest pair, smallest enclosing circle, and
bounding box, Loffler and van Kreveld give efficient algorithms for the largest
and smallest possible values when the regions are squares or disks, and prove
hardness for variants such as the largest possible width under segment
imprecision~\cite{loffler2010largest}. The analogous questions for convex hulls
are polynomial for squares but NP-hard for some segment
models~\cite{loffler2010hulls}. The frontier combines exact predicates on
uncertain regions, randomized robustness certificates, and near-float
algorithms whose combinatorial output is certified with probability $1 -
\delta$.

\section{Massive Geometric Data Sets}
\label{sec:frontiers_massive_data}

Modern geometric inputs---lidar sweeps, sensor networks, simulation output,
and transaction logs---routinely contain hundreds of millions of points. Three
ideas make such data sets tractable: output-sensitive algorithms, parameterized
complexity bounds, and sparse geometric structures.

\index{output-sensitive algorithm}An \emph{output-sensitive} algorithm runs in
time proportional to the size of its output. The canonical example is computing
a convex hull in time $O(n \log h)$, where $h$ is the number of hull vertices,
achieved by Chan's algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:chans_algorithm}); when the
shape is simple, $h \ll n$ and the improvement over the worst-case $O(n \log
n)$ is dramatic~\cite{berg2008}.

\begin{theorem}[Output-Sensitive Hulls]\label{thm:frontiers_output_sensitive}
The convex hull of $n$ points in the plane can be computed in $O(n \log h)$
time, where $h$ is the number of hull vertices, and this is optimal in the
comparison model; in $\mathbb{R}^3$ the hull can be computed in $O(n \log n)$
time, matching the worst-case output size~\cite{berg2008}.
\end{theorem}

The same philosophy extends to \emph{parameterized} bounds, where the running
time is expressed as a function of both $n$ and an independent parameter such
as the number of reflex vertices, the treewidth of an underlying graph, or the
number of outliers, so that near-linear behavior is provable on the structured
instances that arise in practice.

\index{spanner}\index{well-separated pair decomposition}A second cornerstone
is the \emph{geometric spanner}: a sparse graph that approximately preserves
all pairwise distances.

\begin{definition}[Geometric Spanner]\label{def:frontiers_spanner}
A $t$-spanner of a point set $P$ is a graph $G$ with vertex set $P$ such that
for all $p, q \in P$ the graph distance satisfies
\[
d_G(p,q) \;\le\; t \cdot \|p - q\|.
\]
The parameter $t \ge 1$ is the \emph{stretch factor}.
\end{definition}

Spanners with $O(n)$ edges and stretch $1+\varepsilon$ can be built in
$O(n \log n)$ time via the well-separated pair decomposition (WSPD) of Callahan
and Kosaraju~\cite{callahan1995}, and serve as compact stand-ins for the
complete Euclidean graph in routing, motion planning, and proximity structures;
the monograph of Narasimhan and Smid develops the full theory~\cite{smid2007}.
The frontier concerns spanners for \emph{moving}, \emph{streamed}, and
high-dimensional point sets~\cite{smid2007,guibas1998}.

\index{metric embedding}Finally, when data is high-dimensional but
intrinsically low-dimensional, one embeds it into a lower-dimensional space
while preserving the geometry of interest. The Johnson--Lindenstrauss lemma
(Section~\ref{sec:highdim_jl}) preserves pairwise distances up to a factor
$1\pm\varepsilon$ in $O(\log n)$ dimensions; the frontier is \emph{nonlinear}
embedding, treated from the geometric point of view in
Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry_data_ml}, and sparse embedding schemes whose cost
scales with the intrinsic rather than the ambient dimension.

\section{Streaming Geometry}
\label{sec:frontiers_streaming}

\index{streaming algorithms}A \emph{data stream} is a sequence of items that
arrives once, in a fixed order, too quickly and too voluminously to store.
Streaming geometry asks what can be computed about a point set that arrives as
a stream, using memory polylogarithmic in $n$ and a single (or few) passes over
the data~\cite{muthukrishnan2005}. The hallmark of the model is that exact
answers are usually impossible: the algorithm must approximate, and the
guarantee is a \emph{summary} from which the desired measure can be recovered
within error $\varepsilon$.

\subsection{Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Streaming Summary]\label{def:frontiers_stream}
A \emph{summary} of a stream of points is a data structure of size $s(n) \ll
n$ from which one can answer the query of interest within a specified
approximation factor. An algorithm is \emph{single-pass} if it maintains the
summary while reading each point exactly once.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\varepsilon$-Coreset]\label{def:frontiers_coreset}
An $\varepsilon$-\emph{coreset} of a point set $P \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ for a
cost function $f$ is a subset (or weighted subset) $Q \subseteq P$ such that
for every candidate solution $X$,
\[
(1 - \varepsilon)\, f(P,X) \;\le\; f(Q,X) \;\le\; (1 + \varepsilon)\, f(P,X),
\]
where $f(P,X)$ is the cost of $X$ with respect to all of $P$. For $k$-means
and $k$-median, $X$ ranges over sets of $k$ centers; for extent measures such
as the diameter, $X$ ranges over directions and the coreset is an
$\varepsilon$-kernel.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Theory}

\index{coresets}Har-Peled and Mazumdar showed that a point set admits an
$\varepsilon$-coreset for $k$-median and $k$-means clustering of size
$O(k \varepsilon^{-d} \log n)$, giving the first streaming algorithm with
provably sublinear memory for these problems~\cite{harpeled2004}. For extent
measures, an $\varepsilon$-kernel of size $O(\varepsilon^{-(d-1)/2})$ can be
maintained in a stream using $O(\log^d n)$ space and $O(1)$ amortized time per
point~\cite{agarwal2001extent}.

\begin{theorem}[Streaming $k$-Center]\label{thm:frontiers_streaming_kcenter}
There is a single-pass, $O(k)$-space algorithm that, given the optimal
$k$-center radius $r^*$, either outputs $k$ centers of radius at most
$2 r^*$, or correctly reports that $r^*$ was chosen too small. Guessing $r^*$
from a geometric progression yields a $2$-approximation in $O(k \log
\Delta)$ space, where $\Delta$ is the spread of the
input~\cite{gonzalez1985,muthukrishnan2005}.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Streaming $k$-center (greedy, radius $r$)}
\label{alg:frontiers_streaming_kcenter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A stream $S$, an integer $k$, a radius candidate $r$.
\Ensure $k$ centers covering the stream within radius $2r$, or FAIL.
\Function{StreamingKCenter}{$S, k, r$}
  \State $C \gets \emptyset$
  \For{each point $p$ in the stream}
    \If{$d(p, C) > r$}
      \State $C \gets C \cup \{p\}$
      \If{$|C| > k$}
        \State \Return \textsc{Fail}
      \EndIf
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $C$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The accepted centers form an $r$-separated set, so if the optimal radius is
$r$, at most $k$ centers are accepted and every stream point lies within $2r$
of some center~\cite{gonzalez1985}.

\subsection{Parameters}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\varepsilon$}: the target approximation determines the coreset
  size; $d$-dependencies such as $\varepsilon^{-d}$ make small fixed
  dimensions essential.
\item \textbf{$k$}: if not known in advance, a range of guesses for $r$ is
  maintained in parallel.
\item \textbf{Pass count}: one pass forbids recovering from a bad partition,
  so randomized orderings and merge-reduce schedules bound the error.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Space}: $O(k)$ for streaming $k$-center; $O(k \varepsilon^{-d}
  \log n)$ for $k$-means coresets; $O(\varepsilon^{-(d-1)/2} \log^d n)$ for
  extent kernels.
\item \textbf{Lower bound}: any single-pass exact algorithm needs $\Omega(n)$
  space, so the approximation is unavoidable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Network monitoring}: summary statistics over IP-flow streams.
\item \textbf{Large-scale lidar}: approximate density and extent summaries
  before full reconstruction (Chapter~\ref{ch:shape_reconstruction}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Muthukrishnan \cite{muthukrishnan2005}: the standard survey of
  data-stream algorithms.
\item Har-Peled and Mazumdar \cite{harpeled2004}: coresets for $k$-means and
  $k$-median.
\item Agarwal, Guibas, Hershberger, and Veach \cite{agarwal2001extent}:
  extent measures and $\varepsilon$-kernels for moving and streamed points.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:approximation_algorithms},
  Section~\ref{sec:approx_coresets} for the coreset framework in the batch
  setting.
\end{itemize}

\section{External-Memory Geometry}
\label{sec:frontiers_external_memory}

\index{external memory}\index{I/O complexity}When a data set exceeds main
memory, the cost model changes: random-access memory is replaced by a
two-level hierarchy in which a block transfer (an \emph{I/O}) between fast
internal memory and slow disk dominates all arithmetic. External-memory (EM)
geometry designs algorithms whose I/O count is nearly optimal, exploiting the
fact that block transfer is roughly as cheap as single-record transfer, so
locality is the currency of the model~\cite{aggarwalvitter1988,vitter2001}.

\subsection{Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Parallel Disk Model]\label{def:frontiers_io_model}
In the \emph{parallel disk model} (PDM), $N$ records reside on disk, at most
$M$ records fit in internal memory, and each I/O transfers a contiguous block
of $B$ records, where $B \le M/2 < N$. The cost of an algorithm is measured by
the number of I/Os, the disk space, and the internal CPU time~\cite{vitter2001}.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Theory}

\begin{theorem}[I/O Complexity of Sorting]\label{thm:frontiers_io_sorting}
Sorting $N$ records requires, up to a constant factor,
\[
\Theta\!\left( \frac{N}{B} \log_{M/B} \frac{N}{B} \right)
\]
I/Os, and external merge and distribution sort achieve this bound. The bound
also holds for permuting and for the Fourier transform~\cite{aggarwalvitter1988}.
\end{theorem}

The sorting bound is the transfer theorem of EM geometry: because most
geometric primitives can be reduced to sorting, the same bound applies to
constructing convex hulls, Voronoi diagrams, and Delaunay triangulations of
massive point sets. Beyond sorting, the survey paradigms include the
\emph{distribution sweep} for spatial joins, \emph{buffer trees} for batched
sweep-line processing, persistent B-trees for batched point location, and
\emph{external fractional cascading} for red--blue segment intersection
~\cite{vitter2001}. Online, multidimensional indices such as the R-tree
support range queries with linear space~\cite{guttman1984,beckmann1990}.

\subsection{Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{External merge sort (skeleton)}
\label{alg:frontiers_external_sort}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Records on disk ($N$), memory $M$, block size $B$.
\Ensure The records in sorted order, on disk.
\Function{ExternalMergeSort}{$N, M, B$}
  \State $r \gets 0$
  \State read $\lfloor M/B \rfloor$ blocks, sort internally, write a \emph{run}
  \Repeat
    \State merge $\Theta(\lfloor M/B \rfloor)$ runs in one pass, outputting one
           block at a time
    \State $r \gets \lceil r / \lfloor M/B \rfloor \rceil$
  \Until{$r = 1$}
  \State \Return the sorted file
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Parameters}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Block size $B$}: larger $B$ amortizes transfer latency; the
  running time depends on $B$ only through $\log_{M/B}$, so the exact value
  matters little once $M/B$ is comfortable.
\item \textbf{Memory $M$}: the fan-in $\lfloor M/B \rfloor$ determines the
  number of passes; memory must be shared between the buffer pool and the sort
  work area.
\item \textbf{Locality of the geometry}: grid or space-filling-curve orderings
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:space_filling_curves}) make point sets block-local before
  the geometric algorithm begins.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{I/Os}: $\Theta(\frac{N}{B}\log_{M/B}\frac{N}{B})$ for sorting
  and most reduction problems; $O(\log_B N)$ I/Os per operation for
  B-tree-based dynamic dictionaries.
\item \textbf{Space}: linear in blocks on disk; internal memory $M$ as given.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Usages}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Geographic information systems}: spatial joins over terrain and
  cadastral data (Chapter~\ref{ch:applications_gis}).
\item \textbf{Spatial databases}: R-tree and B-tree based indexing of moving
  objects and map tiles~\cite{guttman1984,beckmann1990}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{References}

\begin{itemize}
\item Aggarwal and Vitter \cite{aggarwalvitter1988}: tight I/O bounds for
  sorting-related problems.
\item Vitter \cite{vitter2001}: the standard survey of EM algorithms and data
  structures.
\item Guttman \cite{guttman1984} and Beckmann et al.\ \cite{beckmann1990}:
  R-tree and R*-tree.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_data_structures} for the internal-memory
  structures that these indexes externalize.
\end{itemize}

\section{Geometric Algorithms for Autonomous Systems}
\label{sec:frontiers_autonomous}

Autonomous systems---self-driving vehicles, warehouse robots, and drones---ask
geometry to run on a moving platform with stale, noisy, and partial
information. Two classical bodies of theory supply the foundations: motion
planning (Chapter~\ref{ch:motion_planning}) and kinetic data structures
(Chapter~\ref{ch:dynamic_kinetic_geometry}).

The configuration-space viewpoint reduces a robot and its obstacles to a point
moving in a high-dimensional space (Section~\ref{sec:configuration_space}), and
the classical exact algorithms of Lozano-Perez, Latombe, and successors solve
the planar case~\cite{lozanoperez1983,latombe1991}. For articulated robots with
many degrees of freedom, exact methods are intractable and the probabilistic
roadmap (PRM) of Kavraki, Svestka, Latombe, and Overmars builds a random graph
of feasible configurations (Section~\ref{sec:sampling_motion_planning})
~\cite{kavraki1996}. The frontier is \emph{closed-loop} planning: replanning as
the world model is updated by perception, respecting both geometric constraints
and the latency and uncertainty of the sensor loop.

\index{kinetic data structure}When the environment moves---other vehicles,
pedestrians, weather fronts---the geometric structures describing it must be
maintained over time rather than rebuilt from scratch. The kinetic data
structure (KDS) framework formalizes this.

\begin{definition}[Kinetic Data Structure]\label{def:frontiers_kds}
A \emph{kinetic data structure} (KDS) for a set of moving objects maintains a
discrete structure $S$ together with a set of \emph{certificates}, each a
geometric predicate whose truth at the current time guarantees the correctness
of $S$. The motion is described by known trajectories (typically low-degree
polynomials in time); a \emph{failure event} is the first time a certificate
becomes false, at which point the structure and the certificate set are
updated~\cite{guibas1998,basch1999}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Certificate]\label{def:frontiers_certificate}
A \emph{certificate} for a structure $S$ is a predicate $c(t)$ over the object
positions at time $t$ such that if all certificates hold, the structure is
correct. The structure is \emph{compact} if the number of certificates is
near-linear, \emph{local} if each object participates in few certificates, and
\emph{responsive} if a single event costs few certificate changes.
\end{definition}

The classic KDSs for kinetic sorting, the convex hull, the closest pair, and
the Delaunay triangulation all achieve near-linear storage
~\cite{basch1999,guibas1998}. Agarwal, Guibas, Hershberger, and Veach proved
that some extent measures are intrinsically costly to maintain
exactly~\cite{agarwal2001extent}.

\begin{theorem}[Kinetic Extent Bounds]\label{thm:frontiers_kinetic_bounds}
The diametral pair of $n$ points in the plane, each moving with constant
velocity, changes $\Theta(n^2)$ times in the worst case, so no KDS can
maintain the diameter with fewer than $\Theta(n^2)$ events. This motivates
approximate kinetic maintenance via $\varepsilon$-kernels that are valid for
all time~\cite{agarwal2001extent,guibas1998}.
\end{theorem}

In collision detection, the kinetic tiling of free space by Agarwal, Basch,
Guibas, Hershberger, and Zhang maintains a dynamic decomposition whose
certificates certify that the moving objects remain separated
~\cite{agarwal2002kinetic}; kinetic Delaunay triangulations under uniform
linear motion now achieve a near-quadratic event bound~\cite{rubin2016}.

The perception side of autonomy is also geometric: registering a live sensor
scan to a stored map is the iterative closest point (ICP) problem
(Section~\ref{sec:matching_icp} and Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_matching})
~\cite{besl1992}, and occupancy grids built from range data use the spatial
structures of Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_data_structures}. The frontier combines
these into pipelines whose primitives run at control rates with bounded
latency, and whose failure modes---occlusion, sensor dropout, ambiguity---are
certified rather than merely measured.

\section{Geometry in Machine Learning}
\label{sec:frontiers_ml}

Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry_data_ml} develops the geometric view of data: points
as samples, distances as similarity, and manifolds as structure. The research
frontier runs in the opposite direction as well, using machine learning to
improve geometric algorithms.

\index{algorithms with predictions}\index{learning-augmented algorithm}
Classical analysis is worst-case, but real instances are rarely worst-case.
\emph{Algorithms with predictions} accept a machine-learned oracle and deliver
guarantees that interpolate between ideal performance when the predictions are
perfect and the classical worst case when they are useless
~\cite{mitzenmacher2022}.

\begin{definition}[Learning-Augmented Algorithm]\label{def:frontiers_learning_augmented}
An algorithm \emph{with predictions} receives, alongside its input, a set of
predictions $\hat{y}_1, \ldots, \hat{y}_m$ from an oracle. Its cost is a
function of the input \emph{and} of the prediction error $\eta$; the design
goal is that the cost is near-optimal for $\eta = 0$ and degrades gracefully
to the classical worst case as $\eta$ grows~\cite{mitzenmacher2022}.
\end{definition}

The cleanest geometric instantiation is the \emph{learned index} of Kraska,
Beutel, Chi, Dean, and Polyzotis, which observes that a B-tree is a model
mapping keys to offsets and replaces it with a learned regressor followed by a
small correction structure~\cite{kraska2018}. The same "model plus repair"
pattern is being applied to spatial indexes, Bloom filters, and
nearest-neighbor structures; the open problem is to give learned indexes
worst-case guarantees matching classical ones~\cite{kraska2018,mitzenmacher2022}.

The complementary frontier uses geometric data structures inside learned
pipelines. Approximate nearest-neighbor search, the workhorse of
embedding-based retrieval, is served by locality-sensitive hashing
(Section~\ref{sec:highdim_nearest_neighbor}) and by navigable small-world
graphs such as HNSW~\cite{indyk1998,malkov2018}. For clustering, streaming, and
range counting, the coresets and summaries of
Sections~\ref{sec:frontiers_streaming}--\ref{sec:frontiers_external_memory}
become the feature maps that learned models consume. Section~\ref{sec:data_ml_ai}
collects the applications of this chapter's models to AI systems.

\begin{remark}[Two Kinds of Learning in Geometry]\label{rem:frontiers_learning}
It is important to separate \emph{learning from geometry} (estimating a
function on geometric data, as in geometric deep learning,
Section~\ref{sec:frontiers_deep_learning}) from \emph{learning for geometry}
(replacing a geometric data structure by a learned model). Both are active; the
second has the sharper performance question, because a learned structure must
still justify its worst-case behavior.
\end{remark}

\section{Topological Data Analysis}
\label{sec:frontiers_tda}

\index{topological data analysis}\index{persistent homology}Topological data
analysis (TDA) treats data as a sample of an unknown space and asks for its
topological invariants, which are robust to noise precisely because they are
coarse. The theoretical foundations appear in Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology};
the frontier is making persistent homology scale to massive and
high-dimensional inputs.

\begin{definition}[Filtration]\label{def:frontiers_filtration}
A \emph{filtration} of a simplicial complex is a nested sequence
\[
K_1 \subseteq K_2 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_m
\]
of subcomplexes, typically induced by a function such as the radius of a ball
around each data point (the \v{C}ech, Vietoris--Rips, and alpha complexes of
Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Persistent Homology]\label{def:frontiers_persistence}
For a filtration as above, the $p$-th \emph{persistence module} tracks each
homology class: it is \emph{born} at the first complex in which it appears and
\emph{dies} when it merges or vanishes. The \emph{persistence diagram} of
dimension $p$ is the multiset of birth--death pairs $(b, d)$ of these classes,
including the diagonal.
\end{definition}

Zomorodian and Carlsson showed that the persistence module of a filtered
complex is a graded module over a polynomial ring, and that persistent homology
over a field can therefore be computed from a boundary matrix in cubic time via
a simple matrix-reduction algorithm~\cite{zomorodian2005}; the pipeline is
surveyed in Carlsson's paper~\cite{carlsson2009} and the monograph of
Edelsbrunner and Harer~\cite{edelsbrunnerharer2010}.

\begin{theorem}[Stability of Persistence Diagrams]\label{thm:frontiers_stability}
If two filtrations are induced by functions $f$ and $g$ on the same space that
differ by at most $\|f - g\|_\infty$, then the corresponding persistence
diagrams differ by at most that amount in bottleneck distance. Consequently,
topological features whose birth--death intervals are long are robust to
bounded noise~\cite{edelsbrunnerharer2010,carlsson2009}.
\end{theorem}

The frontier questions are computational: computing persistence in dimensions
above three, streaming and distributed reductions for massive filtrations,
approximate persistence with certified error, and the \emph{Mapper} family of
methods that summarize a data set at many scales. Applications span biology,
materials science, and shape analysis, where alpha shapes and persistence
barcodes bridge the geometry of Chapter~\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations} and
the topology of Chapter~\ref{ch:computational_topology}
~\cite{edelsbrunner1983alpha,dey2010}.

\begin{remark}[Discrete Morse Theory]\label{rem:frontiers_morse}
Discrete Morse theory gives a sparse, combinatorial description of the
critical structure of a function on a complex, reducing the number of simplices
needed to compute homology and persistence by orders of magnitude
~\cite{forman1998,forman2002}. It is the main practical accelerator for
persistence computations in three dimensions.
\end{remark}

\section{Shape and Surface Reconstruction}
\label{sec:frontiers_reconstruction}

Chapter~\ref{ch:shape_reconstruction} develops reconstruction from first
principles: the local feature size, the medial axis, and the certificate that a
sample is dense enough for the crust or cocone algorithm to succeed
~\cite{amenta1998,amentabern1999,dey2007}. The frontier is threefold.

First, \emph{reconstruction without clean samples}: real scans contain noise,
outliers, holes, and nonuniform density. Ball-pivoting reconstructs locally by
rolling a ball of fixed radius over the samples~\cite{bernardini1999}; Poisson
reconstruction fits an implicit indicator function whose gradient matches the
oriented sample normals and is robust to noise and nonuniformity
~\cite{kazhdan2006}. The frontier is making provable guarantees---sampling
conditions, approximation bounds, and output quality---survive these practical
relaxations~\cite{boissonnat2005}.

Second, \emph{reconstruction at scale}: the pipelines that win
benchmarks---Voronoi filtering, Poisson, and their variants---all begin with a
Delaunay or nearest-neighbor computation over millions of points, and the
techniques of Sections~\ref{sec:frontiers_streaming}--\ref{sec:frontiers_external_memory}
are being imported to push reconstruction into the out-of-core regime.

Third, \emph{certified reconstruction}: recent directions certify the output
surface's Hausdorff closeness to the input and its topology, terminating with
either a reconstruction or a statement about the sample that is provably
insufficient. Section~\ref{sec:shape_reconstruction_point_clouds} lists the
point-cloud models; Section~\ref{sec:shape_poisson_reconstruction} develops
the implicit surface view that the frontier generalizes.

\section{Geometric Deep Learning}
\label{sec:frontiers_deep_learning}

\index{geometric deep learning}Geometric deep learning (GDL) studies neural
networks that respect the geometry of their input domain. The unifying
blueprint of Bronstein, Bruna, Cohen, and Velickovic organizes the field by its
\emph{geometric priors}: symmetry and invariance under a domain's group,
stability under deformation, and scale separation~\cite{bronstein2021}. On
grids these priors give convolutional networks; on sets they give
permutation-invariant networks; on graphs and manifolds they give graph neural
networks and intrinsic mesh CNNs~\cite{bronstein2021,bronstein2017}.

\begin{definition}[Geometric Prior]\label{def:frontiers_geometric_prior}
A \emph{geometric prior} is a regularity of the input domain encoded into a
network architecture, typically as invariance or equivariance to a symmetry
group $G$: the network must satisfy $f(g \cdot x) = \rho(g)\, f(x)$ for a
representation $\rho$, so that the model cannot waste capacity on
transformations that leave the meaning of the data unchanged~\cite{bronstein2021}.
\end{definition}

The connection to this book's algorithms is direct. For point clouds, PointNet
uses a symmetric aggregation function (a max over point features) to achieve
permutation invariance, and then a spatial transformer that is a learned rigid
alignment---the same registration problem of Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_matching}
~\cite{qi2017pointnet}. For meshes, intrinsic networks operate on the
Laplace--Beltrami spectrum of Chapter~\ref{ch:spectral_geometry}. The frontier
is \emph{expressivity}: which geometric structures can a network provably
learn, and which classical algorithms (Delaunay tests, persistence diagrams,
optimal transports) can be differentiated through so that a gradient-based
optimizer can search over geometry itself. That last question is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:frontiers_differentiable}.

\section{Differentiable Geometry}
\label{sec:frontiers_differentiable}

\index{differentiable rendering}\index{differentiable geometry}Modern 3D
reconstruction is inverse graphics: given images or scans, estimate geometry,
appearance, and motion so that a forward model reproduces the observations.
This requires \emph{differentiability}---the ability to compute gradients of an
output image with respect to geometric parameters. The foundational issue is
that discrete geometric events (which triangle is visible, which sample is the
nearest neighbor) are not differentiable, so gradient information must be
smoothed, relaxed, or randomized.

\begin{definition}[Differentiable Renderer]\label{def:frontiers_diff_renderer}
A \emph{differentiable renderer} is a forward model $R(\theta)$ of image
synthesis parameterized by scene geometry $\theta$ such that the derivative
$\partial R / \partial \theta$ is available, allowing loss gradients to be
propagated back through the rendering process to the geometry
~\cite{loper2014opendr}.
\end{definition}

OpenDR was the first general framework to expose this derivative, smoothing
the discrete visibility function so that silhouette losses drive shape
estimation~\cite{loper2014opendr}. The frontier now spans differentiable ray
tracing with importance-weighted estimates, differentiable mesh simplification,
and differentiable solvers for PDE-based geometry processing, where
walk-on-spheres estimators provide derivatives with respect to the domain's
geometry~\cite{yu2025}. At the far end, neural field representations collapse
the mesh into a learned simplicial or neural representation; Simplicits shows
that elastic simulation can be carried out on a learned reduced basis, agnostic
to whether the input was a mesh, a point cloud, or a neural
field~\cite{modi2024simplicits}. The open problems are algorithmic: certificate
guarantees for gradient estimates, and differentiable versions of combinatorial
structures such as Delaunay triangulations and persistence diagrams.

\section{Geometry Processing}
\label{sec:frontiers_processing}

\index{geometry processing}Geometry processing is the discipline of operating
on meshes and point clouds---remeshing, parameterization, deformation,
simplification, and repair---with an emphasis on numerical quality and
robustness rather than asymptotic complexity. Its foundations appear in
Chapters~\ref{ch:surface_geometry}, \ref{ch:surface_modeling}, and
\ref{ch:mesh_analysis}; the frontier is replacing fragile, hand-tuned
pipelines with algorithms that work on \emph{in-the-wild} geometry.

The classical guarantee of Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}---a Delaunay
tetrahedral mesh with bounded angle quality---is achieved by refinement for
clean inputs, but real inputs arrive as self-intersecting, non-manifold, dirty
meshes. The \texttt{fTetWild} and \texttt{TriWild} pipelines envelope the
input geometry with an ambient mesh and project back, producing robust
tetrahedral and surface remeshes with angle bounds in the presence of
degenerate input~\cite{hu2018,hu2021}. The frontier concerns output size and
runtime, since the ambient space is typically much larger than the surface.

A second direction replaces the extrinsic (coordinate-based) view of a mesh
with its intrinsic structure---geodesic distances and the Laplace--Beltrami
operator---so that algorithms are invariant to how the shape is embedded;
learned descriptors are trained directly on intrinsic
quantities~\cite{sharp2025}. The frontier is the integration of these tools
with the differentiable pipelines of Section~\ref{sec:frontiers_differentiable}.

\begin{example}[Mesh Repair as Topological Repair]\label{ex:frontiers_mesh_repair}
Repairing a triangle soup requires closing holes, resolving self-intersections,
and fixing orientation. Volumetric repair rasterizes the soup into an occupancy
grid and extracts an isosurface, which guarantees a watertight, manifold output
at the price of a resolution parameter; this is the same march between a
continuous surface and its discretization that Chapter~\ref{ch:voxel_geometry}
develops.
\end{example}

\section{Open Problems in Computational Geometry}
\label{sec:frontiers_open_problems}

Every section of this chapter touches an open problem; this section collects
the most famous and most structural ones. A fuller, living list appears in the
open-problems chapter of the Handbook of Discrete and Computational Geometry
~\cite{goodman2017}, and the long-standing conjectures trace back to Shamos's
thesis~\cite{shamos1978}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The $\Omega(n \log n)$ conjecture for triangulation.} Is there a
  true $O(n \log n)$ algorithm for computing the Delaunay triangulation of $n$
  points in the plane? The best algorithms run in $O(n \log n)$ randomized or
  deterministic expected time, and all rely on sorting; Shamos conjectured that
  $\Omega(n \log n)$ is inherent, but no conditional lower bound separates
  triangulation from the easier problems on which the bound is
  known~\cite{shamos1978}.

\item \textbf{Algebraic decision-tree lower bounds.} Ben-Or's method proves
  $\Omega(n \log n)$ lower bounds for element distinctness and for convex hulls
  and closest pairs in the algebraic decision-tree model~\cite{benor1983}.
  Extending such bounds to \emph{randomized} and \emph{quantum} models remains
  open.

\item \textbf{The complexity of robust computation.} Is there a
  quasi-linear-time algorithm for exact geometric predicates and constructed
  objects whose cost is bounded by a constant factor times the floating-point
  cost? Current adaptive predicates are near-float for signs, but certified
  \emph{construction} of intersections and arrangements still carries large
  hidden constants~\cite{yap1997,halperin2004}.

\item \textbf{Kinetic structures with near-linear total cost.} The kinetic
  Delaunay triangulation under linear motion was recently shown to have
  $O(n^2)$ events, closing a long-standing gap~\cite{rubin2016}. Whether a KDS
  exists whose \emph{total} event processing over any polynomial motion is
  $o(n^2)$ for standard proximity structures remains
  open~\cite{guibas1998,agarwal2002kinetic}.

\item \textbf{Streaming and external-memory trade-offs.} Tight space lower
  bounds for geometric streaming (whether $O(k \log n)$ bits suffice for
  approximate $k$-center in fixed dimension) and matching I/O lower bounds for
  geometric joins beyond the sorting barrier are unresolved
  ~\cite{muthukrishnan2005,vitter2001}.

\item \textbf{Spanners in low doubling dimension.} The spanner theory of
  Section~\ref{sec:frontiers_massive_data} is Euclidean; whether $O(n)$-edge
  spanners with stretch $1+\varepsilon$ exist for arbitrary metric spaces of
  doubling dimension $d$, with running time $O(n \log n) f(d)$, is open in
  several regimes~\cite{smid2007}.

\item \textbf{The robustness of learned structures.} Whether
  learning-augmented data structures can simultaneously achieve worst-case
  guarantees matching classical indexes and average-case performance exceeding
  them is a formal question with only partial answers~\cite{kraska2018,mitzenmacher2022}.

\item \textbf{Imprecision and the price of ignorance.} For the measures of
  Section~\ref{sec:frontiers_robust}, the complexity gap between computing
  worst-case and best-case values over an uncertainty set is far from
  understood; several natural variants such as the largest possible width remain
  open even for simple region models~\cite{loffler2010largest,loffler2010hulls}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{How to Read Computational Geometry Research}
\label{sec:frontiers_reading}

\index{research papers}Computational geometry research is published in the
venues that define the field: the Symposium on Computational Geometry (SoCG),
the Symposium on Discrete Algorithms (SODA), and the Symposium on Theory of
Computing (STOC), with the \emph{Journal of the ACM} and \emph{Discrete \&
Computational Geometry} carrying journal versions. A research paper is read in
a specific order, and the order is not the order it is written:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The title and abstract} tell you the problem, the model, and the
  headline bound. Ask whether the model matches the applications you care
  about.
\item \textbf{The statement of results} tells you what is claimed. Write it
  down with its parameters: what is held fixed, what is worst case, what is
  expected.
\item \textbf{The overview of techniques} (often Section 2) is the most
  valuable part for a reader who does not plan to rederive everything. It names
  the ideas: a duality, a randomized incremental construction, a charging
  argument.
\item \textbf{The proof} is read only as deeply as your goals require. For most
  readers the structure matters more than the algebra: where does the argument
  fail if the input is degenerate, if the dimension grows, or if the arithmetic
  is rounded?
\item \textbf{The applications and experiments}, when present, tell you how
  the theory maps to practice and where the hidden constants live.
\end{enumerate}

The textbooks of this field remain the best gateway to the literature: de Berg,
Cheong, van Kreveld, and Overmars for the classical algorithms
~\cite{deberg2008,berg2008}, Matousek for discrepancy and its applications
~\cite{matousek1999}, and the Handbook of Discrete and Computational Geometry
for the breadth of the field~\cite{goodman2017}. When reading a paper, maintain
a \emph{question log}: for each result, record what assumption is essential,
what would change if it were relaxed, and whether the algorithm would survive
contact with real data. Research often begins exactly at the point where a
logged question has no answer in the literature.

\section{Designing Computational Experiments}
\label{sec:frontiers_experiments}

\index{experiments}Computational geometry is unusual among algorithms
disciplines in that worst-case analysis is both highly developed and frequently
misleading: the notorious cases (cocircular points, collinear sets, degenerate
coordinates) are exactly the ones that break floating-point implementations,
and the worst-case instances of a randomized incremental algorithm may be
vanishingly rare. Experimentation therefore has a specific shape.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Choose a baseline.} Compare against a reference implementation,
  not just against a theoretical bound. CGAL, Boost Geometry, and the libraries
  of Appendix~\ref{app:software} provide the de facto baselines. Cite and
  version them.
\item \textbf{Generate structured instances.} Random uniform points are the
  least informative test set: they are in general position with probability one
  and are trivially well distributed. Use the generators of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:sampling}---Poisson-disk samples and Halton sequences
  (Section~\ref{sec:poisson_disk_sampling})---and add \emph{adversarial
  families}: cocircular and collinear inputs, high aspect ratios, and clustered
  data.
\item \textbf{Measure what matters.} Report running time and peak memory, but
  also the number of predicate evaluations (the quantity the theory controls)
  and the number of degeneracies encountered. A timed run alone cannot
  distinguish an algorithm that is fast because it is clever from one that is
  fast because it is silently wrong.
\item \textbf{Test correctness, then robustness.} For every output structure,
  verify the certificates: is the reported triangulation a valid planar graph,
  and does every triangle satisfy the empty-circumcircle property? Then run the
  robustness suites of Section~\ref{sec:frontiers_robust}, including the
  regression inputs collected by Kettner et al.\cite{kettner2008}.
\item \textbf{Scale systematically.} Increase $n$ geometrically and record the
  empirical order of growth, comparing it to the theoretical one. Report the
  crossover points between algorithms, which are where the hidden constants
  live.
\item \textbf{Reproduce and release.} Record random seeds, parameter values,
  and data generators so that every table is reproducible. The reproducible
  experiment is the unit of currency of the field's experimental literature.
\end{enumerate}

A useful rule of thumb: if an experiment cannot be described in one sentence
that states the question, the instances, the metric, and the baseline, it is
not yet designed.

\section{From Graduate Study to Research}
\label{sec:frontiers_graduate}

\index{graduate study}This book ends where research begins. The transition
from coursework to research in computational geometry is mostly a transition
of attitude, and a few habits accelerate it.

First, \emph{keep a research notebook} in which every problem you meet---in
the exercises of this book, in a paper you read, in a colleague's
seminar---is written down together with its parameters and its status. Open
problems are not hidden in the literature; they accumulate in such notebooks.
Second, \emph{learn the theorems twice}: once for their statements, and once
for the moment in the proof where an assumption is used. The second reading is
what converts a theorem into a research tool, because most research questions
are of the form "what happens if this assumption is relaxed?" Third,
\emph{implement what you study}; the distance between a bound that holds in the
worst case and an algorithm that runs in the real world is measured in
implemented experiments.

Fourth, \emph{choose problems at the intersection of a model and a need}. The
frontiers of Sections~\ref{sec:frontiers_streaming}--\ref{sec:frontiers_processing}
all arose because a classical model stopped matching a real scale or a real
source of noise. The most productive problems are typically ones where a
classical algorithm is provably correct, empirically fragile, and structurally
simple enough that its analysis can be repaired. Fifth, \emph{publish by
solving, not by polishing}: a short paper that resolves a clean question in a
new model---streaming, kinetic, imprecise, learning-augmented---is the standard
currency of the field.

Finally, the field rewards \emph{transfer}: a technique developed for one
problem (an $\varepsilon$-kernel for extent, a WSPD for spanners, an adaptive
predicate for robustness) is frequently the key to an unrelated problem. The
boundary between what is known and what is unknown is where the techniques of
the previous parts of this book meet the questions of the current one. That
boundary is, in the end, the natural starting point of a research career.
