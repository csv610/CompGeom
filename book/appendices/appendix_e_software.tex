\chapter{Computational Geometry Software}
\label{app:software}

This appendix surveys the software landscape for computational geometry:
the general-purpose C++ libraries, the Python packages, the meshing
engines, the polygon and CAD kernels, the visualization tools, and the
exact-arithmetic foundations on which they rest. For each entry we give a
compact profile --- language, license, and the operations it offers ---
together with a one-line ``Good for:'' recommendation. License statements
and version numbers are accurate as of the writing of this book, but open
and proprietary projects both change their terms; verify against the
project's own pages before committing to a library for a product.

Throughout this appendix we assume the reader has worked through the
algorithm chapters of the book: convex hulls
(Chapter~\ref{ch:convex_hulls}), segment intersection
(Chapter~\ref{ch:segment_intersection}), triangulations and Delaunay
triangulations (Chapters~\ref{ch:triangulations} and
\ref{ch:delaunay_triangulations}), Voronoi diagrams
(Chapter~\ref{ch:voronoi_diagrams}), point location
(Chapter~\ref{ch:point_location}), geometric predicates
(Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_predicates}), polygon operations
(Chapter~\ref{ch:polygon_ops}), half-edge data structures
(Chapter~\ref{ch:half_edge}), and mesh generation
(Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}). The libraries below are the ones most
likely to supply production implementations of the algorithms in those
chapters, and the choice among them is the subject of the final section.

\section{CGAL}
\label{sec:app_soft_cgal}

CGAL, the Computational Geometry Algorithms Library, is the closest thing
the field has to a reference implementation of its own theory. It is a
large C++ library, developed since 1996 by a consortium of academic
institutions and companies, and it covers most of the classical
algorithms in this book with a rigor that is unmatched elsewhere.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CGAL} --- C++, open-source (dual-licensed under the GNU
    GPL v3 and the GNU LGPL v3, with the applicable license depending on
    the package; commercial licenses are available from the CGAL
    project) \cite{cgalmanual}. Offers exact and adaptive predicates
    through its kernel architecture (Section~\ref{sec:intro_exact_computation}),
    plus packages for convex hulls in any dimension, 2D and 3D Delaunay
    and regular triangulations, constrained Delaunay triangulations,
    Voronoi diagrams, 2D and 3D arrangements, Boolean operations on
    polygons and Nef polyhedra, point location, polygon offsetting and
    straight skeletons, spatial searching and AABB trees, half-edge and
    polyhedral surface data structures (the analogues of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:half_edge}), mesh generation in 2D and 3D, and
    polygon mesh processing. \emph{Good for:} provably robust,
    exactness-guaranteed implementations of nearly every algorithm in
    this book, and the de facto standard against which other geometry
    software is judged.
  \item \textbf{CGAL exact number types} --- C++, part of CGAL. The
    \texttt{Gmpz}, \texttt{Gmpq}, \texttt{Lazy\_exact\_nt}, and
    \texttt{Interval\_nt} types, together with filtered (adaptive)
    predicates and lazy evaluation, let the same code run fast in the
    non-degenerate case and exact in the degenerate one, exactly as
    described in Sections~\ref{sec:intro_robustness}--\ref{sec:intro_exact_computation}
    and Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_predicates}. \emph{Good for:} exact
    evaluation of orientation, in-circle, and in-sphere predicates on
    integer or rational input coordinates.
  \item \textbf{CGAL 2D Arrangements} --- C++, part of CGAL. Constructs
    and walks planar arrangements of segments, polylines, conics, and
    other curves; supports point location, zone construction, and face
    traversal, the data structure underlying
    Chapters~\ref{ch:segment_intersection} and \ref{ch:point_location}.
    \emph{Good for:} exact planar subdivisions and overlay problems with
    curved input.
  \item \textbf{CGAL mesh generation} --- C++, part of CGAL. The
    \texttt{Mesh\_2} and \texttt{Mesh\_3} packages build triangulated
    and tetrahedral meshes with user-supplied sizing, shape, and
    approximation criteria, including meshing of implicit domains from a
    signed distance function and preservation of sharp features; see
    Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}. \emph{Good for:} high-quality
    meshes with rigorous guarantees on element shape.
  \item \textbf{Geogram} --- C++, open-source (GNU LGPL v3). A geometry
    and mesh-processing library from INRIA (Bruno L\'evy) providing
    robust 2D and 3D triangulations and Voronoi diagrams, a Delaunay
    package, spatial search (k-d trees), and its own graphics layer.
    \cite{geogram} \emph{Good for:} mesh processing and 3D Delaunay
    experiments in C++ without the template complexity of CGAL.
  \item \textbf{libigl} --- C++ (header-only), open-source (Mozilla
    Public License 2.0) \cite{libigl}. A geometry-processing library
    built on Eigen: cotangent Laplacians and mass matrices, mesh Boolean
    operations, decimation, parameterization, and an OpenGL viewer.
    \emph{Good for:} research prototyping in geometry processing, and
    for the discrete differential-geometry operators used in the surface
    chapters.
  \item \textbf{CGAL Python bindings} --- C++/Python, open-source (GPL).
    The \texttt{cgal-bindings} project exposes a subset of CGAL
    (triangulations, arrangements, polygon operations, meshing) to
    Python; support is partial and the API lags the C++ library. For
    Python-side exact geometry the lighter-weight \texttt{scikit-geometry}
    wrapper of Section~\ref{sec:app_soft_python} is often easier.
    \emph{Good for:} prototyping CGAL algorithms interactively from
    Python.
\end{itemize}

\centering
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
\textbf{Library} & \textbf{Language} & \textbf{License} & \textbf{Focus} \\
\hline
CGAL            & C++       & GPL-3/LGPL-3, commercial & broad exact geometry \\
Geogram         & C++       & LGPL-3                   & triangulations, meshes \\
libigl          & C++       & MPL-2.0                 & geometry processing \\

\hline

\caption{General-purpose C++ computational-geometry libraries.}
\label{tbl:appsoft_cgal}
\end{table}

Beyond the software itself, the CGAL project maintains one of the best
reference resources in the field: the \emph{CGAL User and Reference
Manual} \cite{cgalmanual}, organized by package, with exhaustive
documentation of the theory each package implements, and an extensive
tutorial collection that walks the reader from basic kernel usage to
advanced packages such as arrangements and mesh generation. A companion
free journal, the \emph{Journal of Computational Geometry} \cite{jocg},
publishes research papers on computational geometry --- frequently the
same algorithms the library implements --- and is a good place to look
up the theoretical provenance of a CGAL package.

\section{GEOSE}
\label{sec:app_soft_geose}

This section covers the geometry engines and CAD kernels built around
planar and solid Boolean operations: GEOS and its Java ancestor JTS,
the Clipper polygon-clipping family, and the OpenCascade solid modeling
kernel. (The heading ``GEOSE'' stands for the \emph{GEometry Engine}
library family; GEOS itself is the leading member.)

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GEOS} --- C++, open-source (GNU LGPL v2.1). The Geometry
    Engine Open Source, a faithful C++ port of JTS that serves as the
    geometry backend of PostGIS, QGIS, and Shapely. Offers the planar
    operation suite of Chapter~\ref{ch:polygon_ops}: intersection,
    union, difference, and symmetric difference of polygons and
    multipolygons, buffer construction (offsetting with round, bevel,
    and miter joins), predicates such as \texttt{within},
    \texttt{contains}, and \texttt{intersects}, distance and nearest
    feature computations, prepared geometries for repeated predicate
    queries, and an STRtree spatial index \cite{geos}. \emph{Good for:}
    production planar Boolean and overlay operations on geographic data.
  \item \textbf{JTS} --- Java, open-source (Eclipse Distribution License
    v1) \cite{jts}. The JTS Topology Suite is the reference Java
    implementation of the same planar operation model, and the direct
    ancestor of GEOS; it also supplies Delaunay triangulation, Voronoi
    diagrams, and hull construction in Java. \emph{Good for:} Java
    applications and as the reference behavior that other engines
    reproduce.
  \item \textbf{Clipper2} --- C++ (with C\# and Python bindings),
    open-source (Boost Software License 1.0) \cite{clipper2}. Boolean
    operations (intersection, union, difference, XOR) on polygons with
    holes and open polylines, plus the \emph{inflate} and \emph{deflate}
    operations that implement polygon offsetting (the 2D Minkowski
    operations studied in the polygon chapters). Uses 64-bit integer
    coordinates, which makes the booleans exact and extremely fast for
    input that can be scaled to integers. \emph{Good for:} polygon
    clipping and offsetting in CAD/CAM, PCB, and laser-cutting
    workflows. Its predecessor, Clipper, remains widely deployed.
  \item \textbf{OpenCascade} --- C++, open-source (GNU LGPL v2.1 with an
    additional linking exception) \cite{opencascade}. A full 3D CAD
    kernel: boundary representation (BRep) solids, Boolean operations on
    solids, filleting and chamfering, NURBS surface modeling, STEP and
    IGES import/export, and a built-in mesher (BRepMesh) that turns BRep
    faces into triangle meshes for rendering and simulation.
    \emph{Good for:} 3D solid modeling, CAD data interchange, and the
    underlying geometry engine for mesh pre-processing pipelines.
  \item \textbf{Fade2D} --- C++, proprietary (a free license is granted
    for academic and educational use) \cite{fade2d}. A focused 2D
    triangulation library: (constrained) Delaunay triangulations, point
    location, Boolean operations, and offsetting, marketed toward
    industrial geometric algorithms. \emph{Good for:} commercial 2D
    triangulation and polygon offsetting with a permissive commercial
    model.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
\textbf{Engine} & \textbf{Language} & \textbf{License} & \textbf{Strength} \\
\hline
GEOS            & C++        & LGPL-2.1             & planar booleans, GIS \\
JTS             & Java       & EDL-1.0              & reference planar ops \\
Clipper2        & C++/C\#/Python & BSL-1.0           & clipping, offsetting \\
OpenCascade     & C++        & LGPL-2.1 + exception & 3D CAD/BREP kernel \\
Fade2D          & C++        & proprietary, free academic & robust 2D triangulation \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Polygon and CAD geometry engines.}
\label{tbl:appsoft_geose}
\end{table}

A practical observation about this family: the overlay algorithms in
GEOS and JTS are designed to remain combinatorially consistent in the
face of floating-point roundoff (they "snap" nearly-coincident vertices
and nudge intersecting segments), whereas Clipper2 sidesteps the issue
entirely by using integer coordinates. Neither approach replaces the
exactness of CGAL's filtered predicates, but both are considerably faster
on typical GIS or drafting data, which is why they dominate their
application domains.

\section{Boost.Geometry}
\label{sec:app_soft_boost}

Boost contributes two header-only C++ libraries for 2D geometry. They
follow the Boost idiom of generic algorithms over user-supplied geometry
types, and are a reasonable default when a project already builds on
Boost.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Boost.Geometry} --- C++ (header-only), part of Boost,
    open-source (Boost Software License 1.0) \cite{boostgeometry}.
    Generic algorithms for planar geometry: area, length, perimeter,
    distance, \texttt{within}, \texttt{intersects}, \texttt{covers},
    intersection, union, and difference of points, linestrings,
    polygons, and their multi-versions, buffer construction, convex
    hull, envelope, and an R-tree spatial index. Algorithms are written
    against an extensible \emph{concept} interface, so user geometry
    classes can be plugged in. \emph{Good for:} 2D geometric algorithms
    in generic C++ code with arbitrary coordinate types.
  \item \textbf{boost.polygon} --- C++ (header-only), part of Boost,
    open-source (Boost Software License 1.0) \cite{boostpolygon}.
    Boolean set operations on polygons with holes for integer
    coordinates (exact), Voronoi diagrams of points and segments, and
    polygon layout utilities. Its integer model makes the Boolean
    operations deterministic and fast; floating-point input is supported
    only in restricted configurations. \emph{Good for:} fast
    integer-coordinate polygon Boolean operations and segment Voronoi
    diagrams.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
\textbf{Library} & \textbf{Language} & \textbf{License} & \textbf{Focus} \\
\hline
Boost.Geometry & C++, header-only & BSL-1.0 & generic 2D algorithms, R-tree \\
boost.polygon  & C++, header-only & BSL-1.0 & integer polygon booleans, Voronoi \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Boost geometry libraries.}
\label{tbl:appsoft_boost}
\end{table}

Both libraries are solid engineering but limited in scope compared with
CGAL: they are 2D-centric, and their Boolean and overlay kernels do not
make the exactness guarantees that CGAL's filtered predicates provide.
For tasks confined to the planar algorithms of this book and a codebase
already committed to Boost, they are an attractive dependency-free choice.

\section{Qhull}
\label{sec:app_soft_qhull}

Qhull is the classic implementation of the Quickhull algorithm for
convex hulls, Delaunay triangulations, and Voronoi diagrams in arbitrary
dimensions \cite{barber1996}. Despite its age it remains the workhorse
behind most hull and triangulation computations outside CGAL, including
the numerical-computing ecosystems.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Qhull / libqhull} --- C, open-source (permissive custom
    license, BSD/MIT-like, with attribution and notice requirements)
    \cite{barber1996}. Computes convex hulls in 2, 3, and higher
    dimensions, Delaunay triangulations and their Voronoi duals (by
    lifting onto the paraboloid, the construction of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:voronoi_diagrams}), halfspace intersections about a
    point, and furthest-site Delaunay triangulations. Ships as a
    command-line program (\texttt{qhull}) and as a re-entrant C library
    (\texttt{libqhull\_r}); it handles floating-point roundoff by
    detecting inconsistencies and, when asked, by "joggling" input
    points, a practical alternative to the exact-predicate approach of
    Section~\ref{sec:intro_exact_computation}. \emph{Good for:} convex
    hulls, Delaunay, and Voronoi computations in any dimension, and as
    the embedded engine of higher-level packages.
  \item \textbf{scipy.spatial} --- Python, open-source (BSD 3-clause)
    \cite{scipy}. Wraps Qhull to expose \texttt{ConvexHull},
    \texttt{Delaunay}, and \texttt{Voronoi} on NumPy arrays; see the
    SciPy section below. \emph{Good for:} Python access to Qhull without
    writing C.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
\textbf{Interface} & \textbf{Language} & \textbf{License} & \textbf{Focus} \\
\hline
qhull CLI       & C, command line & Qhull (permissive) & hulls/Delaunay/Voronoi, all dims \\
libqhull\_r     & C library        & Qhull (permissive) & embedding in C/C++ \\
scipy.spatial   & Python           & BSD-3             & NumPy-array access to Qhull \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Qhull interfaces.}
\label{tbl:appsoft_qhull}
\end{table}

Qhull's output is numeric rather than exact: with default options it
produces a hull and triangulation that are correct up to roundoff, and
for exact-combinatorics work (for example, when the result must match a
proof or be bit-for-bit reproducible across platforms) CGAL or an exact
Delaunay implementation is the safer choice. Its strengths are
dimensionality, speed, and ubiquity.

\section{Triangle}
\label{sec:app_soft_triangle}

Triangle, by Jonathan Shewchuk, is the reference 2D mesh generator: a
carefully engineered C program for constrained Delaunay triangulations
and quality mesh generation that has served as the research and
educational benchmark for the subject since the mid-1990s
\cite{triangle,ruppert1995,shewchuk2002}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Triangle} --- C, open-source for research and education
    (a commercial license is required for commercial use)
    \cite{triangle}. Reads a planar straight-line graph (PSLG) and
    produces: an unconstrained or constrained Delaunay triangulation, a
    conforming Delaunay triangulation, or a quality mesh with provable
    bounds on the minimum angle and maximum triangle area via Delaunay
    refinement, the algorithm family of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation} \cite{ruppert1995,shewchuk2002}.
    Uses Shewchuk's adaptive exact predicates
    (Section~\ref{sec:intro_exact_computation}, \cite{shewchuk1997}),
    so its combinatorial decisions are exact even though vertex
    coordinates are floating point. Output uses the classic
    \texttt{.node}, \texttt{.ele}, \texttt{.poly}, and \texttt{.neigh}
    formats. \emph{Good for:} 2D quality meshing for finite elements,
   and for teaching and verifying the Delaunay-refinement theory.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\hline
\textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Description} \\
\hline
Input & planar straight-line graphs, segment constraints, holes \\
Delaunay & unconstrained, constrained, conforming Delaunay \\
Quality & Delaunay refinement with min-angle / max-area control \\
Exactness & adaptive predicates from \cite{shewchuk1997} \\
Output & \texttt{.node} / \texttt{.ele} / \texttt{.poly} / \texttt{.neigh} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Triangle at a glance.}
\label{tbl:appsoft_triangle}
\end{table}

Triangle is effectively frozen (its author recommends it for research
and education rather than new production code), but it remains the
standard against which newer 2D meshers --- CGAL's \texttt{Mesh\_2},
Gmsh's 2D mesher, and the triangulators of the Python ecosystem --- are
compared. The algorithms of Chapter~\ref{ch:triangulations} and the
refinement analysis of Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation} map directly
onto its behavior.

\section{TetGen}
\label{sec:app_soft_tetgen}

TetGen is the 3D counterpart of Triangle: a Delaunay-based tetrahedral
mesh generator for volumes, widely used in finite-element simulation.
This section also covers the surrounding meshing ecosystem --- Gmsh,
CGAL's 3D mesher, pygalmesh, MeshLab, and MMG --- so that the reader can
match a meshing task to a tool.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TetGen} --- C++, open-source (GNU AGPL v3; commercial
    licenses are available from the Weierstrass Institute)
    \cite{si2015}. Computes constrained Delaunay tetrahedralizations,
    quality tetrahedral meshes by Delaunay refinement with control over
    element size and quality, boundary recovery, and sliver removal.
    Input is a piecewise linear complex (PLC), the 3D analogue of a
    PSLG; output uses the TetGen mesh format and the standard
    \texttt{.node}/\texttt{.ele}/\texttt{.face} set.
    \emph{Good for:} 3D tetrahedral meshing for finite elements and
    scientific computing.
  \item \textbf{Gmsh} --- C++, open-source (GNU GPL v2 or later)
    \cite{geuzaine2009}. A general 3D finite-element mesh generator with
    a built-in CAD kernel, an optional OpenCascade geometry interface, a
    Python-like scripting language, and interactive pre/post-processing
    tools. Generates 1D, 2D, and 3D unstructured meshes (triangles,
    quadrangles, tetrahedra, hexahedra, and hybrid meshes), supports
    high-order elements and mesh size fields, and can run in parallel.
    \emph{Good for:} end-to-end geometry-to-mesh pipelines in scientific
    computing, especially with parametric or imported CAD geometry.
  \item \textbf{CGAL 3D mesh generation} --- C++, open-source
    (component-dependent license, see Section~\ref{sec:app_soft_cgal})
    \cite{cgalmanual}. The \texttt{Mesh\_3} package produces tetrahedral
    meshes of polyhedral or implicit domains (via a signed distance
    function) with control over element size and shape, preserving
    sharp features and 1D features; it combines Delaunay refinement
    with sliver exudation. \emph{Good for:} high-quality tetrahedral
    meshes with theoretical guarantees, and implicit-domain meshing.
  \item \textbf{pygalmesh} --- Python/C++, open-source (MIT)
    \cite{pygalmesh}. A Python wrapper around CGAL's 3D Delaunay
    refinement that meshes an implicit domain given as a signed distance
    function, returning the mesh as arrays. \emph{Good for:} Python-side
    3D meshing of level-set-defined domains.
  \item \textbf{MeshLab} --- C++/Qt, open-source (GNU GPL v3)
    \cite{meshllab}. An interactive system for processing unstructured
    3D triangular meshes built on the VCG library: cleaning and repair
    (removing duplicate and non-manifold elements), decimation, surface
    remeshing, curvature and metric computation, and alignment.
    \emph{Good for:} mesh cleanup, repair, and decimation of scanned or
    reconstructed surfaces (compare with
    Chapter~\ref{ch:shape_reconstruction}).
  \item \textbf{MMG} --- C, open-source (GNU LGPL v3)
    \cite{mmg}. A suite of mesh-adaptation codes --- \texttt{mmg2d},
    \texttt{mmgs} (surface), and \texttt{mmg3d} (volume) --- that locally
    remesh simplicial meshes to match a prescribed (possibly anisotropic)
    metric, mesh implicit domains from a level-set function, and track
    moving boundaries. \emph{Good for:} anisotropic metric-based
    adaptation in CFD and PDE solvers.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
\textbf{Mesher} & \textbf{Language} & \textbf{License} & \textbf{Focus} \\
\hline
TetGen     & C++     & AGPL-3, commercial & tetrahedral meshing \\
Gmsh       & C++     & GPL-2+             & 3D FEM meshing + CAD \\
CGAL Mesh\_3 & C++   & GPL/LGPL-3         & quality tet meshing \\
pygalmesh  & C++/Python & MIT             & implicit domains in Python \\
MeshLab    & C++/Qt  & GPL-3             & mesh processing/repair \\
MMG        & C       & LGPL-3             & metric-based adaptation \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Meshing software.}
\label{tbl:appsoft_tetgen}
\end{table}

The division of labor among these tools mirrors the theory: TetGen and
CGAL implement Delaunay-refinement with the quality bounds of
Chapter~\ref{ch:mesh_generation}, Gmsh layers a CAD kernel and a
scripting interface on top of similar technology, MeshLab operates on
the output after generation, and MMG re-fines a mesh to match error
estimates, which is a post-generation step.

\section{SciPy Spatial Algorithms}
\label{sec:app_soft_scipy}

SciPy is the scientific-computing workhorse of the Python ecosystem; its
\texttt{scipy.spatial} module is where most Python users first meet
convex hulls, Delaunay triangulations, Voronoi diagrams, and nearest
neighbor search. The closely related \texttt{matplotlib.path} module
supplies fast point-in-polygon tests for plotting and geometry code.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{scipy.spatial} --- Python (C and Cython behind NumPy),
    open-source (BSD 3-clause) \cite{scipy}. Wraps Qhull
    (Section~\ref{sec:app_soft_qhull}) to provide \texttt{ConvexHull},
    \texttt{Delaunay}, \texttt{Voronoi}, and \texttt{SphericalVoronoi}
    on NumPy arrays; also provides \texttt{cKDTree} for fast nearest
    neighbor and fixed-radius queries (the k-d trees of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_data_structures}), the \texttt{distance}
    module of pairwise metric functions, \texttt{Procrustes} analysis,
    and the \texttt{transform} module (rotations, Slerp) used in
    registration. \emph{Good for:} quick, array-oriented hull,
    triangulation, Voronoi, and nearest-neighbor computations in Python.
  \item \textbf{matplotlib.path} --- Python, open-source (matplotlib's
    PSF-derived BSD-style license) \cite{matplotlib}. Implements the
    \texttt{Path} object, a sequence of line and curve segments with a
    closed flag, and \texttt{Path.contains\_point} /
    \texttt{contains\_points}, which test point-in-polygon membership
    using either the even-odd or the non-zero winding rule. The same
    object is the basis of drawing polygons in matplotlib figures.
    \emph{Good for:} point-in-polygon tests, polygon rendering, and
    lightweight clipping in Python.
  \item \textbf{matplotlib.tri} --- Python, part of matplotlib
    \cite{matplotlib}. Builds triangulations on scattered points
    (delegating to \texttt{scipy.spatial.Delaunay} when available) and
    produces interpolated contours and surfaces over them.
    \emph{Good for:} contouring scattered data without a dedicated
    geometry library.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\hline
\textbf{Module} & \textbf{Focus} \\
\hline
scipy.spatial & hulls, Delaunay, Voronoi, cKDTree, distances \\
matplotlib.path & point-in-polygon tests, polygon rendering \\
matplotlib.tri & contouring over triangulated scattered data \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{SciPy spatial algorithms and matplotlib path utilities.}
\label{tbl:appsoft_scipy}
\end{table}

A caution: like Qhull itself, \texttt{scipy.spatial} results are
combinatorially reliable only up to floating-point precision, and the
package makes no exactness promises. For computations whose output must
be certified (or reproducible bit for bit), route them through an
exact-predicate library instead; the rest of the time, SciPy's speed and
array interface make it the right default.

\section{Python Geometry Ecosystem}
\label{sec:app_soft_python}

The Python ecosystem now provides a complete geometry stack, from the
exact planar engines (Shapely, and the CGAL wrapper
\texttt{scikit-geometry}) through 3D mesh processing and point clouds
(PyVista, meshio, trimesh, Open3D) to GIS analysis (GeoPandas) and
visualization (matplotlib, Plotly, VisPy, vedo, Polyscope).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Shapely} --- Python, open-source (BSD 3-clause)
    \cite{gillies2007}. A Python binding to GEOS
    (Section~\ref{sec:app_soft_geose}) that exposes points, lines,
    polygons, and their collections, with predicates
    (\texttt{intersects}, \texttt{within}, \texttt{contains}),
    operations (intersection, union, difference, buffer, convex hull,
    simplification), distance, and WKT/WKB/GeoJSON input and output.
    Version 2 rewrote the interface on top of the GEOS C library with
    NumPy-array (universal function) dispatch, which is also the design
    that absorbed the earlier standalone \texttt{pygeos} binding
    (BSD 3-clause); new code should use Shapely 2.0 directly.
    \emph{Good for:} planar geometric analysis on geographic and
    drafting data in Python.
  \item \textbf{scikit-geometry} --- Python, open-source (GNU LGPL v3)
    \cite{scikitgeometry}. Python bindings (via pybind11) to a curated
    subset of CGAL, exposing exact-predicate operations: polygon
    Boolean operations, point location, arrangements, and 2D
    triangulations. \emph{Good for:} exact geometric computations in
    Python and as a bridge between the theory chapters and CGAL.
  \item \textbf{GeoPandas} --- Python, open-source (BSD 3-clause)
    \cite{geopandas}. Extends pandas with a geometry column type backed
    by Shapely, providing spatial joins, spatial index queries (STRtree),
    reading and writing GeoJSON, Shapefile, and other vector formats,
    and map-style plotting through matplotlib. The GeoJSON format itself
    (RFC 7946) is the lingua franca of the interchange layer.
    \emph{Good for:} tabular GIS analysis, the Python analogue of the
    operations in Chapter~\ref{ch:applications_gis}.
  \item \textbf{PyVista} --- Python, open-source (MIT) \cite{pyvista}.
    A high-level interface to the Visualization Toolkit (VTK)
    \cite{vtk} for 3D mesh plotting and analysis: reading and writing
    mesh formats, filters for decimation, smoothing, clipping,
    slicing, contouring, and marching-cubes isosurfacing, plus
    interactive rendering of surfaces, volumes, and point clouds.
    \emph{Good for:} interactive 3D visualization and mesh analysis on
    scientific data.
  \item \textbf{meshio} --- Python, open-source (MIT) \cite{meshio}.
    Reads and writes dozens of mesh formats (Gmsh \texttt{.msh}, VTK,
    STL, OBJ, PLY, Abaqus, Exodus, CGNS, and others) through a single
    unified mesh object, converting cells of any dimension.
    \emph{Good for:} mesh format conversion between meshers, solvers,
    and visualizers.
  \item \textbf{trimesh} --- Python, open-source (MIT) \cite{trimesh}.
    A library for working with triangle meshes: loading and saving many
    formats, watertight repair, Boolean operations (via external
    engines), slicing and sectioning, cross-section extraction, convex
    hull, voxelization, and integration with the rest of the ecosystem.
    \emph{Good for:} mesh processing, repair, and analysis in Python.
  \item \textbf{Open3D} --- C++ with Python bindings, open-source (MIT)
    \cite{open3d}. A 3D data processing library aimed at robotics and
    3D vision: point cloud I/O and filtering, ICP and point-to-plane
    registration, surface reconstruction (ball pivoting, Poisson, moving
    least squares), mesh processing, and visualization.
    \emph{Good for:} point clouds, 3D vision, and reconstruction
    pipelines (compare with Chapter~\ref{ch:shape_reconstruction}).
\end{itemize}

Visualization deserves a subsection of its own, since almost every
geometric algorithm in this book is best debugged by drawing it.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{matplotlib} --- Python, open-source (matplotlib license,
    PSF-derived BSD style) \cite{matplotlib}. The default 2D plotting
    library: \texttt{plot}, \texttt{scatter}, \texttt{Patch} and
    \texttt{Polygon} artists, and \texttt{PathPatch} make it the natural
    place to draw point sets, polygons, triangulations, Voronoi cells,
    and hulls as figures for this book and for papers.
    \emph{Good for:} publication-quality 2D figures of geometric
    structures.
  \item \textbf{Plotly} --- Python/JavaScript, open-source (MIT)
    \cite{plotly}. Interactive web-based plots with \texttt{scatter3d},
    \texttt{mesh3d} (triangle meshes in 3D), and surface traces;
    good for sharing interactive geometric figures in notebooks and
    dashboards. \emph{Good for:} interactive, browser-shareable 3D
    visualizations.
  \item \textbf{VisPy} --- Python, open-source (BSD 3-clause)
    \cite{vispy}. GPU-accelerated visualization on top of OpenGL; renders
    very large point sets, lines, and meshes at interactive rates, with
    a scene graph and scientific visualization backends.
    \emph{Good for:} high-performance interactive rendering of large
    geometric datasets.
  \item \textbf{vedo} --- Python, open-source (MIT) \cite{vedo}. A
    VTK-based module for scientific visualization and analysis of 3D
    objects and point clouds: high-level mesh plotting, slicing, distance
    computation, animation, and publication-quality rendering.
    \emph{Good for:} scientific 3D visualization with minimal code.
  \item \textbf{Polyscope} --- C++ and Python, open-source (MIT)
    \cite{polyscope}. A lightweight viewer for meshes, point clouds, and
    scalar/vector fields designed for geometry-processing research;
    data registered in a few lines of code can be inspected
    interactively, including picking and clipping.
    \emph{Good for:} debugging and presenting geometry-processing
    algorithms.
  \item \textbf{OpenGL-based tools} --- C++/Python, mixed licenses.
    When an application needs a custom interactive geometry viewer, the
    usual route is direct OpenGL (via GLFW or similar windowing
    frameworks) or a retained-mode toolkit such as VTK \cite{vtk};
    collision-sensitive rendering pipelines are discussed at length in
    \cite{vandenbergen2004}. \emph{Good for:} custom viewers and
    interactive demonstrations of geometric algorithms.
\end{itemize}

\section{Choosing a Library}
\label{sec:app_soft_choosing}

No single library dominates every task, and the right choice is usually
determined by five questions: \emph{what} algorithm family is needed,
\emph{how many} dimensions, whether \emph{exactness} is required,
which \emph{language and ecosystem} the project uses, and what
\emph{license} the deliverable can tolerate.

The central trade-off is the one that pervades this book: \emph{exact
computation versus floating-point speed}. The exactness spectrum, from
most to least conservative, is

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Shewchuk's robust predicates} --- C, open-source
    (unrestricted free use) \cite{shewchuk1997}. The adaptive
    orientation, in-circle, and in-sphere predicates that are the
    standard building block for triangulation, hull, and Voronoi code;
    nearly as fast as plain floating-point evaluation on typical input.
    \emph{Good for:} implementing the predicate layer of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:geometric_predicates} in your own code.
  \item \textbf{CGAL exact number types and kernels} --- C++, open-source
    \cite{cgalmanual}. Filtered exact arithmetic (Gmpz/Gmpq,
    \texttt{Lazy\_exact\_nt}) applied at the kernel level, so whole
    algorithms --- not just individual predicates --- are exact.
    \emph{Good for:} full-algorithm exactness guarantees.
  \item \textbf{GMP} --- C, open-source (dual GPL v3 and LGPL v3)
    \cite{gmp}. The GNU Multiple Precision Arithmetic Library: bignum
    integers and rationals at high speed, the underlying engine for the
    exact kernels above. \emph{Good for:} multiprecision integer and
    rational arithmetic as a building block.
  \item \textbf{MPFR} --- C, open-source (GNU LGPL v3) \cite{mpfr}.
    Arbitrary-precision binary floating point with correct rounding for
    each elementary operation, useful when the geometry is numerical but
    the precision budget must be controllable. \emph{Good for:}
    high-precision floating-point geometry with a certified rounding
    model.
\end{itemize}

A pragmatic rule used throughout the accompanying CompGeom package, and
recommended here: implement with adaptive predicates from the start
\cite{shewchuk1997}; use exact kernels only where the output structure
must be certified (arrangements, Boolean operations, mesh combinatorics);
and reserve epsilon-tolerance tricks for genuinely numerical
measurements, never for combinatorial predicates --- the failure modes
are exactly the ones catalogued in
Sections~\ref{sec:intro_robustness}--\ref{sec:intro_exact_computation}.
For a general, well-tested textbook baseline, the design of
\cite{deberg2008} (and the closely related \cite{berg2008}) is what the
majority of the field implements in one form or another.

Community and reference resources round out the survey. The \emph{CGAL
User and Reference Manual} and its tutorials \cite{cgalmanual} are the
most complete documentation of practical computational geometry in
existence. The \emph{Journal of Computational Geometry} \cite{jocg} is
the field's free, open-access journal and a reliable source of the
theory behind the libraries. Finally, the repository of the CompGeom
package that accompanies this book follows the conventions described in
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation_guide}: one module per algorithm
chapter, pseudocode mirrored by Python implementations, and a test suite
that includes the degenerate cases discussed in each chapter. That
layout is itself a model worth copying --- for a geometry project, the
tests are the only place where the difference between "robust in theory"
and "robust in practice" becomes visible.

One closing caveat. The software landscape is fast-moving: licenses do
change (several libraries listed here re-licensed themselves over the
past decade), APIs evolve, and projects fall into and out of
maintenance. The profiles above are accurate as of the writing of this
book, but any project committed to a specific library should re-check
its license and maintenance status before depending on it for
production work.
